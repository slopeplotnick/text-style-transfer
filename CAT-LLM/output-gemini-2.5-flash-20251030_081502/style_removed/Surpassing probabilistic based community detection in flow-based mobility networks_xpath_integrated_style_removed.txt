It's really important to understand how groups are organized in networks where people or things move around. This helps us see how different areas connect. But the ways we do this now have two big problems: First, the usual methods for finding groups can't see small details or big pictures properly. Second, they don't combine looking at small, local groups with looking at big, overall groups in these movement networks. So, to fix these issues, this research came up with a new way that mixes something called 'surpassing probability theory' with how we find these groups. This new method, called SPBL, which uses surpassing probability with the Leiden method, first changes how connections are weighted to get around the limits of the standard Leiden method, so it can find big groups. Then, another part of it, called TPSPCD, carefully breaks down these big groups into smaller, detailed sub-groups, but still keeps the important main connections intact. The whole system also has a way to measure, called ASD, to figure out how strong the links are inside a group compared to links going outside it. What we found was clear patterns of central areas and outer areas in these movement networks, and groups stuck together strongly around important central points. So, this research says that this new way of finding groups really gets how local movements work in these networks. This whole thing helps improve both the ideas and practical use of finding groups in movement networks, giving city planners useful tools to help areas grow.
