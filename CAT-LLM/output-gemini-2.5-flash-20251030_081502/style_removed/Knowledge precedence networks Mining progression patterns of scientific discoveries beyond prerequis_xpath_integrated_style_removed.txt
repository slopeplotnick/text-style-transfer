It's super important to figure out how scientists learn new things as they do their jobs. This helps make education and new ideas better. So, this study made these things called KPNs. They used them to figure out how science actually moves forward in 19 different areas. They checked out what almost 5 million scientists did and 80 million papers from a huge online database called OpenAlex. We also came up with a method called CoCiTCD. It basically combines how papers cite each other with a way to spot groups that change over time. This helps us figure out how knowledge builds up by finding research groups, picking the main ideas, and seeing how those ideas connect over time. These KPNs, especially in Math, Computer Science, and Engineering, really highlight that basic ideas are super important for understanding the more advanced stuff. For example, Algorithms kind of link Math and Computer Science, and that helps push forward things like Artificial Intelligence and Data Science. We also checked how well our KPNs for 303 ideas lined up with what big AI language programs said were the 'must-know-before' concepts. This helped us see how scientists actually work with knowledge as time goes on. In the best situation, the KPN found about 25.77% of the connections it was looking for. Another system, called KCN, which just uses citations, got pretty close with 26.6%. This low match, which happened all the time, tells us that how topics actually change in the real world is often way different from what the 'should-learn-this-first' theory says. Plus, a score of 0.76 on our test curve proves that our KPN way of doing things is pretty solid at catching all the tricky, new ways knowledge moves forward. Basically, these KPNs give us really useful ideas for planning research, setting up how people learn stuff, getting different fields to work together, and just understanding how knowledge is built, from easy to hard. So, it helps the 'Science of Science' field by showing how knowledge actually moves forward in different subjects.
