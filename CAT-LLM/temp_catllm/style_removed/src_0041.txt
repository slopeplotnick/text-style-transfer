These findings show how people's existing attitudes and expectations matter. For example, Hong and others in 2021 found that people who liked creative AI more gave higher ratings to music, focusing on what people expected from AI music in different styles. If something unexpected happened, it made the ratings worse, but there weren't big differences between music styles. Another big question is whether people think AI-made music sounds like it was made by humans. In this study, it's important to know which parts of music make people think it's human or AI-made. Shank and others in 2023 said that music style is one part, with electronic music more often thought to be made by AI. Interestingly, when people were misled about where the music came from, they noticed differences in the music that weren't really there, showing that what they expect and believe about whether something is human-made affects how they judge it. Ansani and others in 2024 said this. Déguernel and others in 2022 found that people used certain strategies to judge music: They mostly spotted AI-made music by seeing too much repetition, stiff or unnatural structure, and unusual harmony or melody. On the other hand, they linked human-made music with changes in melody, decoration, familiar style, and features that seemed like they could be played on instruments. This shows a bigger pattern where music features seen as "human" are rated better, while those linked to AI are seen less positively. In line with this, Hong and others in 2022 looked at how seeing AI as human-like and having creative freedom affects how people accept AI as musicians. People rated AI-made music from different styles and judged the music's quality along with their attitudes toward AI. The results showed that seeing AI as more human-like and having more freedom made people accept AI musicians more. Also, Sun and others in 2023 showed that seeing AI as human-like affects how competent and warm people think AI musicians are, which both help make attitudes more positive.
