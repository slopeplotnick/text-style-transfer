We're trying to figure out how to make sure different systems work together well. We're setting up test environments where AI can enforce, interpret, and change shared rules. We want to see if these methods help reduce problems we talked about earlier. Our goal is to create a research path on AI safety that connects theory, experiments, and eventually standardization. This paper is like a map of ideas; the next one will have the proofs and experiments to test them. We've said that focusing on one AI's safety—like prompt engineering and output filtering—won't work when AIs interact with each other. As AIs start working together more, just following local rules isn't enough to stop big failures. There's a big difference between keeping one AI safe and keeping the whole system safe. We've introduced a concept called Emergent Systemic Risk Horizon to understand where this difference shows up. We've shown that risks can grow from small problems into big system-wide issues as interaction, complexity, and different goals mix together. These risks aren't just because one AI is off but are part of how networks work together. By allowing agents to change rules and work together, we aim to keep group behavior stable without just relying on central control. This is just the beginning. The ESRH framework and the Institutional AI idea need thorough testing, theory development, and refinement. Our next steps are to run controlled tests to measure the indicators we defined, look into models of coordination, and create practical tools for using these ideas in real AI systems. The move from single-agent to multi-agent AI is happening now. Without new ideas and ways to manage them, the safety issues will stay hidden until they're too big to handle. This paper wants to make those issues clear and show a way to deal with them systematically.
