The survey had a way to check if people were paying attention, like asking, "Who made the music you just heard?" before they rated the music. After that, they were asked how honest and careful they were with their answers on a 5-point scale and whether they listened with headphones or speakers. They were told their answers wouldn’t change their feedback or payment. The study setup is shown in Figure 1. Everyone had to say how familiar they were with the music and how much they liked it on a scale from 1 (not at all) to 5 (very). If they picked 4 or 5 for familiarity, they got another question asking if the music reminded them of a specific person or experience. People in the "AI or human" group also had to say if they thought the music was made by AI or a human and how sure they were about it on a 6-point scale from 1 (definitely AI) to 6 (definitely human). Everyone’s emotional reactions to the music were measured using something called the Geneva Emotion Music scale (GEMS), which has nine types of emotions grouped into three main categories: Sublimity (like Wonder, Tenderness, and Nostalgia), Vitality (like Joy and Power), and Unease (like Sadness and Tension). They used a shorter version of GEMS with one label for each emotion and a few related terms. People rated each emotion on a scale from 0 to 100, starting at 0. If they didn’t pick anything, it stayed at 0. There was also a box where they could add up to three other emotions not listed in GEMS and rate how strong those feelings were.
