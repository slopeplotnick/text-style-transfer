We ran a bunch of tests using different benchmarks, like checking how good machine translation is (WMT23) and evaluating reward models (Reward Bench 2). What we found is that our method, which adjusts how votes are distributed, works way better than other common approaches that rely on self-consistency. By taking a closer look at all the votes, our method turns messy individual model decisions into ratings that are more trustworthy, and it does just as well or even better than humans when compared to a gold standard based on human agreement. Here’s what we did: First, we figured out that the usual ways of combining results from LLM judges during inference aren’t great, and that using a better method for combining results is super important. Second, we came up with a new way to combine results using something called Expected Risk Minimization (ERM) with a Bradley-Terry-Davidson model, which we tested on a small set of calibration data. It ended up beating other methods across different tasks, whether it was reward benchmarks or machine translation. Third, for machine translation specifically, we used a consensus-based evaluation to create better ground truth labels when the data was noisy. This let us compare our results fairly to human raters and showed that LLM judges can sometimes even do better than humans. Recently, LLMs have been used as “judges” to evaluate generated text, and they’ve been really successful at it, offering a scalable way to measure quality instead of using traditional metrics. These models have shown they align well with human judgments across different areas. Some methods just use general-purpose LLMs directly, like G-Eval or JudgeLM, while others tweak models specifically for evaluation tasks, like Prometheus or Auto-J. Even though these approaches are strong, they have problems, like being sensitive to how prompts are designed or having biases, like favoring certain candidate orders or preferring longer outputs. LLM judges also vary in how they make decisions—some are stricter than others when it comes to small differences or ties. Our work is all about reducing this noise and making their decisions more consistent by using a better way to combine results.
