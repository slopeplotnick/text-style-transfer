Some researchers, like those in studies [25] and [15], have come up with ways to check if AI models think about morals like humans do, using things like MoralBench. They found that while these models can sometimes understand morals, they often get confused when things get tricky or when different moral ideas clash. But these tests only show how the models think at one time and don't show how their thinking changes over time or with more training. Other studies have looked at how to make AI explain its decisions and align with human values, which is important for responsible AI. For instance, Shitong and others [9] created DeNEVIL, a model that helps AI learn to reason about morals without bias. Jiao and others [16] talked about testing AI morals across different cultures and building trust, pointing out that AI ethics depend on both technology and society. These methods are good at explaining and building trust, but they rely on set moral categories and manual labeling, which makes it hard for them to adapt to changing moral situations. Although past research has helped with fairness checks and aligning values, there's still a missing piece: a flexible way to evaluate morals over time that doesn't depend on a specific model. Current methods look at ethics at certain times but don't have ways to create, understand, and adapt moral scenarios on their own. Also, none of them offer tools to measure how much AI ethics differ or to track moral consistency across models in real time. Based on ideas from Section 1, the new Moral Consistency Pipeline (MoCoP) is a system that continuously checks AI morals without needing a specific dataset, aiming to measure, understand, and improve the moral stability of large language models. MoCoP is all about evaluating and understanding, not about making the models better or retraining them, to keep things clear and consistent.
