Previous research has shown that smaller models don’t always perform worse in a smooth way when their size is reduced, and our results back that up. We also found that a smaller model, MentalQLM (0.5B), did a good job predicting mental health issues like depression, stress, and suicidal thoughts. This shows that smaller, fine-tuned models can work well for mental health predictions, balancing how much computer power they need with how accurate they are. Other studies have also found that these models keep their ability to reason and answer questions after fine-tuning, so they can handle different tasks and still do well with mental health predictions. Our tests showed that models with 3-7 billion parameters strike a good balance between being efficient and accurate when fine-tuned. Smaller models don’t work as well because they can’t adapt to the tasks, but models in this range can classify psychological signals in both zero-shot and few-shot scenarios. With fine-tuning, our model, Menta, sets a clear minimum size for models to still perform well on complex mental health tasks while staying efficient. When comparing smaller fine-tuned models to larger ones, the smaller ones do just as well or even better at predicting depression and stress but don’t do as well with suicidal thoughts. However, compared to smaller models that haven’t been trained, the fine-tuned ones improve a lot and are more balanced across different tasks. Our results show that well-optimized smaller models can still handle the reasoning and context needed for mental health predictions. These smaller models work well in this area because mental health tasks often rely on specific language cues, like how people refer to themselves, show emotions, or talk about time, which smaller models can pick up on without needing the broad knowledge that bigger models have. Earlier research has shown that fine-tuning on specific types of text helps close the gap between smaller and bigger models, and our work adds to that by showing that efficient fine-tuning methods, like LoRA, along with special loss functions, help smaller models learn what they need for mental health predictions while using less computer power.
