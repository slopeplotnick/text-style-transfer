We ran the analyses again using the ATTARI subscales and found that only the behavioral subscale mattered as a predictor, while the affective and cognitive ones didn’t make a difference when looked at together (check supplements S3). This means people’s attitudes toward AI are mostly influenced by how willing they are to actually use AI, not by their general thoughts or feelings about it. To add to the numbers and get a sense of what people think, we looked at 178 open-ended responses and grouped them into themes, which are shown in Table 2, with the full details in the Supplementary Materials. People talked about things like the quality of the music, how emotional it felt, and whether it seemed human. A lot of people mentioned AI music in terms of how good it sounded technically and musically, how original it was, and whether it made them feel something. The idea of humanness came up the most, and it wasn’t just about how the music sounded. Some people said the human element, like the artist’s personality or the story behind a song, mattered to them, and they felt AI music lacked soul and authenticity. At the same time, some didn’t care where the music came from, saying things like “If it sounds good then I don’t mind if it was artificially generated” (ID926). These responses also showed that people’s attitudes toward AI influence how they judge AI music. Many comments were more about personal opinions on AI in general and didn’t really talk about the quality of the songs, like “I don’t like anything to do with AI-generated music” (ID914). Besides these points, the responses brought up other ways people think about AI music that aren’t usually studied in experiments. For example, people judged AI music based on ethical concerns, like how it might replace musicians, hurt artists financially, or use training data without permission (“is built upon data without the consent of the artists that have dedicated decades of their lives to the craft”, ID615). One person said, “I think it undermines and abuses artists” (ID446), showing that people don’t just look at how AI music sounds but also think about whether it’s fair in how it’s made and shared.
