To manage these systems, safety checks need to shift from just following rules to watching the whole system. This means checking how influence spreads among agents, if oversight signals affect group behavior, and when feedback becomes uncontrollable. The main idea here is that basic safety measures are needed but aren't enough when models are part of interaction networks. Even if everything is perfect locally, it can't stop failures when feedback, connections, and group incentives take over. Recently, two studies looked at risks in systems with many agents, offering useful ways to organize them: MAST (Multi-Agent System failure Taxonomy) and MARAI (Multi-Agent Risks from Advanced AI). MAST sorts failures into three categories, focusing on risks from the system's design, agent coordination, and verification. MARAI looks at three failure types based on how agents interact (miscoordination, conflict, collusion) that can lead to misaligned behaviors. Our new idea is to: 1) combine both approaches into a structured framework centered on the system, moving from agent-level failures to risks that can't be pinned down to individual parts; 2) formalize this with the Emergent Systemic Risk Horizon (ESRH), which marks the point where local reliability turns into group instability. ESRH offers terms and indicators for studying LLM ecosystems as complex systems instead of separate agents. This sets up the rest of the paper. Section 2 covers the shift towards systems with many agents and the regulatory effects of distributed causality. Section 3 explains the ESRH taxonomy and related measurements. Section 4 suggests the Institutional AI architecture as a design idea for putting governance right into interacting model groups.
