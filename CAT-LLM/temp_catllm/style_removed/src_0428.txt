Figure 1 shows how the models did, and the average scores for five areas are in the legend. The weights for these areas are: Phenomenon Congruency (0.3), Prompt Consistency (0.2), Spatio-temporal Coherence (0.2), Correct Dynamism (0.15), and Immutability (0.15). These weights show how important it is to get the expected results, set up the experiment right, and keep things consistent. Table 3 looks at how scores from different setups of VideoScience-Judge compare to human ratings. With GPT-5 pro, the scores are not as inflated, but there are still big differences in areas like Dynamism, Coherence, and Immutability, where the model tends to think it's doing better than it is. Using a checklist and making the model explain its ratings with evidence from key frames helps reduce these overestimations. Adding CV tools like ByteTrack, which helps track identities across frames and gives solid proof for penalizing jumps or unexpected changes, really helps close the scoring gaps, by 0.07 for Coherence and 0.09 for Immutability, compared to the basic VideoScience-Judge without the checklist. This leads to scores being more similar and less able to tell similar models apart, while its rule-based method of checking for clear rule-breaking (like "Does the ball fall down?" or "Does water flow right?") can't catch the full picture of physical realism like consistent movement paths, cause and effect over time, speed changes, keeping momentum, and smoothness of movement that people naturally notice. These issues are especially clear for VideoScience-Bench, which is different from PhyGenBench's simpler, one-rule scenarios. VideoScience-Bench deals with many scientific ideas (like how multiple objects interact or fluid dynamics) where results come from a mix of rules instead of just one, with effects that build on each other where second-level dynamics are important.
