We looked at how the training images we picked out affected the results by checking how similar the generated images were before and after retraining. But just comparing those images doesn’t fully explain why some pictures had a bigger impact. So, we also analyzed the training images using a graph-based method to see how they were structured and what they had in common. Basically, we wanted to find out if the images we picked formed clear, meaningful groups. Table 3 shows some stats from 10 tests we ran (each with 15 prompts) to see how consistent and grouped the images were. These results helped us confirm that our way of picking images works and gave us a better idea of why these images matter in the process. For example, the number of different values per relationship was between 3.9 and 5.1 on average. This means there was some variety, but not too much, which makes sense for something like fashion, where there are only so many styles or patterns. The images were similar enough to fit into clear groups but different enough not to be repetitive. This shows that the influence comes from shared ideas, not just copying the same thing. We also looked at how many attributes the images shared. On average, they shared between 5 and 7.1 attributes, with some groups sharing up to 13. This means the images weren’t just detailed but also matched each other in meaningful ways. Prompts with more specific details led to more shared attributes, while more general or creative prompts resulted in more variety. This shows that the system can adjust to different types of prompts and still find examples that match well.
