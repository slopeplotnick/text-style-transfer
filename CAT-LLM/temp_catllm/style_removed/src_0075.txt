Understanding language involves a step-by-step process. First, we hear speech, then we recognize sounds (phonemes), combine them into words, and figure out what the words mean based on grammar to understand the whole sentence. Here, we’re looking at how we understand language after recognizing words, which is a complex mental process unique to humans, and comparing it to how advanced AI handles language. Research shows that the brain processes language in layers, with simpler tasks like predicting words happening in the lower layers and more complex tasks, like understanding abstract ideas, in the higher layers. The brain learns language by noticing mistakes in predictions and improving over time, similar to how AI learns. Both the brain and AI use this method to predict and understand language. For example, Google’s Neural Machine Translation (GNMT) system translates sentences using three parts: an encoder, an attention unit, and a decoder. The encoder takes in the words of a sentence one by one and creates a signal that represents the sentence’s meaning. It has eight layers, and the final layer combines everything into a single representation of the sentence. The attention unit helps figure out which words in the original sentence are most important for translation and sends this information to the decoder. The decoder then predicts the next word in the translation, layer by layer. The system learns by comparing its translations to correct ones made by humans and adjusting itself based on the errors. On the other hand, GPT-2, a type of AI, works differently. It doesn’t have separate parts like GNMT. Instead, it predicts the next word in a sentence based on the words that came before it. During training, it’s given sentences with missing words and learns to guess the missing word. Later, it uses this ability to generate sentences by predicting one word at a time. GPT-2 is made of repeating blocks that include attention units and simple neural networks, stacked many times. It takes in a sequence of words and outputs the probabilities of what the next word might be. It learns by practicing next-word predictions without needing human-made examples.
