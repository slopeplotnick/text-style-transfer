Big language models are good at clear reasoning, but we don't know much about how they handle things without clear instructions. Research over many years shows people naturally deal with messy signals in a smart way when they need to make sense of what they see. We're checking if these models do the same thing and can mix different types of information well without being taught how. Using methods from psychology, we look at how these models think by studying their behavior. We've made a test called BayesBench with four tasks that measure things like size and time using words and pictures, based on old psychology studies, and we compare nine models with human opinions to set a standard. By changing the amount of noise, context, and instructions, we see how well they work and how they combine different types of information. Besides looking at how correct and efficient they are, we have a score that checks if they act in a way that matches smart behavior even when they're already doing well. Our study shows that while some models adjust well, being correct doesn't mean they're always reliable. For example, GPT-5 Mini is great with text but not so good with pictures. This shows a gap between what they can do and how they do it, meaning tests that focus only on being correct might miss how they handle uncertainty. These results show how models deal with uncertainty and the link between being correct and acting smartly. We share our test and score as tools to help design better systems that use different types of information. Judging things like size, time, or distance is a basic skill for both humans and machines. People do this by smartly mixing messy signals, giving more weight to reliable information and using what they expect to reduce mistakes. This way of thinking happens naturally across different cultures and ages, showing it's a basic way to handle uncertain information.
