Recent licensing models like NOODL and the Esethu License show a big change in how things are done. NOODL treats people in Africa or developing countries differently from those outside these areas, letting benefits be shared in a way that fits the region and making sure changes are shared locally. Esethu uses money from licenses to grow resources and help local jobs, keeping control with the community and making sure resources last. We've learned from working with Indigenous and low-resource language data that just being open can make things unfair if there aren't strong ethical rules. Now, sharing data responsibly means more than just having a basic license; it needs focus on consent, privacy, fairness, and ways for the community to keep benefiting. We picked CC BY 4.0 for Swivuriso because we wanted openness, but it also showed the limits of regular licenses for African and low-resource language datasets. New ideas like NOODL and Esethu show the move towards better, fairer management. When publishing datasets, it's not enough to just say there's an open license; authors should also talk about how they handle data in a way that's ethical, fair, and helps the community. Swivuriso is a big step forward for improving automatic speech recognition and other speech technologies for South African languages. It covers seven different languages and includes both specific and culturally relevant unscripted speech, which can be used for many things like health communication, farming advice, and multilingual language tools. The dataset is made to help not only with testing and improving things like word error rate but also with creating speech systems that understand culture and social needs.
