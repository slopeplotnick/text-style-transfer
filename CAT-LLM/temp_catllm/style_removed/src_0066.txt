So, after looking into it more, it turned out that how people felt about AI had a bigger impact on the results than the actual experiment itself. When they added people's attitudes about AI into the analysis, the experimental setup didn’t really explain whether people liked the music or how strongly they felt about it. But how people felt about AI stayed important and consistent. This lines up with earlier research showing that how people feel about autonomous machines affects how they judge them (Hong et al., 2022). It also adds to what Shank et al. (2023) said about how bias toward composers might depend on the song and situation, like if the song is popular and sounds like it was made by a human. Some personality traits and music-related factors were linked to how much people liked the music and how emotional they felt, but these didn’t matter as much once attitudes toward AI were taken into account. The only exceptions were openness to experience and neuroticism, which are personality traits that have been shown before to influence how people feel about music, especially when it comes to emotions like happiness and energy (Gerstgrasser et al., 2023; Juslin et al., 2011; Liljeström et al., 2013). Those emotions were really noticeable in the music used in this study. The most interesting part was that attitudes toward AI were the only thing about the listeners that was clearly tied to both liking the music and their emotional reactions. Overall, the amount of variation explained by listener traits was about the same as in other studies (Gerstgrasser et al., 2023; Strauss et al., 2024). This shows that how people feel about AI plays a big role in how they judge AI-made music. Unlike what Ansani et al. (2024) found, we didn’t see that attitudes toward AI changed how the experimental groups worked. This might be because we used different attitude measures, focused on listening to tracks instead of live performances, and had three experimental setups instead of two.
