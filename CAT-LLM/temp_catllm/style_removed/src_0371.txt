Checkers and similar methods are good for things like math and coding, but they don’t work well for tasks that don’t have clear answers. Some approaches let the model or another AI act as a judge, like Self-Rewarding Language Models and RLAIF, which use AI feedback instead of human input, or IPO and Self-Rewarding PPO, which use probabilities to figure out preferences. Other research looks at internal activations, like Factoscope and INSIDE, showing that hidden states can tell if something is factual or likely to be wrong. Our method goes in the same direction, using a geometric measure of these activations as a reward to improve the model’s decisions. There’s a theory behind this idea, based on softmax bottleneck analysis. Yang and others showed that models using softmax are limited by the rank of their hidden layers, and Godey expanded this idea to modern AI, showing that natural language probabilities are high-rank. Other studies show that when embeddings get too narrow during training, the quality of what the model generates gets worse. All this research connects the complexity of representations to the quality of what the model produces. Experiments back this up, like Garrido’s work showing that the rank of learned representations predicts performance without needing labels. In language models, hidden states have a lot of meaning built into them: word embeddings show patterns in syntax and meaning, and intermediate layers can tell if something’s factual or likely to be wrong. This proves that the quality of what the model generates leaves patterns you can measure. Our work takes these ideas further by not just analyzing rank after training but using it as a live reward signal to improve the model while it’s learning, turning this geometric insight into a useful way to make the model better.
