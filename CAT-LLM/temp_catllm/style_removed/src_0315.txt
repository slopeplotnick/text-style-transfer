People are getting more worried about legal and ethical issues with generative models, especially when it comes to using copyrighted stuff to train these models. The big question is about who gets credit for creating something and the moral rights involved. Some lawsuits claim that using copyrighted material without permission breaks property rights. For instance, the New York Times is suing OpenAI for using its news archives without permission, and Getty Images is suing Stability AI for making stuff based on its database. Besides lawsuits, artists are speaking up too. Elton John, Dua Lipa, and 200 other artists wrote a letter asking tech companies to protect human creativity from AI that can copy their voices and styles without asking. In response, some organizations are suggesting new rules. The European Union has the AI Act, which talks about being open about AI systems, while the UK and UNESCO have guidelines for responsible AI that protect privacy and property. Denmark recently suggested a change to its copyright law to give people rights over their voice, face, and body likeness, which is the first of its kind in Europe. This change is meant to cover legal issues with AI copying personal traits. The Danish culture minister said it’s about protecting people's rights to their own body, voice, and face, highlighting the need for identity protection against generative AI. Even with these moves, most generative models are still like black boxes, and it’s hard to see how the training data affects what they produce. Even open-source models are too big to copy exactly. Current methods like influence functions or retraining-based attribution need access to the inside workings.
