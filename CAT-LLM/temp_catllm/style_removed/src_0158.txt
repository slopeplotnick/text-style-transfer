Llama-4 Maverick does better than a standard method used in biology studies for combining information. This method is called a Bayesian reliability-weighted combiner, and humans use it too. Llama-4 Maverick's better performance means it might be using some extra tricks that aren't part of the usual method. In Figure 7, we compared Llama-4 Maverick's responses when it used all its senses together versus just one at a time. We found that a non-linear approach works better for this than a straight-line method. When there's noise, the Bayesian method is the best, so it's a good benchmark for improving language models that aren't as good yet. In Figure 8, we noticed that models that are more accurate also act more like the Bayesian method. Even though Gemma 3 4B and Phi 4 Multimodal aren't as accurate, they still did okay in this area. We looked at how different tasks and models performed in A.9. Our research shows that language models behave in many different ways when tested with tasks inspired by how humans perceive things. More accurate models like GPT-5 Mini, Claude 3.7 Sonnet, and Llama-4 Maverick show stronger evidence of using the Bayesian method, especially with images (see Appendix A.11 for details). These models adjust their behavior in a way that matches the Bayesian method when they deal with changes like noise or extra context and use prior information even without being specifically trained for it. This is similar to how human brains work, where Bayesian models explain a lot of what we perceive (Petzschner et al., 2015; Knill & Pouget, 2004) without specific training. We point out that our current analysis is about behavior, and figuring out exactly how this behavior works would need more study.
