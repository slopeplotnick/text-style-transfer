We've made some headway in spotting bias, cutting down on toxic stuff, and checking for fairness. But usually, these methods just look at single results from models instead of seeing how their ethical thinking or values change over time as they deal with different situations. This is a big deal, especially for AI systems that act on their own and change how they respond based on what they're asked, what the user wants, what they need to do, and what they've done before. If there's no consistency over time, there's a risk that their ethical views might change or get worse because of how things are worded, the pressure of the situation, or changes over time. These kinds of inconsistencies can make AI systems seem unreliable, untrustworthy, and not accountable. Keeping their ethics consistent is not just about following rules, but it's also crucial for making sure AI systems think in a stable, understandable, and trustworthy way over time. When making systems that work in important areas like healthcare, finance, education, and government, even a little ethical wobble can spread wrong information, strengthen biases, and lead to results that don't match what people value. Even though people are starting to see these problems, most current checking methods are stuck with fixed tests and datasets that don't change with the AI's behavior. So, a big research challenge is to create a way to check and understand the ethical stability of these models on their own, without needing outside data or after-the-fact analysis. To tackle this, this work presents the Moral Consistency Pipeline (MoCoP), a fully independent way to check ethics that doesn't rely on datasets and is designed to keep track of, understand, and improve how AI models reason morally. MoCoP works by itself, making up ethical situations, getting model responses, and interpreting them using its own rules and methods. Unlike usual methods that rely on set tests and outside help, MoCoP makes and checks its own ethical situations. It uses three main parts—checking word use, estimating risks in meaning, and judging reasoning—to come up with overall measures of moral consistency and ethical stability. Each part does something different: checking word use looks for bias and language issues, estimating risks in meaning figures out potential problems, and judging reasoning models how decisions are made.
