We're talking about a way to get meaningful info from pictures using advanced language models that work with different types of data, matching them with specific knowledge systems to make understandable knowledge graphs. We've also got a process that uses graphs to look at how images created by models compare to the ones they were trained on, helping people understand how similar they are. Our method is useful for things like figuring out who owns what, making things easier to understand, and being clear about where data comes from, showing how this kind of thinking can improve tracking in models that create stuff. We tested our method on models trained locally and on big models through a special experiment focused on the popular Ghibli-style images, to see how well our method can spot style influences, even when we can't see inside the model. The paper is set up like this: Section 2 goes over related work on understanding models that create stuff and building knowledge graphs using advanced language models. Section 3 explains our method. Section 4 talks about our experiments and results with both local and big models. Section 5 wraps up with main points and future plans. As models that create stuff get better, understanding them has become more important in technical, ethical, and policy areas. General methods like t-SNE, LIME, and attention visualization help show what models do but don't directly deal with where training data comes from, which is a big deal in areas like authorship and copyright. To see how training data affects what models make, different techniques have been suggested. Early methods include influence functions, retraining-based attribution, and Shapley value approximations. Even though they're useful and work well in known areas like classification tasks, these methods aren't practical for big models because they need gradients, retraining access, or lots of computing power. So, they're not used in modern deep learning setups, especially for models that create stuff where we can't see inside them.
