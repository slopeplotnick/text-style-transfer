We’re working on expanding a French-Ciluba sentiment word list to include translations in English, Afrikaans, isiZulu, Sesotho, and Sepedi. This involves using tools that translate between languages and match words based on how they’re used in text. To improve the list, we’ll use a method called PMI to find words that show emotion or opinion in African language texts and link them to the words already in the list. We’ll also use a system called RAG, which works with big language models, to clear up any confusion about what certain words mean and make sure the translations are accurate. Then, we’ll train two models, AfriBERTa and AfroXLMR, to classify emotions or opinions in text using this updated word list. We’ll check how well these models work by looking at things like precision, recall, F1-score, and ROC. To make the models easier to understand, we’ll use tools that show which parts of the text the models focus on when making decisions. Finally, we’ll compare how well individual models perform versus combining them into a group to see if that improves accuracy. The rest of the paper is set up like this: first, we review what’s already been done in sentiment analysis, building word lists for multiple languages, and working with African languages. Then, we explain our framework and methods, describe how we ran the experiments and tested the models, go over the results, and talk about what they mean for working with multiple languages in natural language processing. In the past, sentiment analysis mostly used machine learning or word lists to figure out if text shows a positive or negative opinion. Early research showed that statistical methods worked well for this, and later studies showed that word lists were helpful for simpler, rule-based approaches. Popular word lists like SentiWordNet, NRC Emotion Lexicon, and VADER are good for languages with a lot of resources but don’t cover languages that are more complex or don’t have as much data available. People have tried to create word lists for multiple languages by pulling words from text collections.
