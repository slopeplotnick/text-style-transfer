Making Questions. Based on the breakdown in Section 2.1, eight grad students who know a lot about specific fields picked out some categories and came up with 20 prompts each. These prompts needed you to think about at least two science ideas, making them pretty challenging. They were inspired by examples of public educational experiments to make sure they were scientifically sound and could be shown in a video that's 10 seconds or less. Two of these grad students also put together 40 I2V questions, each with a video showing the experiment as it should be. These videos came from public datasets and online sources, and a key frame from the start was used as the main image to show the whole experiment setup. Checking and Reviewing. Once the whole test set was ready, all the grad students got together to review everything. They checked each other's 20 prompts to make sure they were specific, accurate in steps, and clear in what they were supposed to show. For a final quality check, we used video-making models, Sora-2 and Wan-2.5-T2V-Preview, to create the first few seconds of each prompt, making sure the main parts of the experiments could be shown. Using VLM as a Judge. Recent research says that strong VLMs can make judgments similar to humans when prompted and set up right. In VideoScience-Bench, we use a VLM-as-a-Judge setup to look at the difference in performance between VLMs that focus on reasoning and those that don't, like GPT-4o. We noticed that when you ask VLMs, especially the non-reasoning ones, to judge video creations, they often don't justify their ratings well and sometimes give false positives. This led us to create a quantitative auto-eval system with a checklist grading method, where each score needs to be backed up by important video frames and a table of evidence from analyzing the videos.
