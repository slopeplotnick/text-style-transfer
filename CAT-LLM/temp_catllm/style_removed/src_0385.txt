The Menta interface can handle different tasks like checking for stress, depression (both yes/no and how severe it is), and suicide risk (also yes/no and detailed levels). When you click the "Start Evaluation" button, it processes social media posts through the chosen model and shows the results, including the predicted categories, whether they’re correct, and other evaluation details. They tested how well the model works on six tasks, using datasets that had between 10 and 3,000 posts depending on how hard the task was and how much data was available. The analysis showed there’s a balance between how fast and accurate the model is compared to other similar systems. Menta is designed to be accurate, fast, and responsive, making it a good choice for real-world use where performance and quick responses matter. In Figure 9, the chart shows three things: how fast it processes tasks (ITPS), how long it takes to give the first result (TTFT), and how accurate it is. Each circle on the chart is a model, and the size of the circle shows how much memory it uses. Falcon focuses on speed, so it’s fast at processing and starting tasks but isn’t very accurate. StableLM is more accurate but takes longer to respond and uses more resources, which makes it more expensive and slower for users. Menta, on the other hand, is very accurate (except for Task 5, where StableLM and Phi-4 Mini do slightly better) and still manages to be faster and more efficient than StableLM. This means Menta gives good results quickly and doesn’t use too much memory, which is important for systems that lots of people use. Overall, while all the models have their pros and cons, Menta is in a good spot for being ready to use in real-world settings. It performs well without needing a lot of resources, which helps keep costs down and ensures users have a good experience.
