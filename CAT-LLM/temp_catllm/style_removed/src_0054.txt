Udio can take natural-language prompts and turn them into finished music tracks in different styles, including lyrics in various languages. When it comes to how people see AI-made music, studies in computational creativity and machine learning have looked into how creative these models are by having people listen to samples. They rate music from these models compared to other models or music made by humans. They often check for overall quality, creativity, newness, and specific music elements like melody, harmony, and rhythm. Another important thing they look at is how natural or human-like the music sounds. They do this by using rating scales or a musical Turing Test, where people decide if a piece was made by a human or AI, sometimes knowing, not knowing, or being misled about who made it. In tasks like making Bach chorales, even early models like Deepbach got results that were hard to tell apart from real compositions. To check the emotional side of AI music, listeners usually try to identify the emotions intended by categorizing them as "happy" or "sad" or using models that measure emotion dimensions. As AI music gets better, studies have looked into whether people judge this music differently based on what they know or believe about who made it. But the evidence isn't consistent: Early studies that changed what people knew about who composed the music found no big bias when people rated live recordings of string-quartet pieces made by humans and computers. Similarly, Zlatkov and others didn't find a bias against AI music using pop music samples. Research focused on consumer marketing by Moura and Maw also found no clear preference for music based on who made it, whether classical or pop.
