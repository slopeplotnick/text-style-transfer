Self-Consistency is about getting a bunch of results and going with what most of them say. Soft Self-Consistency is about picking the lowest, average, or combined confidence scores in each group. Generative Self-Aggregation gets the language model to make a new answer based on different samples. Universal Self-Consistency uses the language model to choose the answer that fits best among several options. We're looking at some thinking language models: gemini-2.5-flash, qwen3 next-80b, and gpt-oss-120b. We're checking out two machine translation tasks and six tasks from the Reward Bench 2 benchmark. This benchmark is for checking reward models in six areas: factuality, following instructions exactly, math, safety, focus, and ties. For our check, we made preference pairs by matching all possible pairs from each task's source data, which has both accepted and rejected answers. These pairs are split into 'non-tie' pairs (one accepted and one rejected answer) and 'tie' pairs (two accepted or two rejected answers). From this big set, we pick 1000 examples for each of the six tasks to make the final benchmark. We explain the vote distributions for each task in detail in Appendix B. Results for different thinking language models, gemini-2.5-flash, gpt-oss-120b, and qwen3 next-80b show the same pattern, meaning our method works well across these models. The problem's structure: how often the task causes unclear cases, how often the language model gives clear vs. tied votes, and how these traits work with the MAE-aligned Davidson model. Some tasks have smooth, easy-to-work-with calibration landscapes that are easy to use elsewhere, while others have sharper landscapes that don't fit as well. Making good tests to predict when one task can be used for another—and to measure strength under big changes in distribution or targets outside the usual—is something interesting to look into in the future.
