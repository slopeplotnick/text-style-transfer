Here’s what we’re doing: we’re going to explain the process we talked about earlier using an example. We’re using the CelebA dataset, which has over 200,000 pictures of celebrities’ faces. Each picture has labels that say things like whether the person is smiling, wearing glasses, young, or attractive. We’re focusing on two things: figuring out if someone in a picture is young and if they’re attractive. Look at the images in Figure 6. Starting with the original image on the left, the figure shows different versions of the same image where we’ve made changes to make the person not young (Young = 0). The first changed image was created using a specific method. Even though systems that only look at visuals can sometimes be misleading, people still use saliency maps a lot to explain how image classification systems work. So, we’re comparing our causal explanations with Grad-CAM, which is another method, using a version implemented by Gildenblat. Now, look at Figure 7. It’s meant to explain why the two images on the far left of Figure 5 were classified as young. For the male, the chin area was important for the classification, while for the female, it was the area around the eyes. Then, in Figure 8, we look at the classification of the person in Figure 6. The first image was correctly classified as young, and Grad-CAM shows that the classifier focused on the area around the eyes to make that decision. But for the image on the right, which was classified incorrectly, Grad-CAM shows that the classifier focused on the forehead and chin. Grad-CAM doesn’t give causal explanations, so it can’t explain what would happen if something changed. Instead, it just shows which parts of the image were most important for the classifier’s decision. Also, because Grad-CAM explanations are based on pixels, they don’t explain why those areas are considered important.
