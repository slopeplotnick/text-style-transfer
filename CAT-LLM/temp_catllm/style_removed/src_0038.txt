At the end of the study, people were asked to share any extra thoughts they had about AI-generated music (you can check Supplements C for the exact wording). To look at personality traits, researchers used a short version of the Big Five Inventory (BFI-10), which works in both English and German. The five traits they looked at included Openness. They also collected basic info about the participants, like their age, gender, nationality, and education level, to understand the group better and see if these things affected the results. Even though the survey allowed for non-binary answers, everyone identified as either male or female, so gender was recorded as either one or the other. They also checked how well people understood the language used in the survey to make sure they could pick up on small differences in words describing their emotions. All the data analysis was done in Python (version 3.9.20) using pandas (version 2.2.2) for organizing data, scipy (version 1.12.0) for tests like correlation and ANOVA, and statsmodels (version 0.14.4) for mixed-effects models. The graphs were made in R (version 4.3.3) with the ggplot2 package. For the open-ended answers about AI-generated music, researchers used a method by Braun and Clarke (2006) and analyzed them with MAXQDA (version 24.10). They also did extra checks on the data by averaging song ratings for each person, which are explained in Appendix C. These checks confirmed that people liked music differently depending on the composer but didn’t show big differences in how intense the emotions were. To figure out if the experimental setup affected how much people liked the music or how strong their emotions were, beyond personal differences and the type of music, researchers used linear mixed-effects models. These models included random effects for both participants and songs and also considered personality traits, listening habits, and the music model used. The results are shown in Table 1.
