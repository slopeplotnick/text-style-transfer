Cov_tgsp and CSP usually do really well (check out Table 2 & Figure 3), but there are some differences depending on the dataset. When we look at each dataset, CSP and cov_tgsp are usually at the top, but they don't do as well on the PhysionetMI dataset compared to Cho2017 and Zhou2016. This might be because of different ways the experiments were done, like how often they took samples, how many electrodes they used, and how many people were in the study. We found that how well these methods work for figuring out motor imagery from EEG signals really depends on the dataset. For example, on Zhou2016, using CSP with StandardScaler and logistic regression got the best score (0.87), and cov_tgsp with RobustScaler and logistic regression was close (0.84). On Cho2017, cov_tgsp did better than CSP, getting a top score of 0.74 compared to 0.68. But methods that use instantaneous functional connectivity and non-linear features didn't do as well on any of the datasets. For example, svd_entropy with RobustScaler and logistic regression got scores of 0.68 on Zhou2016, 0.59 on Cho2017, and 0.54 on PhysionetMI. On PhysionetMI, the overall scores were lower, with the best setup (cov_tgsp) only getting 0.64, and CSP averaging 0.62 across frequency bands. This shows that even though non-linear and connectivity-based features can pick up on complex EEG signal patterns, they might not be the best for classifying motor imagery in this case. When we look at individual subjects, CSP and cov_tgsp are generally better across datasets, as shown in Figures 5 and 6 for Cho2017 and Zhou2016. But this isn't as clear on the PhysionetMI dataset (Figure 7), where these methods don't always do better than others. In some cases, other methods get the best scores: for example, Hjorth parameters do better than CSP and cov_tgsp for people #57 and #59, while the instantaneous connectivity method with TGSP is best for people #17 and #73.
