Even though we've made big strides in creating sound and music with computers, there's no set way to judge these systems yet. Besides just listening to the music, some researchers have come up with objective ways to check how well these systems recreate audio and how well they can sort the music into existing categories. In our research, we used both listening tests and some objective measures from past studies to see how well our symbolic music generation works. We made 100 MIDI samples for each part of our test set (GoEmotions) and checked the results. For our initial listening test, we wanted to see how well our model's music matched the emotions in the text, so we asked people to listen and tell us. This is even more subjective than usual music tasks because listeners have to figure out the emotion in the story first and then decide if the music fits that emotion. First, each person got a piece of text and had to pick which of the four emotion categories it fit into. Then, they listened to two music options for that story and chose the one they thought matched the story's emotion best. This two-part setup helped us see if people agreed on the emotion in the text and if the music matched that emotion. Since not all music choices matched the exact emotion category the person picked, we looked at how well the music matched the emotion based on positive/negative and high/low energy rather than exact categories. Each category was broken down into positive or negative and high or low energy, and for each person's choice, we checked if the music matched what they thought the story's emotion was in terms of these two things. This gave us a measure of how well the music's emotion matched the story's emotion, using positive/negative and energy levels, which is a more flexible way to see if they agree emotionally.
