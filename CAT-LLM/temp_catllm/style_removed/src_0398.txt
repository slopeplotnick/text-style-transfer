The main skills we want to develop are figuring out 3D structure without being told directly, controlling the camera view in a way that makes sense with the scene (like size, blocking, and layout), understanding how things are arranged in the scene, planning to reach goals, and keeping things consistent over time. We test these skills with two tasks: moving through a scene based on video instructions and finding objects based on text instructions. For moving through a scene, the model uses camera movement instructions to make a video that matches both the instructions and the scene setup. For finding objects, the model follows a text instruction to locate and show a specific object, which checks if it understands how things are arranged and can plan properly. These tasks help us see if VIDEO4SPATIAL can figure out and work with scene details just by using video. Since there aren’t big public datasets made for these kinds of tasks, we use indoor scanning datasets like ScanNet++ and ARKitScenes, which have long videos of different rooms and let us create pairs of context and target video clips. We use a vision-language model to pick clips that end with a focused object and to write instructions for finding objects. For moving through a scene, we turn camera movement data into instructions to follow. Tests show that this framework handles tough tasks well. Just from video, the model learns about the scene’s layout and meaning and does a good job at finding objects and moving through scenes. Even though it’s trained with short videos, it works well with longer ones during testing, which makes it even better. Also, even though it’s trained on indoor scenes, it works for outdoor ones and objects it hasn’t seen before. To sum up, we made VIDEO4SPATIAL, a simple but effective video-based framework for visuospatial tasks. We focused on important design choices like using guidance for context and instructions together, adding bounding boxes to improve accuracy, and sampling context in a way that makes understanding more efficient. We also created and tested two spatial tasks.
