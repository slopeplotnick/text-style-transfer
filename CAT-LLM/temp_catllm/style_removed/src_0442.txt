Big language models often make stuff up, sounding smooth and clear but getting facts wrong. These mistakes make people not trust them and stop them from being used in real life. To fix this, researchers have come up with two ways: one focuses on the model itself, called Hallucination Detection, and the other checks the facts, called Fact Verification. Even though they aim for the same thing, they've grown separately, using different ideas, data, and ways to check their work. This split has slowed down progress. In this study, we're trying to bring them together. We've made something called UniFact, which lets us directly compare the two by creating model outputs and checking if they're factual. After testing lots of models and methods, we found three main things: (1) Neither way is always better; (2) They each catch different types of factual mistakes; and (3) Combining both methods works best. Besides comparing them, we also looked into why they went different ways and showed why they need to be unified. The results suggest we need a new plan to bring Hallucination Detection and Fact Verification together in big language models. These models have changed the digital world, helping with advanced searches and doing hard tasks that seemed impossible before. But using them in important areas is held back by a big problem: they make fluent but wrong content, known as hallucination. In fields like healthcare, law, and journalism, where getting facts right is crucial, these mistakes can make people lose trust, spread wrong info, and cause harm. So, finding strong ways to spot these errors is not just a tech issue but essential for creating reliable smart systems.
