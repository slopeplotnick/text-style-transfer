Language models are really helpful for improving how we keep track of mental health compared to older methods. They're better than other types of computer programs at understanding context and gathering information, which makes them more useful for checking mental health on a large scale. But big language models have some problems: they're expensive to run and can be a privacy risk because they handle sensitive data on remote servers. They're also too big to use on devices with limited resources, so we need more efficient options. Smaller language models, on the other hand, can be just as good or even better for digital health because they're simpler, cheaper to run, and can work directly on devices. This means they can predict mental health issues from social media without storing personal data, allowing for quick and ethical monitoring. However, we haven't looked into their potential enough yet. In this study, we check out how well small language models can predict mental health outcomes and show that they can be a good option for checking multiple mental health issues on devices. We test these models on tasks related to stress, depression, and risk of suicide. We introduce a small model called Menta, which is fine-tuned using a special method and trained on different datasets to focus on accuracy. Menta does well in various mental health tasks, especially in spotting stress and depression. We also show how useful it is by running it on an iPhone 15 Pro Max, where it works in real-time without using much memory, providing private and scalable mental health support on the device.
