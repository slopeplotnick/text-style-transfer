Car AI systems in cars have to work under some pretty strict rules. They need to respond super fast, in just a few milliseconds, so drivers don’t get distracted or unsafe. They also can’t use too much power because of the car’s electrical limits. On top of that, these systems have to work well in tough conditions, like when it’s really bright during the day or pitch dark at night, when it’s freezing cold or really hot, when the car is shaking, and with all kinds of people using them in different ways. To handle all this, the AI needs to use special hardware that’s built to work fast and accurately, even when it’s dealing with simplified data. We use a dataset called Infinity-MM to train the AI. It’s a big collection of about 44.8 million examples that teach the AI how to handle different tasks involving pictures and words. The dataset covers a lot of ground, like answering questions about everyday stuff in pictures, recognizing objects, understanding scenes, reading documents like forms and receipts, figuring out charts and diagrams, reading text in images, having back-and-forth conversations, and even dealing with specific areas like medical images, self-driving car situations, and factory inspections. Each example in the dataset includes a picture, some instructions, and the right answers. The focus is on giving detailed answers that require thinking through multiple steps, not just picking a simple label. This is important for car AI because it needs to explain its decisions clearly so people can trust it. We train the AI in four steps, following a plan designed for the Infinity-MM dataset. In the second step, we work on general tasks like recognizing objects, understanding scenes, and answering basic questions about pictures. At this stage, we adjust all parts of the AI, including the parts that handle pictures and language. We train it with a specific learning rate and start with small batches of data to avoid problems, then switch to bigger batches later on.
