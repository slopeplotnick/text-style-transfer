There are five main categories that break down into 16 specific types of edits, covering everything from basic text formatting to complex editing workflows. Instead of having lots of simple cases, the focus is on fewer but more detailed ones, following the approach of Maia Polo et al. These cases include things like replacing text or solving problems that involve multiple edits and using both images and text to figure things out. For example, one case involves matching pictures to captions across slides by using both what you see and what you read. Some cases overlap across different categories, so the total number of categories goes over 100. PPTArena is different from other benchmarks because it focuses on how hard the edits are in four ways: how many steps it takes to figure things out, how slides depend on each other, how much understanding of meaning is needed, and how much planning is involved. Other tools like PPTC-R and T2US mostly deal with quick edits based on templates, but PPTArena is designed to handle harder, more realistic scenarios to show where systems fail. It uses both decks made by people and ones created with Python, gives clear answers for evaluation, works across different platforms, and includes accessibility requirements. One example task is matching each picture to the right caption on a slide, arranging them in a balanced way, updating the slide titles, and making sure all pictures are the same size, 3.2 inches wide by 2.4 inches high. These tasks are meant to test systems on things like reasoning through multiple steps, connecting information across slides, and planning ahead. This setup helps reveal problems that simpler tasks don’t show and is a good way to test advanced systems. The evaluation looks at two main things: how well instructions are followed and how good the visual results are. These are scored by expert judges on a scale from 0 to 5, where 0 means it failed and 5 means it was perfect.
