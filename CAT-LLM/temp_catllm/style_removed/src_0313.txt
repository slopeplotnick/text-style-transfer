We grouped languages into clusters shown in Figure 2 and called them In-Domain languages. We tested our model on both In-Domain and Out-of-Domain languages. We picked 11 low-resource languages for the Out-of-Domain group because they represent their clusters well and cover different language groups. For testing, we used six safety classification datasets: Aegis-Content-Safety-2.0-Test (Aegis-CS2) from 2025, HarmBench from Mazeika and others in 2024, Redteam2k from Luo and others in 2024, JBB-Behaviors (with Behaviors as JBB-Behav and Judge-comparison as JBB-Judge) from Chao and others in 2024, and StrongReject from Souly and others in 2024. These datasets cover various harm types like Hate/Identity Hate, Sexual, Suicide/Self-Harm, Violence, Guns/Illegal Weapons, PII/Privacy, Sexual Minor, Toxicity, Abuse, and more, making them good for checking safety measures. For training, we used the XLM-RoBERTa-Base with 279 million parameters and XLM-RoBERTa-Large with 560 million parameters as our main multilingual models. These models are good for cross-language tasks and are trained on 100 languages, which fits our goals. We added a single-layer classification head to the encoder that matches the encoder's output size, with a binary output layer. We trained both the encoder and the classification head fully using the combined training data. All training and testing were done on an NVIDIA H100 GPU cluster using Bfloat16 precision for better efficiency and performance. For baseline comparisons, we tested our model against smaller guardrails like DuoGuard-0.5B from 2025, PolyGuard(PG)-Qwen-Smol, WalledGuard-C from 2024, and larger guardrails like PolyGuard(PG)-Qwen from 2025, LlamaGuard3 from 2024, and Aegis-Defensive from 2025. These baselines cover a range of language support, from English-only models to multilingual ones supporting up to 29 languages, allowing us to compare how well our safety guardrail works in both high-resource and low-resource language situations.
