We check how good the citations are by looking at how many right ones are included (Recall), how accurate they are (Precision), and a mix of both (F1). Recall is about how many correct references from a human-made survey are in the computer-made one, showing how much of the literature is covered. Precision checks if the references in the computer-made survey are actually in the human-made list, showing accuracy. This way, we see how close the computer's references are to the human ones, showing how well it matches human research. This method helps us compare how well computers and humans look into literature. Using LLM-as-a-Judge is popular for checking computer-made text because it's easy to scale, consistent, and cheap. Usually, a model is given guidelines and scores to rate the quality of what's produced. But this way can be too easy-going, especially for complex tasks like long surveys. So, we use two versions of LLM-as-a-Judge: one with a human-written survey to check overall content, and one with principles for outline checking. For checking survey quality, we use a setup where the model gets a human survey on the same topic with the computer-made one. The human survey helps the model make better judgments instead of just using guidelines. This setup fixes some problems with just using guidelines. First, having a real expert example helps the model not overrate unclear or shallow content. Second, comparing the computer survey to a detailed human one helps the model notice important things like strong arguments and evidence, which guidelines alone might miss. Third, using a top-quality example helps avoid giving all systems high scores even if they're not all equally good. This way, we can see the differences between systems better and understand what they do well or need to improve in making surveys.
