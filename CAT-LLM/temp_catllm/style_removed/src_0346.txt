Samese got an average F1 score of 84.65 on Assamese tests, while using Hindi got 83.91. Sindhi showed the same pattern, with direct supervision getting an F1 score of 85.59, and the Hindi-trained model getting 86.11 through cross-lingual transfer. For RQ2, we checked our models on 11 low-resource languages, picked from 8 groups. Languages using Latin script, like Galician and Slovenian, did better across tests, probably because the script and subword tokenization in the shared XLM-R vocabulary helped them, as they were better represented during pretraining. Icelandic, even though it uses Latin script too, didn't do as well because its complex structure leads to high token fragmentation and less clear sentence representations, which can make multilingual encoders using subword tokenization less effective. To see how well training on a high-resource language helps transfer within and between groups and answer RQ3, we trained models with Hindi-only, Chinese-only, and Hindi+Chinese combined setups. We tested each model on six languages from the Indic (Hindi) and East-Southeast Asian (Chinese) groups. In the Indic group, the Hindi-trained model did better than the Chinese-trained model for all tests. In the East-Southeast Asian group, the Chinese-trained model did better than the Hindi-trained model for most tests. This shows that transferring within a group from its own high-resource language to other languages in the group works better than transferring across groups from a high-resource language in a different group.
