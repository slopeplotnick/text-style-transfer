The proposed method enhances the precision of actions, as demonstrated in simulation tests. Furthermore, it addresses issues associated with policy failures resulting from inadequate teleoperation data. During the Pen Grasping stage, variations in the operator's grasping techniques and timing result in significant discrepancies in states between grasping modes, leading to a sparse observation-action distribution within the expert dataset. Consequently, the base policy often exhibits instability, such as failing to close the gripper properly, reopening it post-grasping, or toggling between two grasping modes. The implementation of our scaling method during testing effectively mitigates these issues by ensuring correct gripper closure and preventing the robot from entering undesirable modes. This improvement is attributed to the filtering of suboptimal paths induced by noise in the teleoperated dataset by our scaling method. The challenge of mapping from features to action errors complicates learning, as the model must discern both advantageous and disadvantageous features. However, during test-time scaling, the focus is solely on selecting the optimal action. Our experimental results indicate that performance degradation is attributed to overfitting on the training set, thereby impairing generalization. When separate encoders are utilized instead of Internal Features and trained in conjunction with CFN, the features converge to a similar state, complicating accurate pseudo-counting and significantly reducing performance. Additionally, this approach diminishes efficiency by not leveraging VLA's inherent comprehension of scenes and actions. In this study, we introduce TACO, a test-time-scaling framework employing a straightforward pseudo-count estimator as a quality checker for action chunks. TACO is compatible with various VLA models and consistently enhances the base policy across numerous real-world and simulated tests. Despite its limitations, such as its inability to evaluate novel actions generated solely through perturbation and its reliance on the underlying VLA's representational capacity, our method demonstrates that simple test-time scaling effectively curtails exploration and mitigates instability and performance decline caused by distribution shifts in action prediction.
