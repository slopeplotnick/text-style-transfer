In the domain of accounting, a journal entry constitutes a fundamental record of a financial transaction, typically encompassing a debit and a credit entry across one or more accounts. It is essential to note that each entry encapsulates comprehensive details such as the transaction date, an identification number, the currency utilized, the individual responsible for the entry, applicable tax rates, amounts, and occasionally a descriptive note. These elements may manifest as numerical values, categorical labels, or textual information, thus rendering journal entries highly suitable for systems designed to detect atypical patterns within mixed data types. Both synthetic and anonymized datasets exhibit identical critical attributes, thereby facilitating consistent model development and testing. The application of machine learning (ML) by researchers has been prominent in enhancing the detection of anomalous or suspicious entries within accounting datasets. Specifically, unsupervised methodologies, including clustering and Isolation Forest, have been employed to scrutinize journal entries, identifying those that deviate from established patterns, as indicated by Herreros-Martinez et al. [2025]. Recent investigations utilizing synthetic general ledger data have demonstrated that such methods can surpass traditional rule-based systems in performance, as revealed by Gronewald et al. [2024]. Nevertheless, certain challenges persist. Conventional ML models predominantly operate with structured data and may lack the contextual understanding necessary to ascertain whether an entry is genuinely risky or merely appears anomalous. Conversely, Large Language Models (LLMs), exemplified by GPT-4, have introduced novel approaches to auditing practices. These models exhibit proficiency in comprehending and reasoning with linguistic data, and preliminary research suggests their potential utility as AI assistants in financial data analysis, as proposed by Gu et al. [2023]. Furthermore, certain studies have indicated that LLMs can assist in identifying unusual patterns, such as SigLLM for time-series data, as demonstrated by Alnegheimish et al. [2024], and AnoLLM for tabular data, as investigated by Tsai et al. [2025]. These models perform effectively without necessitating extensive data preprocessing. However, the deployment of LLMs for critical tasks such as auditing necessitates careful consideration. LLMs may occasionally produce inconsistent outputs, exhibit biases, or fabricate information. Consequently, mechanisms have been developed to verify the outputs of these models, such as AuditLLM, as developed by Amirizaniani et al. [2024a], and LLMAuditor, as proposed by Amirizaniani et al. [2024b]. These tools underscore the imperative of establishing guidelines and oversight when utilizing LLMs for decision-making processes in domains such as auditing.
