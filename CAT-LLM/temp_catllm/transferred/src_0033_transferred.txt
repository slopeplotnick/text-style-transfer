The present study reveals that the utilization of prompt-engineered large language models (LLMs) in conjunction with Isolation Forest scores demonstrates superior performance in anomaly detection compared to traditional Joint Event Tracking (JETs) and machine learning (ML) methods, by effectively identifying anomalies and providing explanations in accessible language. However, challenges persist in terms of false alarms, cost implications, and practical application in real-world scenarios. Nevertheless, it is demonstrated that artificial intelligence (AI) can enhance auditing accuracy and comprehensibility. Notably, our research is pioneering in illustrating the efficacy of LLMs in processing tax ledger data, which is critical for its clarity and reliability. The findings suggest that integrating LLMs with conventional anomaly signals offers a promising approach to balancing accuracy, recall, and clarity. Future investigations will focus on larger and more diverse datasets to assess the robustness of this approach across varied prompts and model alterations. The current examination was confined to a single anonymized tax ledger and an artificial benchmark, acknowledging the variability of real ledgers across industries, systems, and regions, and the difficulty in obtaining authentic labels. Simulated labels from JETs may introduce biases, with simulated anomalies defined by simulators. Consequently, further evidence is required to validate this method with expansive and heterogeneous datasets and actual audit labels. The stability and transparency of the model are contingent upon the phrasing and model selection, with minor modifications potentially influencing results and discrepancies across different models. Although the AuditCopilot design aims to mitigate inconsistencies, it is not impervious to the impacts of phrasing variations, complex inputs, or model updates. Furthermore, while plain language explanations are perceived as beneficial by auditors, they may not invariably align with decision rules. The application of LLMs in financial audits appears promising based on preliminary assessments, yet risks such as errors, biases, prompt-related challenges, and fluctuations in model weights remain. It is imperative to highlight that, although the proposed method is anticipated to assist decision-making processes, it necessitates human oversight, control mechanisms, and regulatory frameworks prior to deployment in practical settings.
