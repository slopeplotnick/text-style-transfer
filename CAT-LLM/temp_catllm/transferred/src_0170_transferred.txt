The existing methodologies encounter significant challenges in two principal domains. Firstly, concerning data utilization, the majority of studies predominantly employ quantitative and temporal data, neglecting the in-depth integration of textual information. Consequently, this oversight impedes the synthesis of diverse information types, such as market sentiment and policy interpretation. Secondly, the inherent limitations of the models are evident. Although neural networks are proficient in pattern recognition, they do not possess an intrinsic understanding of the underlying implications of situations. They fail to discern causal relationships between events and price fluctuations, thereby rendering them ineffective in intricate market environments. Recent advancements, exemplified by large language models (LLMs), have shifted the focus from mere pattern recognition to the incorporation of memory systems. Notably, FinMem has developed a financial memory system capable of storing summaries of news and events, while MagicNet has introduced a layered memory system that organizes market data temporally. Nevertheless, these methodologies exhibit deficiencies in the configuration of their memory systems, as they generally store discrete pieces of text or data without integrating them into a cohesive narrative of event progression. Furthermore, reliance on semantic similarity complicates the handling of more complex scenarios. Overall, despite the shift towards memory utilization, substantial efforts remain necessary to enhance the understanding and application of relevant information, which constitutes the focal point of this paper. With regard to event-driven stock prediction, this approach endeavors to elucidate the primary causes behind stock price variations. Prior to the advent of LLMs, this field evolved from examining the impact of events on stock returns to the automatic extraction of event signals from text, utilizing rudimentary models such as Bag-of-Words, topic models, and subsequently more advanced deep learning tools like BERT. However, these methodologies share a fundamental limitation: they remain predominantly numerical in focus. Even when extracting event labels or sentiment scores, these elements are merely transformed into features for temporal models such as LSTM or Transformer. Consequently, these models tend to learn patterns within the data rather than the actual event logic, resulting in the loss of crucial details and a lack of deep analytical capability.
