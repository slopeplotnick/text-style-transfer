The present study explores the inadequacies of existing safety configurations designed for human and model interactions, particularly when large language models engage in intercommunication. Current safety protocols predominantly emphasize safeguarding individual models through techniques such as prompting, fine-tuning, and moderation, which are intended to regulate the behavior of a single model. However, these strategies do not adequately address the dynamics of multiple model interactions. It is generally presumed that one model communicates with a singular user under consistent supervision. Nevertheless, both research and industrial sectors are rapidly advancing towards systems characterized by model-to-model communication, wherein models utilize each other's outputs as inputs, forming sequential chains. Within such frameworks, even if each model operates according to its intended function, systemic failures may still occur. Therefore, it is proposed that the focus should shift from the safety of individual models to the overall safety of the interconnected system. This paper introduces the concept of the Emergent Systemic Risk Horizon (ESRH) to illustrate how issues arise from the interactions among models, rather than from isolated model malfunctions. The study provides (i) a theoretical framework for understanding the risks associated with model interactions, (ii) a methodology for correlating various levels of failure, and (iii) a blueprint for Institutional AI, which proposes enhanced oversight mechanisms in systems featuring multiple agents. Traditional safety methodologies assume that a model operates under human supervision, where inappropriate behavior can be mitigated through adjustments to prompts, human feedback learning, or output regulation. Although these approaches have rendered models more dependable and courteous, their efficacy is limited to singular instances. They address the response of one model to one input, without considering the collaborative dynamics of multiple models. As large-scale models become increasingly prevalent in applications, agents, and systems comprising numerous agents, the model-focused safety paradigm begins to falter. Models that perform adequately in isolation may collectively engender issues that no single model is trained to avert, due to feedback loops, replication, or unforeseen cooperation. This situation engenders a discontinuity between ensuring individual model performance and maintaining overall system safety, wherein guaranteeing the functionality of each model component does not equate to effective control over the entire network.
