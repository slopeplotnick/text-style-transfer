The present study aims to demonstrate that large language models (LLMs) are capable of addressing complex optimization problems. Specifically, it investigates the efficacy of LLMs in resource allocation through the utilization of dynamic prompts. Furthermore, a comparative analysis is conducted to evaluate the performance of LLMs against established algorithms such as Deep Q-Network (DQN) and Q-learning. The proposed framework achieves this by deconstructing tasks and optimizing resource allocation strategies. Notably, extensive construction tasks are segmented into smaller, manageable tasks, utilizing strategies derived from the SMART-LLM framework as proposed by Kannan et al. (2024). One strategy categorizes tasks based on the objects involved and the requisite actions, while another emphasizes the necessary skills, such as delicate handling or the application of force. Subsequently, these segmented tasks are allocated to various robots according to their capabilities and task requirements. The LangGraph-based LTAA framework is employed to systematically organize tasks, ensuring task completion through an assessment of each robot's likelihood of success. Initial observations indicated an imbalance in workload distribution among robots, prompting the development of adaptive strategies that prioritize success rates initially and later transition to equitable workload distribution. The developmental process comprised three distinct phases. Initially, the SMART-LLM framework was applied to multi-robot task allocation (MRTA), revealing challenges related to validation and reliability. These issues were addressed in the second phase through the implementation of a Self Corrective-Agent Architecture, incorporating multi-stage validation and retry mechanisms. The final phase involved the integration of these concepts within the LTAA framework, introducing novel allocation strategies for comparison with traditional optimization techniques. The authors elucidate the establishment of a conventional LLM-based MRTA framework, SMART-LLM, and the primary challenges encountered, such as validation and coordination issues. These challenges necessitated the development of a phase-adaptive and self-corrective architecture employing LangGraph, which effectively resolves these concerns through multi-stage validation and retry mechanisms, culminating in the LTAA framework. The study further examines the performance of the LTAA framework against traditional optimization methods using the TEACH dataset, facilitating direct comparisons with Q-learning, DQN, and dynamic programming under identical task conditions. The analysis focuses on workload balance, task execution based on specific features, and the quality of reasoning, thereby evaluating both allocation efficiency and performance outcomes.
