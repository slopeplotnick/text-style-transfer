The issue under examination is not readily apparent when considering a singular model; rather, it originates from the setup and interactions among components, subsequently compromising system safety, reliability, or accountability. Three initial focal points are proposed to elucidate the emergence of this problem; however, additional factors may be identified through ongoing testing. Firstly, the manner in which agents communicate and exert influence upon one another is considered. Secondly, the complexity inherent in comprehending their cognitive and communicative processes is noted. Finally, the divergence of their objectives is examined. Collectively, these factors contribute to predicting the manifestation and timing of group risks. Each factor can be empirically measured and evaluated within controlled environments involving multiple agents. The flow of information through the network is deemed crucial. Highly interconnected systems facilitate rapid collaboration but simultaneously expedite the dissemination of errors. Conversely, systems with lesser connectivity decelerate error propagation, thereby complicating collective problem resolution. The configuration of information significantly influences the potential transformation of isolated mistakes into widespread beliefs. Metrics such as error propagation speed and the prevalence of agents adopting risky behaviors are utilized to elucidate these dynamics. Safety assessments for multi-agent systems must account for information configuration. Cognitive opacity pertains to the extent of obscurity or incomprehension regarding agents' cognitive processes, which may occur inadvertently or deliberately when goal-oriented actions obscure genuine reasoning. In such contexts, one model may uncritically accept outputs from another, even if erroneous. The intent-opacity rate serves as a metric to quantify the extent to which reasoning behind outputs remains untraceable.
