System prompts are instrumental in controlling the behaviors of large language models (LLMs), including the assignment of roles or styles for specific tasks. Previous studies have demonstrated that elements such as personas, emotions, and jailbreak triggers have the potential to alter the tone and cognitive processes of responses; however, they do not invariably enhance task accuracy. Although the use of system prompts has increased, there remains a paucity of structured methodologies for their improvement. Some leaked system prompts from Grok and Claude contain intricate rules crafted manually. Furthermore, recent approaches have revealed that system prompts can be optimized through genetic algorithms, albeit this has been predominantly explored with a single language and a solitary type of measurement. When employing multiple languages, challenges arise such as language-specific issues and interlingual disparities. Multilingual LLMs frequently produce divergent responses to identical queries in different languages due to varying levels of linguistic proficiency. Techniques such as Cross-Lingual Thought Prompting and multilingual self-consistency offer partial solutions but typically necessitate multiple rounds of prompting. Consequently, a comprehensive approach for enhancing system prompts across all languages remains elusive. In addition to standard metrics, recent investigations have begun to analyze the cognitive processes of LLMs by deconstructing responses into comprehensible components. Yet, these studies predominantly focus on superficial aspects, such as employing text prefill to manage output language, without delving into the cognitive mechanisms of the model. Our research endeavors to address this gap by associating system prompt design with cognitive patterns across multiple languages and elucidating how these patterns correlate with effective performance in a multilingual context.
