This study investigates the capability of a computer program designed to simulate human language acquisition in independently determining grammatical structures and lexical categories through interaction with a language model known as Delilah, which provides examples of adult language usage. Upon acquiring this grammatical knowledge, the program, referred to as the daughter model, is able to utilize it to engage in dialogue with another program, the mother model. Specifically, the daughter model is capable of generating novel sentences based on the learned grammar and communicating these sentences to the mother model during their interaction. Under certain conditions, the mother model is permitted to offer evaluative feedback concerning the grammatical accuracy, which the daughter model can employ to enhance its linguistic competence. Furthermore, the daughter model applies the acquired knowledge to comprehend new sentences from the mother model, including the identification of lexical categories utilized by the mother model. It is established in prior research that words serving grammatical functions exhibit high frequency, whereas words conveying specific meanings tend to occur less frequently, with some content words appearing only once or rarely within a given text. This distribution pattern aligns with Zipf's law, which postulates that the most frequently used words in a language are considerably more prevalent than less common words, a phenomenon investigated by scholars such as Mandelbrot and Zipf. Additionally, there exists a correlation between the distribution of words in languages and the grammatical categories examined by linguists. This article aims to ascertain whether a similar correlation is observable in language generated by a computer program.
