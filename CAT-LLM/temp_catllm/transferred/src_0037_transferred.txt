The concept of self-consistency pertains to obtaining a multitude of outcomes and adopting the consensus among them. Soft self-consistency involves selecting the lowest, average, or aggregated confidence scores within each group, while generative self-aggregation employs the language model to synthesize a novel response based on diverse samples. Universal self-consistency engages the language model in electing the answer that aligns optimally among multiple options. The focus of our investigation is directed towards certain language models, namely gemini-2.5-flash, qwen3 next-80b, and gpt-oss-120b, across two machine translation tasks and six tasks derived from the Reward Bench 2 benchmark. This benchmark serves to evaluate reward models across six dimensions: factuality, precise adherence to instructions, mathematical accuracy, safety, concentration, and ties. For the evaluation process, preference pairs were constructed by matching all conceivable pairs from each task's source data, which include both accepted and rejected responses. These pairs were categorized into 'non-tie' pairs, consisting of one accepted and one rejected response, and 'tie' pairs, comprising either two accepted or two rejected responses. From this extensive dataset, 1000 examples were selected for each of the six tasks to constitute the final benchmark. The vote distributions for each task are elucidated comprehensively in Appendix B. The results pertaining to various language models, including gemini-2.5-flash, gpt-oss-120b, and qwen3 next-80b, exhibit a consistent pattern, thereby indicating the efficacy of our method across these models. The structural attributes of the problem encompass the frequency with which the task generates ambiguous cases, the frequency of definitive versus tied votes produced by the language model, and the interactions of these characteristics with the MAE-aligned Davidson model. Certain tasks possess smooth calibration landscapes conducive to application in other contexts, whereas others exhibit more abrupt landscapes that are less adaptable. Developing robust tests to foretell when one task may be applicable to another, and to evaluate resilience amidst substantial changes in distribution or targets beyond the conventional scope, presents a compelling avenue for future exploration.
