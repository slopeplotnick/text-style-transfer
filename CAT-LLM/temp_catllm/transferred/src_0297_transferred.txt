Experiment 2 revealed that the utilization of a singular system prompt significantly enhances performance across the tested languages, indicating a systematic alteration in the model's behavior. The investigation focused on understanding alterations in the model's output by examining reasoning steps and analyzing the correlation between changes in reasoning and performance across various languages. The inquiry commenced with an examination of the languages employed within the model's reasoning steps. Figure 7 illustrates that, despite utilizing identical random prompts, the model exhibits language preferences contingent upon the language of the question, which aligns with the availability of training data for each language. For instance, in model families such as Qwen, the abundance of Chinese data results in the model responding to Chinese queries in Chinese nearly as frequently as it does in English. Following enhancements to the prompts, these language preferences become more pronounced, with the model predominantly responding in the language of the question rather than defaulting to English, as observed with the initial system prompt. Nonetheless, for languages with limited training data, such as Hindi, the proportion of English responses remains relatively unchanged despite improved prompt design. Furthermore, it was determined that languages other than the task language and English do not substantially contribute to the model's performance, as their presence diminishes following prompt optimization. Additional insights into language identification are available in Appendix A.9, and further data is presented in Tables 6 and 7. Figure 8 provides an analysis of reasoning behavior across different languages, revealing consistent overall patterns, yet specific language preferences exist. For example, English exhibits a propensity for Reframing, Chinese for Logical Reasoning, and Spanish for calculation. Conversely, languages with sparse training data, such as Hindi, show a prevalence of the Other category, indicating a lack of structured reasoning practice during training. Despite the consistency of these trends, they appear to be influenced less by prompt design and more by language-specific patterns inherent in model pretraining.
