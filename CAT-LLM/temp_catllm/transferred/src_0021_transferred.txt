The present study undertook an evaluation of the Swivuriso dataset by implementing training on three distinct speech recognition models, namely Whisper-large-v3-Turbo, Wav2Vec-BERT 2.0, and Massively Multilingual Speech (MMS). The Word Error Rate (WER) for each model was documented after 1,000 and 10,000 training steps, as illustrated in Table 6. It was observed that extended training durations resulted in enhanced model performance, with Whisper-large-v3-Turbo demonstrating the most significant improvement by 0.22, followed by Wav2Vec-BERT at 0.27, and MMS at 0.15. Furthermore, an investigation was conducted into the impact of training data quantity on model efficacy. Specifically, Whisper-v3-turbo was subjected to training for one round utilizing datasets comprising 50 hours, 150 hours, and 250 hours of data, respectively. The experimental outcomes revealed that the Swivuriso dataset is instrumental in augmenting speech recognition capabilities across diverse models and languages. It was consistently found that training led to reductions in error rates, even during the preliminary phases of training, and the addition of training data contributed positively to performance across most languages. Consequently, these findings affirm that Swivuriso constitutes a valuable resource for advancing South African speech recognition and enhancing multilingual speech models. The creation of high-quality datasets for speech recognition, which demands high precision and minimal errors, has been extensively pursued by other researchers. However, progress concerning African languages and other low-resource languages has been relatively slow. A study conducted by Abdulmumin et al. focused on the FLORES dataset to improve its utility for evaluating four African languages: Hausa, Northern Sotho (Sepedi), Xitsonga, and isiZulu. Native speakers participated in reviewing and rectifying dataset issues that could potentially impact tasks such as machine translation, thereby increasing the dataset's accuracy and reliability, as demonstrated through both reviews and data analysis. This study highlighted the critical importance of involving native speakers to ensure datasets are culturally and linguistically appropriate. In alignment with the recommendations from that study, native speakers were similarly engaged to enhance the accuracy and quality of our dataset. Complementary insights were derived from another study concentrated on sentiment analysis, which underscored the availability of numerous sentiment analysis datasets for widely spoken languages but not for low-resource languages such as Hindi. This particular study addressed sentiment analysis in Hindi, emphasizing socially relevant topics, and developed a balanced dataset incorporating positive, negative, and neutral sentiment labels.
