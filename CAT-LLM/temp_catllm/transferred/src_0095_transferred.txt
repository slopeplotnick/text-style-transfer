The present study investigates an energy model that demonstrates the consumption of energy by statically typed programming languages operating on the Java Virtual Machine (JVM). Specifically, the research analyzes the influence of factors such as basic data types, data sizes, operations, and varying devices on energy utilization. Moreover, it reveals that even devices with identical hardware may exhibit disparate energy consumption, thereby complicating the general prediction of energy use. The paper is structured as follows: Section 2 outlines the background on Bayesian statistics, Section 3 elucidates the methodology employed, Section 4 discusses the practical implementation of the method, Section 5 examines the testing procedures and potential issues with the results, Section 6 presents the test outcomes, Section 7 offers a discussion of these results, Section 8 reviews related work, and Section 9 concludes the study. Bayesian statistics, a method of utilizing prior knowledge to formulate probability-based estimates regarding unobservable phenomena, is contrasted with frequentist statistics, which is more prevalent in computer science. Although both methods aim to interpret models, frequentist statistics rely solely on observed data while eschewing assumptions, whereas Bayesian statistics incorporate assumptions, rendering them explicit for verification. The application of Bayesian statistics is justified for two reasons: firstly, it clarifies and facilitates explanation of assumptions, and secondly, it predicts energy consumption more comprehensively by offering a range of potential outcomes as opposed to a singular estimate, which is typical of frequentist approaches. Within this paper, it is postulated that energy consumption adheres to a normal distribution, falling within a range informed by prior research. This range serves as a preliminary point for modification via a Bayesian algorithm to align more closely with empirical measurements. In instances of limited data availability, initial assumptions may exert significant influence, potentially leading to inaccurate results; however, with increased data, the algorithm can rectify initial assumption errors and enhance model-data congruence.
