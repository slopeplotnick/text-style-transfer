The initial focus of this study pertains to the foundational research on language models employing Bayesian methods and n-grams, which were instrumental in enhancing speech recognition systems by addressing word sequences and uncertainties inherent in human speech. These models significantly contributed to the development of contemporary systems utilized in speech processing. Subsequently, attention is directed toward advanced speech models that operate in an end-to-end manner, enabling the direct incorporation of contextual information within the processing workflow. Such models extend beyond the analysis of lexical elements to encompass features including tone, emotion, and speaker identity, thereby facilitating improved comprehension and generation of speech. Furthermore, the role of large language models (LLMs) in rectifying errors within automatic speech recognition (ASR) systems is examined, with specific emphasis on issues surrounding fairness and bias, particularly concerning the impact of accents, dialects, and diverse speaking styles on speech processing outcomes. Emerging solutions addressing these challenges are explored alongside ethical considerations to ensure equitable system functionality. The discussion further incorporates theoretical perspectives on how LLMs adapt to speech input, encompassing aspects such as risk measurement for various demographic groups and the evaluation of transferability between speech models and LLM adaptation, which subsequently informs the development of design principles for spoken agents. Building upon these insights, methodologies integrating text and speech in training processes are analyzed, highlighting advancements in understanding through the alignment of dual learning objectives. Additionally, the capacity of LLMs to estimate voice quality and adapt dynamically to varying speakers in real-time is investigated, underscoring the potential for enhanced system performance and user interaction.
