Additionally, the placement of padding significantly impacts model performance, as positioning the context following the task description has been observed to result in diminished performance relative to placing it beforehand. Large language model (LLM) agents, which are artificial intelligence systems leveraging pre-trained language models in iterative loops, are designed to make decisions and manage tasks involving multiple steps and interactions autonomously. Context window capacities have expanded substantially, increasing from 2,000 tokens in GPT-3 to 2 million tokens in Grok 4 Fast, facilitated by advancements in position embeddings and attention mechanisms. Nevertheless, these models continue to exhibit a propensity to prioritize recent information while demonstrating limited retention of earlier inputs. Empirical evaluations, such as those conducted using NIAH and LongBench, reveal that while larger context windows enable models to process more extensive information, they do not necessarily enhance cognitive capabilities or the efficient application of the ingested data. Safety evaluations of LLMs focus on aspects such as toxicity, the dissemination of misinformation, and potential vulnerabilities to adversarial inputs, with LongSafety assessing the performance of models when processing extended inputs of up to 5,000 tokens. In contrast, safety assessments for agents primarily examine their performance in interactive tasks, including their resilience to prompt injection attacks, as evaluated by AgentSecurity and Agent Dojo, as well as their handling of risks associated with executing code, as tested by RedCode and MobileSafetyBench. AgentHarm serves as a framework designed to evaluate agents' responses to harmful tasks and their capability to execute workflows involving multiple steps. Recent modifications to the AgentHarm framework have incorporated the assessment of agents' behavior and performance when exposed to long-context inputs in both safe and harmful task scenarios. Additionally, AgentHarm examines the extent to which agents effectively utilize tools during multi-step tasks. The framework includes tools that are manually configured to simulate real-world systems, such as Google Search or an API for social media posting, thereby enabling comprehensive control over task execution.
