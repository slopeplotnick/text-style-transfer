Fact verification methods are significantly influenced by the effectiveness of the retrieval component and the availability of sufficient external information. Specifically, as evidenced in Table 3, inaccuracies in the retrieval process, particularly the failure to supply requisite evidence, result in substantial challenges for subsequent verification tasks. When retrieval mechanisms are unable to locate relevant information, potentially due to keyword mismatches within the LLM-Q configuration, the verification component is compelled to make determinations in the absence of adequate supporting data. This insufficiency often leads to anomalous model behavior, either by generating unwarranted skepticism, thereby producing false positives (as observed in Case 1), or by erroneously accepting inaccurate information without proper scrutiny, leading to oversight of errors (as demonstrated in Case 2). Conversely, when the retrieval process incorporates generated responses via the LLM-QA setup, it typically identifies pertinent documents more effectively, thereby enabling the fact verification model to produce more accurate assessments. Regarding hallucination detection, existing methods frequently encounter difficulties due to the inherent flexibility of language generation. Table 4 illustrates that hallucination detection techniques perform satisfactorily with straightforward responses (Group A) but exhibit reduced accuracy when addressing more complex or creative formulations (Group B). In such scenarios, models may display confidence in factual accuracy, such as identifying the formation date of a band, yet demonstrate uncertainty in articulating the information. Current hallucination detection approaches are unable to reliably distinguish between legitimate variations in phrasing and actual falsehoods, resulting in the erroneous flagging of diverse but valid expressions as inaccuracies. Consequently, scenarios involving high linguistic variability are often misclassified as errors despite their factual correctness. The findings from this study underscore the distinct limitations associated with both hallucination detection and fact verification, highlighting the necessity of a hybrid approach. Fact verification methods, characterized by their precision, are highly effective when supported by accurate evidence but are limited in scenarios where such evidence is unavailable. Conversely, hallucination detection functions as a broader safeguard capable of identifying potential errors even in the absence of external information; however, it is prone to misinterpretation in cases of linguistic flexibility. Therefore, the optimal hybrid framework should differentiate between the two methodologies, prioritizing fact verification when sufficient evidence is available while leveraging hallucination detection signals in instances where retrieval processes fail.
