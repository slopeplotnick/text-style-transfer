Concerns regarding the legal and ethical implications associated with generative models have increasingly intensified, particularly in relation to the utilization of copyrighted materials during model training processes. A critical issue under scrutiny pertains to the attribution of credit for creative outputs and the moral rights inherently connected to such creations. Legal disputes have emerged, asserting that the usage of copyrighted content without explicit authorization constitutes a violation of intellectual property rights. For example, the New York Times has initiated litigation against OpenAI due to the alleged unauthorized use of its news archives, while Getty Images has filed a lawsuit against Stability AI for generating outputs derived from its proprietary database. In addition to these legal proceedings, artists have actively voiced their concerns regarding potential infringements on human creativity, exemplified by a letter signed by Elton John, Dua Lipa, and 200 other artists urging technology companies to safeguard creative expression against artificial intelligence systems capable of replicating vocal and stylistic elements without consent. In response to these developments, various institutions have proposed regulatory frameworks aimed at addressing these challenges. The European Union's AI Act advocates for transparency in AI systems, whereas the United Kingdom and UNESCO have introduced guidelines emphasizing the importance of privacy and intellectual property protection. Notably, Denmark has proposed amendments to its copyright legislation, granting individuals rights over their voice, facial features, and bodily likeness—an unprecedented initiative within the European context. This legislative proposal is specifically designed to address the legal complexities arising from AI's replication of personal attributes, with Denmark's culture minister emphasizing the necessity of safeguarding individuals' identity against generative AI technologies. Despite these regulatory advancements, the operational mechanisms of most generative models remain opaque, rendering it difficult to ascertain the influence of training data on their outputs. Even open-source models pose substantial challenges in terms of reproducibility due to their scale and complexity. Contemporary approaches such as influence functions or retraining-based attribution require access to internal model structures, further complicating efforts to achieve transparency and accountability.
