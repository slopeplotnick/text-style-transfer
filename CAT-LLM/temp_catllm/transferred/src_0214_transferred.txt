The phenomenon wherein adversarial training facilitates the transferability of attacks is not attributable to specific model architectures but rather to the alterations in information processing induced by adversarial training itself. This effect is observed across diverse model designs, underscoring its significance in the context of overall security considerations. To investigate the underlying mechanisms through which adversarial training enhances transferability, researchers employed the integrated gradients method to examine how models rationalize their decision-making processes. Specifically, an illustrative comparison was conducted between two versions of the DenseNet121 model: one subjected to adversarial training (AT) and the other trained conventionally (ST). The findings revealed that the AT model demonstrated a focus on essential components of an image, such as the head, body, and legs of an ostrich, whereas the ST model concentrated more on granular details, including feather textures and background elements. This distinction is critical as AT models prioritize generalizable features that are prevalent across a wide array of images, in contrast to the ST models, which emphasize specific details that are less effective for transferability between models. Consequently, adversarial examples generated by AT models exhibit superior efficacy across different models due to their targeting of features that are commonly shared among models. As an example, DenseNet121 and DenseNet201 were identified to possess analogous image processing characteristics, while MobileNetV3 was similarly vulnerable owing to its incorporation of design principles derived from EfficientNet. Such similarities contribute to the susceptibility of MobileNetV3 to adversarial attacks originating from EfficientNet-based models.
