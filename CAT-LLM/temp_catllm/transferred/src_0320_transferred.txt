Enhanced taxonomic frameworks recognize the unique challenges faced by construction sites when robots with specialized skills are employed to execute complex tasks. These challenges differentiate construction from controlled environments where traditional methods have historically proven effective. Construction sites are inherently chaotic, characterized by fluctuating material flows, adverse weather conditions, and intricate interactions among various trades. Consequently, systems are required that can adeptly manage spatial constraints, temporal dependencies, and operations critical to safety. Furthermore, human oversight remains indispensable in construction, as it cannot be pre-set or easily programmed. These challenges necessitate flexible coordination methods capable of adapting to evolving conditions and disorganized environments. Traditional optimization methods have endeavored to address these challenges through diverse algorithmic strategies. Classical optimization methods serve as the bedrock for established multi-robot coordination approaches, providing mathematical precision and performance assurance via an array of computational techniques. Kuhn (1955) established the theoretical foundation for optimal assignment problems with the Hungarian method, demonstrating that task-resource matching could be efficiently resolved through linear programming. However, the Hungarian method was constrained to simple one-to-one assignments and was incapable of accommodating varying robot skills or task dependencies. Within the construction domain, robots possess specialized skills such as heavy lifting or precision assembly, and workflows necessitate coordinated task sequences with explicit dependencies. These limitations have prompted the development of more sophisticated optimization methods. Atay and Bayazit (2006) advanced mixed-integer linear programming (MILP) for multi-robot task allocation, taking into account robot skill constraints, task order relationships, and communication limitations. Military and aerospace sectors have refined MILP-based coordination with advanced techniques that address diverse skills and timing constraints. Consequently, MILP offers substantial advantages in modeling complex multi-robot scenarios with clear constraint satisfaction. Nevertheless, MILP approaches rely on centralized optimization systems, which encounter scalability challenges when coordinating large teams of robots. Construction projects often involve numerous robots dispersed across expansive and disordered sites with restricted communication, rendering centralized coordination impractical. These scalability issues have catalyzed the adoption of distributed coordination methods.
