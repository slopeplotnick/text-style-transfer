The expansion of a French-Ciluba sentiment word list to encompass translations in English, Afrikaans, isiZulu, Sesotho, and Sepedi is currently being pursued, necessitating the utilization of translation tools to facilitate cross-language word matching based on contextual usage. In order to enhance the word list, a method known as Pointwise Mutual Information (PMI) is employed to identify words that convey emotion or opinion within African language texts and subsequently link them to pre-existing entries in the list. Furthermore, a system referred to as Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG), which operates in conjunction with large language models, is utilized to resolve ambiguities concerning word meanings and ensure translation accuracy. Subsequently, two models, AfriBERTa and AfroXLMR, are trained to perform emotion or opinion classification in text utilizing this revised word list. The performance of these models is evaluated by examining metrics such as precision, recall, F1-score, and Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC). To facilitate interpretability, tools that highlight text segments influencing the models' decision-making processes are employed. Ultimately, a comparative analysis is conducted to assess the performance of individual models versus ensemble approaches to determine potential improvements in accuracy. The paper is organized as follows: initially, a review of existing work in sentiment analysis, multilingual word list development, and African language processing is provided. Subsequently, the framework and methodologies are elucidated, followed by a detailed account of the experimental procedures and model testing. The results are then discussed in terms of their implications for multilingual natural language processing. Historically, sentiment analysis has predominantly relied on machine learning or word lists to ascertain textual sentiment polarity. Early studies demonstrated the efficacy of statistical methods, while subsequent research affirmed the utility of word lists for simpler, rule-based methods. Widely used word lists such as SentiWordNet, NRC Emotion Lexicon, and VADER prove effective for resource-rich languages but lack coverage for more complex languages or those with limited data availability. Efforts have been made to develop multilingual word lists by extracting words from textual corpora.
