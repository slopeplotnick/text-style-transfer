The origins of the methodologies under consideration are not the primary focus of this investigation. Hallucination Detection (HD) methods rely on internal model states that Fact Verification (FV) settings are unable to provide. Efforts to address this limitation have included the development of static benchmarks for outputs generated by large language models (LLMs), such as HELM; however, these benchmarks are inadequate for evaluating novel or atypical models and for tracking variations in generative signals. Consequently, this disconnect has resulted in the emergence of two distinct research trajectories, leading to inefficiencies and unresolved critical inquiries. Specifically, there remains uncertainty regarding the comparative effectiveness of these approaches, the differentiation of their respective strengths, and their potential for integration. The separation of these methodologies further inhibits the synergistic utilization of their advantages, wherein HD’s reliance on internal signals could enhance FV’s evidence-based reasoning, and FV’s contextual grounding might contribute to HD’s uncertainty assessment. To address this divide, the present research undertakes a significant initiative by conducting the first comprehensive study aimed at directly comparing Hallucination Detection and Fact Verification within a unified framework. This endeavor extends beyond merely establishing benchmarks to provide an in-depth examination of the interconnections between these two approaches. By integrating these methodologies, three pivotal research questions are addressed: (1) How do HD and FV perform in identifying factual inaccuracies within identical LLM outputs? (2) Do HD and FV exhibit complementary strengths by targeting different categories of errors, or do they fail in analogous contexts? (3) Is it feasible to merge signals derived from both methods to develop a hybrid system with enhanced effectiveness compared to each method individually? While prior assumptions suggested that such a comparison was unachievable due to the inherent differences between the two approaches, this study introduces UniFact, a framework designed to resolve the incompatibility that has historically fragmented this research domain. Rather than relying on static outputs from LLMs, UniFact employs a dynamic process wherein any LLM is prompted to respond to factual queries in real time. Subsequently, an automated system evaluates the accuracy of these responses by comparing them to verified answers and pre-labeled reference knowledge. The dynamic nature of this framework is integral, as it simultaneously provides the outputs required for FV and the internal signals essential for HD, thereby facilitating a direct and systematic comparison of the two methodologies on identical content.
