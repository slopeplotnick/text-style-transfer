Flow-based diffusion models have emerged as a prominent choice for training generative models capable of creating high-quality images and videos. However, the mechanisms by which these models retain and generalize information remain inadequately understood. This study revisits the flow matching objective and examines its marginal velocity field, which can be precisely computed, thereby facilitating the identification of the exact FM target. Analysis of this velocity field reveals that these models inherently possess a dual-phase training objective: initially emphasizing a synthesis of diverse data types, and subsequently concentrating on the nearest data instance. This bifurcated objective engenders distinct learning actions: the initial phase aids in the comprehension of various data types, thereby fostering the generation of overarching patterns, while the latter phase accentuates the retention of minute details. Utilizing this understanding, the efficacy of certain techniques such as temporal step adjustments, classifier-free guidance, and latent space design choices is elucidated. Consequently, this research advances the comprehension of diffusion model training dynamics and offers insights for prospective enhancements in design and algorithmic strategies. Diffusion models represent a robust category of generative methodologies, adept at producing quality samples across domains including image and video generation. These models acquire intricate data patterns by progressively transforming an initial distribution, typically Gaussian, into the target data distribution through processes involving denoising or transport directions. Various approaches have been proposed to characterize this diffusion process, encompassing probabilistic models, score-based ODEs/SDEs, and flow matching, which lead to differing training objectives such as predicting noise, score, or velocity, and are associated with diverse configurations of the probability flow ODE. Recently, consensus has largely coalesced around the flow matching method, where the model is trained to predict the velocity field utilizing a straightforward schedule. Owing to its simplicity and stable training dynamics, this method has attained prominence as the standard for training leading diffusion models.
