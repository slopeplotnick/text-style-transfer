Researchers have devised two principal methodologies to address the substantial issue of factual accuracy in information processing, namely, Fact Verification (FV) and Hallucination Detection (HD). Fact Verification, a well-established domain, involves the validation of facts through the utilization of resources such as web pages or databases, irrespective of whether the statement originates from human or machine sources; it essentially determines the veracity of claims based upon corroborative evidence. Conversely, Hallucination Detection has emerged contemporaneously with the advent of large language models (LLMs) and is focused particularly on content generated by these models. Unlike FV, which scrutinizes text in isolation, HD incorporates the generative process of the text, emphasizing intrinsic model signals such as word certainty, activation patterns, and consistency across iterations. Consequently, HD primarily concerns itself with the model's internal mechanisms, enabling the identification of fabricated content even in the absence of external corroboration. Although both techniques aim to identify factual inaccuracies, they have evolved independently, employing distinct evaluation metrics, success measurements, and publication venues due to their fundamentally divergent assessment methodologies. Specifically, FV concentrates on text itself, utilizing evaluation frameworks such as FEVER, where claims are examined as discrete textual units.
