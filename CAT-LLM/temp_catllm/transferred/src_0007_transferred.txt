Innovation in model development continues to progress; however, the practical application of these models necessitates consideration of certain decisions which often receive limited attention in academic discourse. Specifically, defining virality, selecting data types (such as text or numerical data), and evaluating models amidst real-world constraints such as accessibility, temporal factors, and class imbalance are crucial choices. This study prioritizes evaluation in decision-making processes. Two contemporary datasets are analyzed to demonstrate that label design and data selection, whether solely text-based or incorporating additional signals, are as impactful as modifications to the model itself. Clear examples are provided to elucidate these trade-offs. Although the focus remains on evaluation design rather than on novel models, it is demonstrated that simpler models can achieve comparable, if not superior, performance relative to newer multimodal and graph-based systems on standard benchmarks like FAKENewsNET. Specifically, the study examines tasks related to fake news detection and early virality prediction to ascertain the data and definitions critical for maintaining stable performance under real-world conditions. Text data, such as sentence-level transformer embeddings, are compared with basic numeric data, including user statistics, initial engagement figures, and timing information. These tasks undergo testing on datasets with varying structures: one comprising standalone news items and another involving sequential social media posts. Two supervised classification tasks are undertaken across two datasets, culminating in four test scenarios: fake news detection and virality prediction on EvONS and FAKENewsNET. For fake news detection, predictions are made regarding the veracity of items, be they articles or tweet series, on both EvONS articles and FakeNewsNET tweet series, with results reported using standard metrics. With respect to FAKENewsNET, tweet series constructed from the repository are utilized. The data are anonymized with IDs and necessitate API hydration; the Politifact split was sourced from prior academic collaborations, although GossipCop was irretrievable due to current limitations. Each instance constitutes a series of tweets pertaining to the same news article, illustrating the dissemination process.
