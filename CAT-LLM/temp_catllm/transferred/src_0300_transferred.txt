The present study examines the parallels between neural processes and artificial intelligence (AI) systems, with a particular emphasis on the concept of a "world model," defined as a predictive framework facilitating comprehension and anticipation of events. Specifically, the investigation compares the mechanisms by which the brain and AI manage information, highlighting convergent strategies observed in large language models. This analysis has led to the formulation of a novel perspective regarding large-scale information processing within the brain. Notably, regions such as the neocortex and cerebellum appear to construct predictive models by anticipating future occurrences, learning iteratively from prediction errors, and subsequently applying these models across diverse functional domains, including sensory perception, auditory processing, and motor control. This conceptual framework integrates established theories, namely the internal model theory and the mirror neuron system, proposing that the existence of such predictive systems is integral to achieving multifunctionality within a unified structural paradigm. Moreover, this perspective may facilitate the integration of neuroscientific and AI research, thereby advancing the understanding of human-like intelligence. The cerebellum, in particular, performs a predictive role by assimilating external sensory inputs, generating anticipatory hypotheses, and adapting based on discrepancies between expected and actual outcomes.
