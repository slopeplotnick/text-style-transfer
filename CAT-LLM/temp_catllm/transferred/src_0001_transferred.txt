In the realm of model evaluation, certain models demonstrate greater reliability compared to others. This review investigates the impact of model size, structure, and training data on the occurrence of hallucinations, which are patterns identified across various models. A study reveals these patterns and proposes methodologies for amelioration, such as enhanced training techniques and the imposition of constraints on outputs. Furthermore, another study indicates that hallucinations constitute a significant issue for large language models (LLMs) irrespective of their size or training approaches. It is suggested that selecting appropriate models and training methodologies may mitigate hallucinations, although the authors posit that achieving a complete absence of hallucinations remains implausible. This notion holds significance as it aids in establishing realistic objectives and assessing model suitability for specific tasks. When considering the selection of an LLM to minimize hallucinations, factors such as model architecture, the quality and diversity of training data, and strategies including fine-tuning or supplementary information integration warrant consideration. Presently, a multitude of models are accessible, such as GPT, BERT, and T5. GPT frequently produces coherent and well-structured text; however, these models are susceptible to generating erroneous information, termed hallucinations. BERT, due to its bidirectional training for particular tasks, exhibits fewer hallucinations, although it may still fabricate information. Models like PaLM and LLaMa have been developed with the intention of reducing hallucinations, yet their efficacy is contingent upon their design and training methodologies. Understanding the capabilities and limitations of these models is imperative when selecting an appropriate tool for tasks where accurate information is paramount and the minimization of hallucinations is desired.
