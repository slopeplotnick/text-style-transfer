This study examines an approach for deriving meaningful information from visual data through the application of advanced language models capable of integrating diverse data types with specific knowledge systems, ultimately generating comprehensible knowledge graphs. Additionally, a process has been developed utilizing these graphs to evaluate the similarity between model-generated images and the original training data, thereby enhancing interpretability. The proposed methodology demonstrates utility in various applications, including ownership attribution, improving interpretability, and ensuring data provenance, thereby illustrating its potential to enhance traceability in generative models. The method was evaluated on both locally trained models and large-scale models through an experimental framework focusing on Ghibli-style images, aiming to assess its capacity to identify stylistic influences even in the absence of direct model interpretability. The structure of the paper is as follows: Section 2 reviews related work concerning the interpretability of generative models and the construction of knowledge graphs using advanced language models; Section 3 details the proposed method; Section 4 presents the experimental setup and results obtained from both local and large-scale models; and Section 5 concludes with a summary of key findings and potential directions for future research. As generative models continue to advance, understanding their mechanisms has become increasingly significant across technical, ethical, and policy domains. While general interpretability techniques, such as t-SNE, LIME, and attention visualization, offer insights into model behavior, they do not directly address the provenance of training data, which remains a critical issue in contexts such as authorship attribution and copyright. To investigate the influence of training data on generative outputs, various techniques have been proposed, including early methods such as influence functions, retraining-based attribution, and Shapley value approximations. Although these methods have demonstrated efficacy in specific domains, such as classification tasks, they are often impractical for large-scale models due to their reliance on gradients, retraining access, or substantial computational resources. Consequently, these approaches are seldom employed in contemporary deep learning frameworks, particularly for generative models with opaque internal mechanisms.
