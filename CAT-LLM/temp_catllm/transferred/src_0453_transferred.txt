The findings indicate that employing reasoning to enhance safety alignment significantly improves the efficacy of omnimodal guardrails across diverse input modalities, thereby facilitating comprehension and generalization in complex safety scenarios where reliance solely on labels is insufficient. To assess the transferability of safety knowledge acquired from one input type to another, a series of evaluations were conducted. Specifically, OMNIGUARD was trained utilizing data from three distinct input types and subsequently tested on the same types, excluding one type to evaluate its performance on unfamiliar inputs. The F1 scores and accuracy metrics were averaged across all evaluations, with the results presented in Table 5. Two primary conclusions were drawn from these analyses. Firstly, OMNIGUARD demonstrates robust capability in transferring learned patterns from one input type to another, evidenced by performance on novel input types nearly matching those on the training types (79.4 vs. 81.8 F1 on average). This finding reveals OMNIGUARD's aptitude in identifying harmful patterns across various formats such as text, images, videos, and audio, thereby acquiring universally applicable safety rules. Secondly, an anomaly was observed concerning audio inputs. The F1 score for audio inputs it was trained on (64.6%) was marginally lower than for audio inputs it was untrained on (65.1%). This discrepancy arose because the OMNIGUARD version trained without textual data exhibited reduced efficacy on the WildGuard-TTS test, which constitutes an audio format derived from text via a text-to-speech model. The absence of textual data in the training phase impeded the model's proficiency in processing audio, illustrating that harmful content across different modalities, such as text and speech, can exert mutual influence in the context of learning safety rules. Furthermore, it underscores the potential for knowledge acquired from one modality to be applicable in another, conveying equivalent meaning.
