The area under the curve (AUC) metric is employed to assess the ranking performance of models independently of specific decision thresholds, a feature particularly advantageous when dealing with imbalanced datasets. This study introduces an innovative evaluation framework that integrates Hallucination Detection and Fact Verification, two domains that have historically been examined in isolation. The initial focus of the investigation centers on elucidating the reasons for the separation of these fields and identifying the challenges inherent in existing evaluation paradigms that impede the simultaneous assessment of both aspects. Subsequently, the foundational principles and operational mechanisms of the proposed framework are elaborated upon. A critical preliminary consideration involves understanding the rationale behind the historical separation of these domains. The primary constraint arises from the divergent nature of traditional Fact Verification datasets and Hallucination Detection methodologies. Fact Verification datasets, exemplified by FEVER, typically consist of static claim-label pairs that disregard the provenance of claims, whereas Hallucination Detection methods necessitate an analysis of generative processes, including internal model states and cross-sample consistency. These generative signals are absent in conventional Fact Verification datasets, thereby complicating the concurrent evaluation of both domains. A potential solution involves the construction of evaluation datasets utilizing pre-generated outputs from large language models, such as HELM. While this approach offers certain advantages, it is accompanied by notable limitations within the rapidly evolving landscape of artificial intelligence. Specifically, datasets constructed using current state-of-the-art models, such as LLaMA-3, risk becoming obsolete as newer models emerge, thereby rendering the datasets historical rather than contemporarily relevant. Furthermore, fixed evaluation frameworks are inherently incapable of assessing new or diverse models, which is particularly problematic for Hallucination Detection methods that often rely on model-specific characteristics. Consequently, systems constrained by fixed tests hinder methodological advancements and fail to adequately demonstrate adaptability within dynamic research contexts.
