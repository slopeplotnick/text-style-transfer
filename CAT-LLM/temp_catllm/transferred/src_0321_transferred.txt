The division of each article into smaller components was conducted to ensure that both the summary and each segment remained within a 512-token constraint. The comparison of the summary with each component resulted in the final SummaC score, obtained by averaging scores across all parts, thereby yielding a numerical value between 0 and 1. Heatmaps and a one-way ANOVA were employed to ascertain whether the position of the input influenced the summaries. It was discovered that summaries exhibit greater similarity to the initial article in the input sequence. Figure 3 illustrates average scores for three metrics across six input orders and three stance categories. The average for each condition is calculated by averaging scores across all article groups sharing the same input order and stance. Variations in shading and scores indicate that input position impacts similarity patterns in summaries generated by large language models (LLMs). Summaries demonstrate higher ROUGE-L and BERTScore values when compared to the first document in the sequence. Notably, BERTScore values surpass those for Position 2 and Position 3, implying that documents presented initially contribute more substantively to the summaries. Concerning RQ1, findings consistently reveal that summaries possess greater similarity to the first article in the input sequence. The metric values identified differ from those typically reported in summarization research due to the direct comparison of generated summaries with complete original articles rather than human-authored reference summaries as in preceding studies. The summaries, comprising approximately 220 words, contrast with source articles ranging from 300 to 1600 words, naturally reducing token overlap and similarity scores. Consequently, relative scores across varied input conditions are examined instead of absolute metric values. An additional challenge lies in the absence of metrics to evaluate similarity between a summary and its source documents. Predominantly, evaluation frameworks are reference-based, limiting methods for assessing source alignment. SummaC, focusing on sentence-level entailment, assigns low scores and exhibits minimal variation with positional changes. Abstract summaries tend to omit details from the source, and entailment-based scoring penalizes unsupported or implied assertions. Even when the model prioritizes the first article, the reuse of its exact wording is improbable. Thus, SummaC fails to reward subtle content biases unless explicit supporting sentences are retained.
