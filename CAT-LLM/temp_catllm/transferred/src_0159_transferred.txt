Energy consumption in software, including mobile applications and data center operations, is a topic of increasing concern. Accurately measuring and reducing energy usage in even relatively simple software applications is a complex task, further complicated by the dynamic optimization processes inherent in modern computing systems. Programming languages such as Java, Scala, and Kotlin, which execute on a virtual machine utilizing Java bytecode, exemplify this complexity. The energy consumption of such software is influenced by multiple interdependent factors, including the compiler, the Java Virtual Machine (JVM), the garbage collector, the operating system, and the underlying hardware. To comprehensively understand the implications of software design decisions on energy consumption, software engineers are required to possess an in-depth knowledge of these various layers as well as the specific platform on which the software operates. Previous research has investigated methodologies for energy profiling based on workload analysis, wherein software is executed under predetermined workloads in specialized environments capable of quantifying energy consumption and attributing it to specific code segments. However, the practical implementation of such methods necessitates the use of specialized hardware, presenting significant barriers to their routine adoption in software engineering practices. While certain hardware configurations provide energy consumption metrics for specific components, such as the CPU, very few facilitate holistic assessments of energy usage across the entire platform. A proposed model addresses these limitations by employing Gaussian distributions to characterize individual code segments based on four descriptive parameters, thereby enabling the grouping and analysis of factors influencing energy consumption, including operations, data types, data sizes, and hardware devices. This model elucidates the energy implications of modifying data types within algorithms and emphasizes worst-case energy consumption scenarios at the level of individual code statements. Predictions derived from these distributions facilitate energy consumption estimations for functions and entire programs. By focusing on worst-case scenarios, the model accommodates variability in energy measurements arising from factors such as temperature fluctuations and external interference. Furthermore, it preserves critical statistical properties, including averages, skewness, and modes, while enabling the computation of entropy and probability functions, which are unattainable through single-point estimates. The incorporation of worst-case energy consumption predictions is instrumental in ensuring that software systems meet predefined energy constraints, a consideration of particular importance for battery-operated devices and cost-efficient operations in data centers. Utilizing this framework, program analysis and software verification tools can integrate the proposed method to enhance energy consumption estimations, thereby advancing energy-aware software design.
