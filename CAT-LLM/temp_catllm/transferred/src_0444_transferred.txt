In contemporary artificial intelligence systems, particularly those involving large language models (LLMs) integrated to manage complex tasks collaboratively, the significance of LLMs in subsequent decision-making processes emerges as crucial. A bias toward initial processed content can potentially disrupt the functionality of the entire system. Therefore, addressing this bias is essential to ensure that these systems operate with fairness and transparency. The present study identifies two primary concerns: firstly, despite maintaining uniform article lengths, the extent to which evaluation methods depend on document length was not explicitly assessed. Given that length may impact metric outcomes, future investigations could categorize data based on length or implement controlled length tests to elucidate the importance of information sequencing. Secondly, the evaluation methods yielded relatively low scores overall, highlighting a broader issue with these evaluative techniques in summarization contexts, notably when juxtaposing long documents with concise summaries. Subsequent research should expand the focus beyond topics such as abortion to include areas like gun control, LGBTQ+ rights, and immigration to ascertain whether order bias is prevalent in other contentious subjects. Moreover, examining various LLMs, including GPT and Claude, could determine whether this bias manifests across different models. Incorporating additional measurement approaches, such as assessing opinion bias and summary polarization, may enhance comprehension regarding the impact of information order on summaries. It was determined that LLMs typically prioritize the first article processed in multi-document summarization, particularly concerning semantic interpretation. This observation corroborates prior research indicating that information order influences output. Such order sensitivity may pose challenges for systems like AI summaries or configurations where initial document exposure excessively influences subsequent outcomes. Consequently, mitigating this bias is imperative to develop systems characterized by enhanced fairness, clarity, and reliability.
