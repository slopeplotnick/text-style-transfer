The survey incorporated a mechanism to verify participant attention by including questions such as "Who made the music you just heard?" prior to requesting participants to rate the music. Subsequently, participants were inquired about the honesty and care with which they approached their responses on a 5-point scale, as well as whether they listened using headphones or speakers. It was explicitly communicated that their responses would not influence their feedback or compensation. The study design is illustrated in Figure 1. Participants were required to indicate their familiarity with and preference for the music on a scale from 1 (not at all) to 5 (very). Should participants select a 4 or 5 for familiarity, an additional query was posed regarding whether the music elicited memories of a specific person or experience. Those in the "AI or human" group were also tasked with determining whether they believed the music was generated by AI or a human, and to express their confidence on a 6-point scale ranging from 1 (definitely AI) to 6 (definitely human). Emotional reactions to the music were assessed using the Geneva Emotion Music Scale (GEMS), which encompasses nine emotions categorized into three primary groups: Sublimity (such as Wonder, Tenderness, and Nostalgia), Vitality (such as Joy and Power), and Unease (such as Sadness and Tension). A condensed version of GEMS was utilized, featuring one label for each emotion accompanied by a few related terms. Participants rated each emotion on a scale from 0 to 100, with 0 as the default if no selection was made. Additionally, a provision was made for participants to add up to three emotions not listed in GEMS and to rate the intensity of those emotions.
