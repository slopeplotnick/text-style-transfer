The process of generating recommendations within marketplaces characterized by extensive textual content and dynamic interactions presents inherent complexities due to the constant evolution of both the content and the connections. GraphMatch represents an innovative system that integrates pre-trained language models with graph neural networks to address these challenges effectively. Rather than relying on disparate models, GraphMatch employs a cohesive framework wherein text encoders and graph networks function synergistically. Specifically, the system utilizes techniques such as adversarial negative sampling and training on discrete graph snapshots to capture both the intricate semantic nuances of textual data and the evolving structural properties of the graph over time. Empirical evaluations were conducted using datasets derived from Upwork, a prominent job marketplace, with the system optimized to ensure operational efficiency suitable for real-time applications. The findings indicate that GraphMatch outperformed alternative approaches that exclusively relied on either language models or graph models in the context of matching tasks, while simultaneously demonstrating computational efficiency. These results substantiate the efficacy of integrating language and graph data for recommendation systems operating in environments typified by extensive textual information and fluctuating relational dynamics. Platforms such as e-commerce websites, job marketplaces, academic networks, and news feeds can be categorized as instances of Temporal Text-Attributed Graphs (TTAGs), within which nodes are characterized by textual attributes subject to temporal variation, and edges represent interactions such as transactions or communications. Effective matching or recommendation processes necessitate the simultaneous consideration of the granular semantic content of textual data and the overarching graph structure as it evolves over time, thereby presenting a significant challenge within the domain. Emerging language models, including SentenceBERT and E5, exhibit proficiency in converting documents into embeddings suitable for semantic comparison; however, these models fail to account for critical aspects inherent to TTAGs. Firstly, the association between two entities frequently depends on their contextual connections within the surrounding graph, an aspect overlooked by text-based models. Secondly, the semantic significance of nodes and the underlying rationale for link formation are subject to rapid changes within TTAGs. For instance, developments in generative artificial intelligence have shifted the focus of "AI Writing" job posts from editing to prompt engineering, while global events continue to influence the dynamics of freelancer-client matches within the international labor market. The treatment of textual attributes as static features introduces potential pitfalls, including the inadvertent disclosure of future information that should remain inaccessible, thereby highlighting the necessity for advanced methodologies capable of addressing these issues comprehensively.
