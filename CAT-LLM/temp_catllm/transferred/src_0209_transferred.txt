In all three models, the influence of the variable 'young' on image and prediction remains consistent, indicating its effectiveness as a measure irrespective of the level of detail considered. However, when an explanation model treats variables independently and fails to account for their interrelations, it only reveals the direct effect, which may coincide with the total effect in the initial model but result in a null effect in the final one. Consequently, direct influence is not invariably a dependable measure. Section 5.3.1 addresses issues arising from incorrect model assumptions, thereby recommending meticulous selection of models. Should assumptions induce bias in explanations, it is imperative to either modify the model or ensure that the explainer is cognizant of the biased context in which explanations operate. This insight does not imply that complex models are invariably superior to simpler ones. In order to ensure that explanations align with how they are intended to be comprehended, the causal model should correspond to the level of detail that individuals grasp about the world. Simplifying the understanding is crucial; therefore, omitting certain detailed causal mechanisms is preferable, thereby transforming vital information into a more comprehensible form. Moreover, a salient advantage of the causal explanation model discussed in Section 5.3.1 is its utilization of expert knowledge, which enhances the detection and elimination of spurious correlations compared to models solely reliant on data. Therefore, the explanation model is capable of identifying false relationships learned by the predictor, even when the same data is employed for the learning process of the explanation model. With expert knowledge rendered more accessible through large language models (LLMs), as demonstrated in the example, this approach proves particularly beneficial for refining models by identifying unintended behavior.
