It's really important to understand how groups are structured in networks where people move around, so we can see how different regions connect up. But the ways we usually do it have two big problems: First, the usual computer programs for finding groups have trouble seeing them at different sizes. And second, they don't combine looking at small groups and big groups at the same time in these movement networks. So, to fix these problems, we came up with a new way of doing things that mixes something called 'surpassing probability theory' with finding communities. This SPBL thing first changes how important the connections are to fix the issue where the usual Leiden method couldn't see groups at different sizes, so we can find the really big groups. Then, another part of our system, called TPSPCD, carefully splits these big groups into smaller, more detailed groups without losing the key links that hold them together. Our system also brings in something called ASD to figure out how strong the connections are inside a group compared to outside it. What we found showed clear central-and-outlying patterns in these movement networks, with groups sticking together strongly around important central points. So, this research says that our new way of finding groups really picks up on the local connections in these movement networks. This helps both the thinking and the practical side of finding groups in movement networks, so planners have practical tools for developing regions.
