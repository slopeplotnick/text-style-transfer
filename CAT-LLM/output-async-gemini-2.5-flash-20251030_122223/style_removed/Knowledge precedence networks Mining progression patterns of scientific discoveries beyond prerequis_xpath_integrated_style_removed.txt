It's really important to know how scientists learn new things over time to make education better and create new ideas. This study built something called Knowledge Precedence Networks, or KPNs. It did this to figure out how science usually moves forward in real life, across 19 different fields. They looked at what nearly 5 million scientists studied and 80 million papers from a database called OpenAlex. We came up with a method called CoCiTCD. It mixes Co-Citing networks with Temporal Community Detection to understand how knowledge grows. It does this by finding groups of researchers, picking out key ideas, and then linking these ideas over time. The KPNs for math, computer science, and engineering show that basic ideas are super important for understanding more complex stuff. Like, algorithms connect math and computer science, and this helps push forward things like Artificial Intelligence and Data Science. We checked how well the KPNs for 303 ideas matched up with what big AI models said were necessary background knowledge. This showed us how scientists actually use knowledge over time. The KPN only got about 25.77% right in the best situation. And another similar thing, called KCN, which uses citations, only got 26.6% right. This low match, all the time, means that how topics actually change in real life often doesn't follow the textbook order of what you should learn first. Also, a score of 0.76 on our test curve (called ROC) shows our KPN method is pretty solid at understanding how knowledge really grows in new and complex ways. These KPNs give us good ideas for planning research, making learning plans, working with people from different fields, and seeing how knowledge is built up in layers. So, they help the 'Science of Science' field by showing how knowledge really moves forward across different study areas.
