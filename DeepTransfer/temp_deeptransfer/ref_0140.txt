In this section, we introduce our novel evaluation framework, designed to integrate the domains of Hallucination Detection and Fact Verification—two areas historically examined in isolation. We begin by analyzing the factors contributing to this separation and critically evaluate the limitations of existing methodologies that hinder comprehensive assessment of both domains in a unified manner (§3.1). Subsequently, we elucidate the core principles underlying our proposed system (§3.2) and provide a detailed account of its operational mechanisms (§3.3). Before delving into the specifics, it is imperative to address the fundamental rationale for the historical separation of these two domains. The primary challenge lies in the divergent focal points of traditional Fact Verification datasets, which emphasize textual analysis, and Hallucination Detection methodologies, which concentrate on the interpretability and behavior of model architectures. Fact Verification assessments, exemplified by FEVER, employ predetermined pairs of claims and labels without accounting for the origin of the claims. This configuration proves inadequate for Hallucination Detection methodologies, which necessitate an examination of generative processes, such as the analysis of internal states or consistency across samples. The absence of these indicators in the static Fact Verification datasets poses a challenge for the concurrent evaluation of both paradigms. An alternative approach to address this issue involves conducting evaluations using pre-generated outputs from large language models, such as HELM. While this method holds utility, it encounters two significant challenges within the rapidly evolving domain of artificial intelligence: Model Obsolescence and Inability to Evaluate New Models. Firstly, the swift advancement of language models means that a dataset constructed with outputs from contemporary leading models, such as LLaMA-3, may soon become merely a historical artifact as newer models are developed. It is not feasible to evaluate novel models using fixed testing frameworks, as such approaches are unable to assess hallucination detection methodologies across diverse or emerging models. Given that these methodologies frequently rely on distinct model-specific characteristics, the inability to accommodate new models constrains advancements in the field and fails to demonstrate genuine adaptability.
