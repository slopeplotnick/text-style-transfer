In this section, we establish the framework used throughout this work. We define the datasets, solvers, and verifiers, introduce the metrics used to evaluate solver and verifier behaviors, and specify the verification settings in our empirical analysis. It is worth noting that verifier gain is an asymptotic metric; it reflects the limit of infinite sampling and therefore serves as a bound on the improvement attainable by verifier-based rejection sampling. Throughout this work, we use verifier gain to compare differences in verifier behavior. We group models into families, such as LLaMA3 and QWEN2.5, where each family contains related models of varying sizes. Because foundational and fine-tuned models often exhibit substantially different behaviors, we treat them as distinct subgroups within a family. For example, the foundational model meta-llama/Meta-Llama-3-70B belongs to the LLaMA3-Base subgroup, whereas the fine-tuned variant meta-llama/Meta-Llama-3-8B-Instruct belongs to the LLaMA3 subgroup. We categorize each solver-verifier pair from our pool of models into one of three verification settings. Each solver-verifier pair is considered an instance of: 1. Self-Verification, where the solver and verifier are the same model, so the model verifies its own solutions. For example, when a 70B LLaMA3 model is used as both the solver and verifier, the verification metric, such as accuracy, is computed on this single pairing. 2. Intra-Family Verification, where the verifier evaluates solutions produced by other models within the same family. For instance, a 70B LLaMA3 verifier may evaluate outputs from 8B or 13B LLaMA3 solvers. The reported metric is averaged over all such within-family solvers, excluding the self-verification case. 3. Cross-Family Verification, where the verifier evaluates solutions produced by models from different families. For example, a base LLaMA3 verifier may evaluate outputs from QWEN2.5 or from an enhanced LLaMA3. The reported metric is averaged over all such cross-family solvers.
