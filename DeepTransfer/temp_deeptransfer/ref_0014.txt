(1) Knowledge Injection and Reasoning Foundation: Domain-specific knowledge serves as the cognitive bedrock for understanding user inquiries and item specifications, necessitating its integration from the outset. With this foundational knowledge, the system can accurately discern user intent, extract salient item attributes, and align them with user requirements. (2) Multimodal Information Extraction: Visual representations of items often convey intricate details, such as aesthetic and tactile qualities, that are challenging to articulate through text alone. To address this, the system incorporates item image information alongside textual data, synthesizing insights derived from both modalities. By leveraging the complementarity of textual and visual features, the system constructs a more comprehensive and nuanced representation of item attributes. (3) Rule-Oriented Discrimination: Subsequent to query intent recognition and item attribute extraction, the process necessitates adherence to predefined rules for attribute verification and matching, ensuring consistency and accuracy in the final outputs. At this juncture, the model employs these rules to ensure logical coherence and operational efficacy. Step 1: Knowledge and Reasoning. In Section 2.3, we delineated knowledge into two categories: general world knowledge and e-commerce-specific knowledge. For the former, existing large language models have amassed substantial information through extensive pre-training, thereby facilitating their comprehension of common concepts and entity interrelations. However, within the e-commerce domain, there exists a plethora of phrases and terminologies that elude understanding when considered solely through their intrinsic semantic content. These challenges predominantly manifest in the following two dimensions: (1) Long-tail Queries: These queries pertain to highly specific and atypical items, for which existing models often lack the requisite prior knowledge reserves, thereby impeding the establishment of accurate semantic mapping relationships. For instance, when a query such as "strange things are also loved by someone" is presented alongside an item described as "novel and interesting toys," the query actually pertains to the title of a book. In the absence of this prior knowledge, the model may encounter difficulties in making accurate relevance judgments based solely on literal semantics. Similar instances encompass brand-specific jargon, product nicknames, marketing terminology, and other expressions unique to the e-commerce domain. (2) Semantically Ambiguous Items: Certain products exhibit such rarity that the system lacks sufficient information for their accurate comprehension. For instance, the designation "lying gate" cannot be discerned or classified merely from its nomenclature without an enhanced understanding of its inherent characteristics. Addressing these instances necessitates the integration of supplementary domain-specific e-commerce knowledge to facilitate precise interpretation.
