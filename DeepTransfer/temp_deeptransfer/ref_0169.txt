This paper explores why safety mechanisms designed for human-model interactions fail to scale in environments where large language models (LLMs) interact with one another. Current governance strategies predominantly focus on single-agent safety measures, such as prompts, fine-tuning, and moderation layers, which constrain individual model behavior but overlook the dynamics of multi-model interactions. These approaches operate under the assumption of a dyadic framework: one model responding to one user under stable oversight. However, the rapid development of LLM-to-LLM ecosystems, where outputs are recursively used as inputs across chains of agents, challenges this paradigm. In such systems, local compliance can aggregate into systemic failures, even when each model is individually aligned. To address this, we propose a shift from model-level safety to system-level safety, introducing the Emergent Systemic Risk Horizon (ESRH) as a framework to understand how instability arises from interaction architectures rather than isolated misbehavior. Specifically, this paper contributes (i) a theoretical framework for collective risk in interacting LLMs, (ii) a taxonomy linking micro, meso, and macro-level failure modes, and (iii) a design proposal for Institutional AI, an architecture embedding adaptive oversight within multi-agent systems. Existing safety mechanisms assume a single model operating under direct human supervision, where undesired behavior is mitigated through prompt engineering, reinforcement learning from human feedback, or output moderation. While these techniques have improved reliability and civility in isolated settings, they remain pointwise, governing how one model responds to one input rather than how multiple models interact. As LLMs are increasingly deployed in tool-using, agentic, and multi-agent configurations, the single-agent safety paradigm begins to falter. Models that are individually well-aligned can collectively produce outcomes that no single instance was trained to avoid, due to feedback amplification, imitation, or emergent coordination. This creates a gap between local alignment and systemic safety, where compliance at the component level fails to ensure control at the system level.
