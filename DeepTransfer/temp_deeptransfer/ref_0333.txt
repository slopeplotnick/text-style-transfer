In recent years, brain-computer interfaces (BCIs) have garnered significant attention as both invasive and non-invasive tools for enabling direct communication between the brain and external devices or digital systems. These interfaces, which can be categorized as invasive, requiring surgical intervention, or non-invasive, not necessitating such procedures, have captivated the scientific and medical communities, as well as mainstream media. BCIs are multidisciplinary systems designed to assist individuals with severe motor or speech impairments, such as those resulting from locked-in syndrome, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, spinal cord injuries, or stroke, in regaining the ability to communicate and, in some cases, to move. Consequently, it has become imperative to enhance the performance of non-invasive BCIs by examining a broader spectrum of EEG-derived features. Feature extraction techniques are highly diverse and play a critical role in EEG analysis, ranging from one-dimensional methods (such as time-domain and frequency-domain techniques) to more sophisticated multi-dimensional frameworks. Transforming raw EEG signals into meaningful representations, including time-domain statistics, frequency-domain features (e.g., power spectral density), and time-frequency methods (e.g., wavelet transforms), is crucial for accurate signal classification and robust performance. Moreover, the development of feature representations that generalize effectively across individuals is critical, given the substantial variability inherent in EEG signals across different users. The challenges posed by temporal data variations and inter-dataset discrepancies necessitate the implementation of transfer learning and domain generalization strategies to ensure the reliability of feature representations. A critical inquiry involves determining the optimal combinations of feature extraction and classification methodologies, particularly given the substantial variability inherent in individual EEG signals. Addressing this inquiry requires the application of evaluation techniques that mitigate the risk of overly optimistic assessments. This necessitates the implementation of participant-independent validation methodologies, such as leave-one-subject-out cross-validation, alongside the utilization of performance metrics adept at addressing class imbalances, including balanced accuracy and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC). Furthermore, it is imperative to employ statistical significance testing that does not rely heavily on parametric assumptions, such as nonparametric or permutation tests, while meticulously ensuring the prevention of data leakage. For instance, preprocessing steps such as filtering or normalization must be strictly confined to the training dataset and not applied to the test dataset.
