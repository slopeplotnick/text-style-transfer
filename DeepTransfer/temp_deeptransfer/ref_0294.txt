We propose a methodology to extract semantic information from images using advanced multimodal language models, aligning them with domain-specific ontologies to construct interpretable knowledge graphs. Furthermore, we introduce a graph-based analysis pipeline that evaluates the influence of generated images by comparing them with training images through graph structures, thereby enabling human-understandable assessments of similarity. This approach is particularly beneficial for tasks such as ownership attribution, enhancing interpretability, and ensuring transparency in data provenance, thereby demonstrating the potential of this framework to improve traceability in generative models. We evaluate our method on locally trained models and on large-scale models through a style-specific experiment. In the latter case, we focus on the recently popular Ghibli-style generations to assess our method's ability to detect stylistic influence, even in black-box settings. The paper is organized as follows: Section 2 reviews related work on interpretability in generative models and knowledge graph construction using advanced language models. Section 3 delineates our proposed methodology. Section 4 presents our experimental procedures and findings concerning both local and large-scale models. Section 5 concludes with a synthesis of the principal insights and outlines prospective research directions. As generative models continue to advance, understanding their implications has become increasingly crucial across technical, ethical, and policy domains. While broad methodologies such as t-SNE, LIME, and attention visualization provide insights into model behavior, they do not directly address the attribution of training data, which is a significant concern in domains such as authorship and copyright. To elucidate the influence of training data on model outputs, various attribution techniques have been proposed. Initial approaches encompass influence functions, retraining-based attribution, and Shapley value approximations. Despite their efficacy in established domains such as classification tasks, these methodologies are often impractical for large-scale models due to their requirements for gradient computation, retraining capabilities, or substantial computational resources. Consequently, they are seldom employed in contemporary deep learning frameworks, particularly for generative models where internal processes remain opaque.
