While model innovation remains active, the practical implementation of these models often hinges on considerations that are frequently underemphasized in academic discourse: how we define virality, what signals are feasible to collect (e.g., textual versus numeric or metadata), and which evaluation methodologies best account for real-world constraints such as restricted access, temporal limitations, and class imbalance. This study adopts an evaluation-first approach to inform these critical design decisions. We demonstrate across two contemporary datasets that the manner in which labels are constructed and the selection of data types—whether solely textual or augmented with additional signals—can be as pivotal as modifications to the model itself, releasing reproducible baselines that make these trade-offs explicit. Despite our emphasis on evaluation design rather than model innovation, we establish that more streamlined models can achieve performance on par with, or surpass, that of recent multimodal and graph-based systems on established benchmarks such as FAKENewsNET. Concretely, the present study focuses on two primary tasks: the detection of fake news and the prediction of early virality. Our objective is to ascertain which data types and definitional frameworks are most critical for maintaining robust performance under real-world conditions. Therefore, we undertake a comparative analysis of textual data, specifically sentence-level transformer embeddings, against lightweight numeric signals, such as user statistics, early engagement metrics, and temporal features. The tasks under consideration are evaluated using datasets characterized by distinct configurations: one comprising isolated news articles and the other consisting of sequential social media posts. We address two supervised classification tasks across these datasets, resulting in four distinct experimental scenarios: the detection of fake news and the prediction of virality on both the EVONS and FakeNewsNet datasets. In the context of fake-news detection, the objective is to ascertain the veracity of an item, determining whether it is false or true. This analysis is conducted on both Evons articles and the FakeNewsNet tweet series, with results reported utilizing standard classification metrics. For the FAKENewsNET dataset, we employ tweet series constructed from the repository. The dataset is anonymized using identifiers and requires API hydration; we obtained the Politifact split through prior academic collaboration; however, the GossipCop split could not be retrieved due to current limitations. Each instance comprises a sequence of tweets pertaining to an identical news article, illustrating the manner in which it disseminates.
