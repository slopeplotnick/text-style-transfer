Predictions can be modeled using machine learning methods, such as neural networks or generative AI algorithms. Offline and online sequential planning under uncertainty can be effectively addressed using Monte Carlo Tree Search algorithms. These two major components of artificial intelligence—prediction and decision-making—are fundamental to their application in exploration. However, making accurate predictions requires well-constructed datasets. In mineral exploration, the primary challenge lies not in selecting the appropriate machine learning method but in the creation and systematic curation of datasets. These datasets often present unique challenges, as they can be multivariate, three-dimensional, compositional, or characterized by extreme values—complexities rarely encountered in other domains. Two critical yet often overlooked aspects of data curation include the lack of uniformity in data scaling and the inappropriate pooling of disparate datasets into a single entity. Additionally, methodologies effective in one domain may not be directly applicable to another. For instance, ground truth data, such as core samples, is frequently upscaled to align with geophysical data, while deterministic interpolation of geophysical and geochemical datasets is sometimes conducted without geological justification. Such practices, including the unvalidated application of machine learning models across domains, often result in suboptimal outcomes, such as an increased incidence of false positives. Consequently, these errors undermine the perceived reliability of AI systems, even though the root cause lies in data mismanagement. A well-documented issue, particularly in mining geostatistics, is the variability in data characteristics across scales, which requires careful consideration to avoid errors stemming from discrepancies in variance. For example, interpolated geophysical datasets inherently exhibit reduced variance compared to the original measurements due to the interpolation process. Similarly, drilling data collected at fine spatial resolutions is often upscaled or spatially adjusted to enable comparison with geophysical datasets. These manipulations introduce artificial correlations between labels and predictors, arising directly from the methodological handling of the data. As a result, current mineral potential or prospecting maps frequently contain a significant number of false positives.
