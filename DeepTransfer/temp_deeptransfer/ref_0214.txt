The phenomenon whereby adversarial training facilitates the transferability of attacks is not attributable to specific architectural designs of models but rather to the manner in which adversarial training alters the information processing mechanisms of these models. This effect is observed across diverse model architectures, underscoring its significance for comprehensive security considerations. To elucidate the reasons behind the enhanced transferability induced by adversarial training, researchers have employed the integrated gradients method to examine how models articulate their decision-making processes. In a comparative analysis of two DenseNet121 model variants—one subjected to adversarial training (AT) and the other trained under standard conditions (ST)—notable differences in feature attribution were observed. Specifically, the AT model predominantly highlighted semantically meaningful regions of the image, such as the head, body, and legs of an ostrich, whereas the ST model exhibited a tendency to focus on fine-grained textural details, including feather patterns and background elements. This distinction underscores the capacity of AT models to prioritize object-level features that encapsulate high-level visual concepts. In contrast, ST models rely on localized, non-generalizable features that exhibit limited transferability across different models. Consequently, adversarial examples generated by adversarially trained (AT) models exhibit enhanced cross-model transferability due to their targeting of features commonly shared among various architectures. For instance, DenseNet121 and DenseNet201 demonstrate analogous image processing methodologies, while MobileNetV3 shares similarities by incorporating design principles from EfficientNet.
