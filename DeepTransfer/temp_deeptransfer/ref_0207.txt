In the rapidly evolving field of Natural Language Processing (NLP), large language models (LLMs) like GPT and BERT have become pivotal drivers of innovation. These models excel at generating text that mimics human language, answering complex questions, and engaging in fluent conversations. When a method produces an output that seems logically coherent at first glance, it can be likened to following true north on a compass. However, while the responses generated by LLMs may align with the input and adhere to logical reasoning, they often lack truthfulness, as they are not grounded in factual information. This phenomenon, known as hallucination, represents a major barrier to their broader adoption. To illustrate, it is akin to an experienced storyteller who, despite aiming to convey the truth, occasionally embellishes their narrative with fictional details. Similarly, LLMs may produce content that appears accurate but is disconnected from the training data they rely on. A simpler analogy would be a GPS confidently providing directions, only to lead users to nonexistent destinations. From a technical perspective, hallucinations occur when the generated content deviates from factual information or expected outcomes based on the models' training data. This can manifest as false statements, fabricated references, or flawed reasoning. Much like the misguided GPS, these models may seem reliable but ultimately falter. Addressing this issue has become a focal point for the AI community. Even the most advanced models are prone to hallucinations. While LLMs demonstrate extraordinary capabilities, they remain inconsistent in producing factual information—a critical shortcoming in high-stakes domains such as healthcare, legal practice, and academic research, where accuracy is paramount.
