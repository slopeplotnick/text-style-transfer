However, further analysis revealed that individual differences in attitudes toward AI played a more substantial role in shaping the outcomes than the experimental manipulation itself. When attitudes toward AI were included as covariates in linear mixed effect models, the experimental condition no longer significantly predicted participants' liking of the music or the intensity of their emotional responses. In contrast, attitudes toward AI remained a consistent and significant predictor throughout the analysis. This finding aligns with prior research demonstrating that attitudes toward autonomous machines strongly influence their evaluation (Hong et al., 2022) and extends the work of Shank et al. (2023) by suggesting that biases toward composers may depend on the song and its context, such as its popularity and perceived human origin. Although several personality traits and music-related factors were initially associated with liking and emotional responses, their effects were largely attenuated when attitudes toward AI were controlled for. Notable exceptions to this trend were openness to experience and neuroticism, two personality traits previously identified as significant predictors of emotional responses to music, particularly in relation to emotions such as joy and vitality (Gerstgrasser et al., 2023; Juslin et al., 2011; Liljeström et al., 2013), which were especially salient in the musical stimuli used in the present study. Most notably, attitudes toward AI emerged as the only listener characteristic significantly associated with both liking and emotional responses to AI-generated music. The proportion of variance explained by listener characteristics was comparable to that reported in previous studies (Gerstgrasser et al., 2023; Strauss et al., 2024). These findings underscore the pivotal role of attitudes toward AI in shaping evaluations of AI-generated music across diverse audiences. However, in contrast to Ansani et al. (2024), we did not observe a moderation effect of attitudes toward AI on the functioning of the experimental groups. This discrepancy may stem from differences in the attitude scales used, our focus on listening to tracks rather than live performances, and the inclusion of three experimental conditions instead of two.
