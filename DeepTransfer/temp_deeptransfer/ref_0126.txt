Position Bias. Despite the advanced capabilities of language models in natural language processing, recent studies have highlighted their vulnerability to the structural arrangement of input data, a phenomenon known as position bias. This bias refers to the tendency of models to disproportionately prioritize information presented at specific positions within the input sequence, often neglecting important details elsewhere. In multi-source summarization tasks, where models process multiple long documents concatenated into a single context, this bias implies that the likelihood of information being included in the summary can depend on a document's placement within the context window. Consequently, summaries generated by these models may exhibit skewed perspectives or imbalanced content coverage depending on the input order. This issue is exemplified by the Lost in the Middle phenomenon. Empirical evidence suggests that models exhibit optimal performance when salient information is positioned at the beginning or end of the input, while their efficacy diminishes when such information is situated in the middle. This pattern is evident in tasks such as multi-document question answering and key-value retrieval, where a characteristic U-shaped performance curve is observed: accuracy peaks when relevant information appears at the beginning (primacy) or end (recency) of the context but degrades when the information is placed in the middle. Moreover, when models are employed to assess or evaluate information, there is a propensity to prioritize the initial or final options presented, even if the optimal choice resides elsewhere within the data. This phenomenon is frequently referred to as lead bias, wherein excessive emphasis is placed on the commencement of the input sequence. While some models, such as GPT-3.5-Turbo, exhibit relatively mild positional bias, others demonstrate pronounced sensitivity to content presented in initial positions, particularly in contexts involving extreme summarization tasks. Beyond degrading performance, such positional sensitivity raises significant fairness concerns. For instance, when tweets are categorized by dialect and arranged in a specific sequence, the resultant summaries may disproportionately favor the dialect group that is positioned first. This demonstrates that the sequential arrangement of input data can exert a profound influence.
