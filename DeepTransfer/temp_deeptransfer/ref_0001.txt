Some models may be more reliable than others. This review addresses the influence of model size, architecture, and training data on the occurrence of hallucinations. Prior research highlights these tendencies across different models and offers solutions, such as improving training techniques and imposing limitations on the generated output to tackle the issue. Similarly, another study notes that hallucinations remain a fundamental problem for large language models (LLMs), regardless of their dimensions or training methodology. It proposes that meticulous model selection and training can help restrain hallucinations; however, the authors do not believe that it is possible to develop models entirely free from such phenomena. This insight is of great significance as it aids in formulating realistic goals and evaluating the appropriateness of the chosen model for a given task. When selecting an LLM to reduce hallucinations, it is essential to consider factors such as the model's architecture, the quality and diversity of its training data, and the implementation of strategies like fine-tuning or retrieval augmentation. Many models are available in the market today, including GPT, BERT, and T5. Among these, GPT has been observed to produce text that is logically and cohesively consistent with the content; however, such generative models are prone to generating fabricated data, commonly referred to as hallucinations. BERT tends to exhibit lower rates of hallucinations due to its use of bidirectional training for specific tasks; however, it remains susceptible to creating new material. Other models, such as PaLM and LLaMa, have been developed to reduce hallucinations, yet their success varies based on their training methods and designs. Understanding the advantages and limitations of these models is crucial when selecting a tool for applications where the accuracy of information is paramount and minimizing hallucination rates is imperative.
