Forecasting stock prices represents a fundamental and enduring challenge in the field of finance. The inherent volatility of stock price movements, combined with their acute sensitivity to real-time information, often renders traditional predictive methodologies inadequate for processing unstructured textual signals such as breaking news and market sentiment. Consequently, these conventional approaches frequently fail to capture the key drivers of price fluctuations. The recent explosion of online textual data, coupled with transformative advancements in the natural language processing capabilities of Large Language Models (LLMs), has significantly accelerated research into text-based stock price forecasting. However, most existing studies leveraging LLMs remain limited to shallow text analysis and immediate inference. They often lack systematic knowledge integration and a robust long-term memory framework, which severely constrains their ability to adapt to the highly dynamic and complex nature of financial markets. This highlights the critical need for external memory mechanisms that enable models not only to comprehend immediate textual inputs but also to retain, associate, and retrieve salient information over extended periods. Furthermore, the unique characteristics of financial markets exacerbate the inadequacy of general-purpose memory architectures. Stock price movements are rarely driven by isolated events; rather, they emerge from the sequential interplay of multiple interrelated developments over time. As a result, the task of predicting stock price movements based on news diverges fundamentally from traditional question-answering paradigms. The prediction target, such as "tomorrow's stock price movement," is not explicitly stated in news texts and cannot be directly extracted. Instead, predictive models must construct dynamic market contexts by synthesizing information from diverse sources, identifying analogous historical patterns, and engaging in deep reasoning to transcend basic text matching and achieve meaningful market pattern recognition. Moreover, financial texts are inherently noisy, containing substantial amounts of redundant and irrelevant information that can obscure critical predictive signals. Therefore, the ability to effectively filter and align relevant content emerges as a crucial prerequisite for accurate market forecasting.
