Our investigation spans three pertinent domains: methodologies incorporating external feedback, strategies to reduce dependency on human annotations, and theoretical frameworks addressing the impact of representational paradigms on the quality of generated outputs. Typically, aligning large language models with human preferences necessitates the implementation of Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF). This approach employs human preference data to train a model for reward assignment, subsequently refining the policy to optimize outcomes while maintaining proximity to a reference model. Models such as ChatGPT and Llama rely on this methodology; however, it is not without challenges. The acquisition of preference data is often costly and may lack accuracy, and reward models are susceptible to manipulation or may exhibit suboptimal performance across varying contexts. In response to these limitations, methodologies such as Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), KTO, Bayesian RLHF, and MaxMin-RLHF seek to enhance the policy directly through pairwise comparisons, obviating the need for an explicit value network, yet they still necessitate meticulously curated preference datasets. Moreover, certain models provide evaluative scores and feedback pertaining to reasoning and assessment, maintaining the supervisory mechanisms external to the model's core processes. Alternatively, employing automatic supervision or intrinsic signals from the model itself serves to diminish the dependency on human-generated labels. Methods such as DeepSeek-R1 and TÜLU 3 leverage programmatic feedback mechanisms as substitutes for human-generated input.
