We will begin by discussing the foundational work in Bayesian and n-gram-based language models, which were instrumental in constructing probabilistic frameworks for speech recognition tasks. By modeling word sequences and addressing uncertainties inherent in spoken language, these models established the groundwork for contemporary systems. Building on this, we will explore the rise of modern end-to-end speech models that integrate contextual information directly into the modeling process. These models not only capture linguistic content but also incorporate paralinguistic features such as prosody, emotion, and speaker characteristics, enabling more robust and nuanced speech understanding and generation. Furthermore, we will examine the role of large language models (LLMs) in refining and correcting the outputs of traditional automatic speech recognition (ASR) systems during the post-processing phase. Particular attention will be given to issues of fairness and bias in speech processing, especially concerning variability introduced by accents, dialects, and sociolects. We will discuss how recent advancements are addressing these challenges and consider the ethical implications of ensuring equitable performance across diverse speech populations. Additionally, we will review theoretical frameworks for adapting LLMs to speech input, including topics such as population risk measurement and model transferability estimation, which motivate distinct design pipelines for spoken agents. Building on these foundations, we will delve into joint text-speech pre-training methodologies that integrate learning objectives for both modalities, thereby enhancing multimodal comprehension. Finally, we will explore the application of LLMs for voice quality assessment, highlighting their ability to dynamically adapt to diverse speaker characteristics in real-time.
