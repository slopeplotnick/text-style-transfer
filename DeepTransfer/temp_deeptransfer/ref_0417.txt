Engineering practice has increasingly adopted hierarchical systems wherein supervisory agents function as intermediaries between human operators and worker agents. These overseers evaluate outputs, identify anomalies, and possess the authority to halt operations when predefined thresholds are exceeded. This framework aligns with the principles of the "Institutional AI" paradigm, which will be elaborated upon in subsequent sections. However, the mere presence of supervisory mechanisms is insufficient to ensure robust oversight. Supervisory systems operate on the same underlying architecture as the agents they monitor, rendering them susceptible to analogous vulnerabilities and recursive feedback dynamics. Moreover, excessive alignment among supervisory agents can give rise to collective errors or, in extreme cases, coordinated malfeasance within the oversight framework. Therefore, to enhance management efficacy, it is imperative to implement additional supervisory layers, diverse verification mechanisms, auxiliary observation techniques, and a distinct delineation of authority among supervisors. As systems evolve to operate autonomously with multiple agents, the complexity of ensuring safety is significantly heightened. Control can no longer rely on a singular centralized system or static rules; instead, it necessitates the development of networks capable of autonomously identifying, addressing, and rectifying issues. Models in these ecosystems no longer fail alone; rather, they fail together. Thus, ensuring the safety of AI systems necessitates an understanding of the origins of these collective vulnerabilities and the development of robust methodologies for their measurement. The emergence of interactions among language models underscores the inadequacy of existing risk frameworks—such as bias, misuse, and robustness—in addressing the complex dynamics of distributed AI ecosystems. While it is possible to evaluate and constrain the behavior of an individual model in isolation, once models interact, the potential for failure proliferates in ways that remain insufficiently understood. To address this complexity, we propose the concept of the Emergent Systemic Risk Horizon (ESRH), an analytical framework delineating the threshold at which the collective dynamics of interacting models become unstable, even when each individual model operates within acceptable parameters.
