Large language models (LLMs) demonstrate exceptional proficiency across a wide range of tasks. Their performance on specific tasks can be enhanced through instruction-based adaptation, where task-specific instructions and examples are provided as input. Since these instructions, often referred to as prompts, guide the output of LLMs, their effectiveness is heavily influenced by the quality, formulation, and presentation of the instructions. This sensitivity to prompts highlights the potential of optimizing them for specific tasks, much like hyperparameter tuning improves performance in classical machine learning. However, manually crafting effective prompts is both challenging and unreliable. To address this, automatic prompt optimization has emerged as a systematic approach to identify optimal instructions for LLMs, akin to how AutoML automates hyperparameter search. This development is particularly relevant in today’s landscape, where multi-agent systems play a pivotal role in both industrial applications and academic research. Given the high costs and extensive data requirements of retraining or fine-tuning entire models, automatic prompt optimization offers a more efficient and accessible alternative, especially for black-box LLMs with limited interpretability or restricted access to internal parameters. Despite the growing number of prompt optimizers introduced in the research domain, their practical application presents several challenges. Each optimizer is housed in its own research repository, requiring users to manage multiple codebases and resolve conflicting dependencies when comparing different tools. Furthermore, these repositories are often not actively maintained, with research code that lacks proper testing, documentation, and robustness. Additionally, many of these tools are inflexible, making their application to complex tasks or diverse LLM deployments cumbersome. While some libraries and tools aim to address these limitations, they often come with their own drawbacks, such as being proprietary and closed-source, supporting only a single optimization algorithm, or being overly complex and difficult to integrate into routine workflows.
