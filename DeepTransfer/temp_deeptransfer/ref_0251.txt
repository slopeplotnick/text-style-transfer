When conceptualizing a narrative, individuals can start with an event as the starting point, shaping the traits and development of the entities associated with the event. Alternatively, they may choose an entity as the centerpiece and construct a series of events surrounding it. E2, a PhD student in literature, posits that narratives can be deconstructed into units such as story outlines, sequences, propositions, and parts of speech. By progressively combining these structural units in reverse order, one can ultimately create a coherent story. The parts of speech, such as a person, an action, or an attribute, can form a proposition. Several propositions can be organized into a sequence, and two or more sequences can ultimately constitute a complete narrative. However, mere concatenation of these units does not yield coherent stories. Therefore, the elements that bind these units together function as distinct structural components, defined as relationships. Interviews conducted with two experts revealed four foundational constructs: entities, events, relationships, and frameworks. In discussions with experts, we examined the attributes of each foundational unit, noting that each encompasses multiple interpretations. Events are central components of a narrative, yet the specific attributes of these events may be implicitly present in the narrative framework. While events possess a degree of autonomy, their interactional patterns are instrumental in constructing the narrative's tension and overarching meaning. Moreover, the identity of entities and the nature of their relationships within the narrative can significantly influence the unfolding of events. However, the specific attributes of an entity's identity and the content of a relationship can also be independently categorized within their respective units.
