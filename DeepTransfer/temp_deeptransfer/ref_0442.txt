Large Language Models (LLMs) frequently exhibit hallucinations, generating content that appears fluent and coherent but is factually incorrect. These inaccuracies undermine trust and hinder their adoption in real-world applications. To address this challenge, two distinct research paradigms have emerged: model-centric Hallucination Detection (HD) and text-centric Fact Verification (FV). Although both share the common goal of identifying factual errors, they have evolved independently, relying on divergent assumptions, datasets, and evaluation protocols. This separation has created a significant research gap, impeding collective progress. In this work, we take a critical step toward bridging this divide by introducing UniFact, a unified evaluation framework that enables direct, instance-level comparison between HD and FV through the dynamic generation of model outputs and corresponding factuality labels. Extensive experiments across multiple LLM families and detection methods reveal three key findings: (1) neither paradigm consistently outperforms the other; (2) HD and FV capture complementary aspects of factual inaccuracies; and (3) hybrid approaches that integrate both methods achieve state-of-the-art performance. Beyond benchmarking, we provide the first comprehensive analysis of the underlying divergences between HD and FV, offering empirical evidence that underscores the necessity of their unification. These findings highlight the need for a novel, integrated research agenda aimed at harmonizing Hallucination Detection and Fact Verification in LLMs. LLMs have fundamentally transformed the digital landscape, enabling next-generation search engines and automating complex tasks once deemed infeasible. However, their deployment in high-stakes domains such as healthcare, law, and journalism remains critically constrained by a persistent challenge: the generation of fluent yet factually inaccurate content, commonly referred to as hallucination. In these domains, where factual accuracy is paramount, such errors can erode user trust, propagate misinformation, and lead to harmful consequences. Therefore, developing robust methodologies to detect and mitigate these inaccuracies is not merely a technical challenge but a foundational requirement for building reliable and trustworthy intelligent systems.
