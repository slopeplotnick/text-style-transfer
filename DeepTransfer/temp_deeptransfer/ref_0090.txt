We evaluate a diverse set of recent language models, spanning both closed- and open-weight releases (see Appendix A.4 for details). Where possible, we disable extended-thinking or reasoning controls to probe the models' natural, emergent behavior. This was feasible for all models except GPT-5 Mini, which only allows adjusting reasoning depth; therefore, we set this to the lowest level. Additionally, we conducted a human baseline study for comparison across all tasks, incorporating a limited number of ablations (see Appendix A.5 for details). Human results are presented in the left panel of Figure 8. This experiment serves solely as a reference point, as our main focus is on comparing language models against each other. Extensive studies on human psychophysics, including the magnitude estimation effects examined here, are well-documented in the literature, such as in Jazayeri & Shadlen (2010) and Petzschner & Glasauer (2011). Notably, most models perform better in the text modality than in the image modality, except on the maze distance estimation task. The text input for this task is a long, detailed path description, significantly more complex than the simplistic text prompts used in other tasks (refer to Fig. 2). Consequently, most models perform worse in the text modality for this task; however, GPT-5 Mini is an exception, achieving near-perfect text performance due to residual reasoning capabilities that can be attenuated but not fully disabled, illustrating the task-dependent nature of model behavior. Across tasks, the factor evidence for Bayesian behavior is consistently higher in the image modality than in text (Appendix A.11). Transitioning from unimodal to multimodal inputs does not uniformly improve performance. Nonetheless, some models effectively leverage information from the additional modality: Llama-4 Maverick attains its best performance under multimodal conditions across all tasks, while Claude 3.7 Sonnet and GPT-4o improve on two of the three tasks.
