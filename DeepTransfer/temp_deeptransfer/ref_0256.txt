In expressions like "pearl white" or "ivory white," the core need is for a specific shade of white, not the literal attributes of "pearl" or "ivory." Similarly, in the phrase "phone gas station," the underlying need is a power bank, with "gas station" serving as a metaphor. In these cases, a higher-level semantic understanding is necessary, as pure semantic parsing is insufficient. Moreover, domain-specific knowledge scenarios arise where niche attributes cannot be inferred from surface forms alone and must rely on structured domain knowledge. Typical examples include domain-specific jargon in vertical e-commerce, shop or brand taxonomies, model coding rules, and category-specific descriptions of functions or effects, such as mouthfeel descriptions for tea or wine. Additionally, challenges like misspellings and aliases occur when users express needs using typos, homophones, aliases, or industry slang. This situation requires a stronger integration of general and domain knowledge, with the model needing robust capabilities in spelling correction, variant normalization, and alias alignment. Furthermore, intent recognition, or entity-relation recognition, depends on reasoning to determine whether a query asks for items themselves or item-related information, such as reviews, comparisons, or usage guides. It is crucial to distinguish which entities are the main targets versus which are modifiers or constraints. For example, in "charger/headphones for iPhone 17," the actual target items are the charger or headphones, while "iPhone 17" imposes compatibility or model constraints. Handling abstract needs that point to categories or domains, such as "phones with the best battery life," requires the system to capture the semantic meaning of "battery life" in the phone domain, construct reasonable ranking or filtering rules, and support inference and comparison over candidate sets. Finally, mitigating ambiguity caused by missing context is essential, as users often do not specify all constraints or preferences in a single query. Therefore, the system must exploit dialogue history, user profiles, and default preferences to address these omissions.
