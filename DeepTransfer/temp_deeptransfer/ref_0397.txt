From an evolutionary perspective, research has explored the emergence of communication through multi-agent systems, with particular attention to the potential evolution of language. A notable example is the Talking Heads framework, developed by Steels (2000, 2015), which models language evolution through interactions between agents that initially refer to elements in their environment without linguistic means. These agents develop an artificial language through interactive processes, such as naming games aimed at assigning labels to objects in their surroundings (Steels 2001; cf. Steels and Kaplan 2001, Steels and Loetzch 2012 for studies on word naming). More recently, this research has been extended to investigate the emergence and acquisition of syntactic structures, including sentence formation and grammatical rules (cf. Steels and Beuls 2017, Steels et al. 2018, van Trijp 2016). A key distinction between the Talking Heads framework and the MODOMA project lies in the active participation of both agents in the creation and acquisition of language in the former, whereas the latter focuses on a single agent acquiring a fragment of a natural language. Furthermore, the Talking Heads framework involves the acquisition of an artificial language, unlike the MODOMA project. Alishahi has conducted research on language acquisition by applying algorithms to corpora that are either human-constructed or artificially generated. For studies modeling the acquisition of lexical categories, semantic representations, and early syntactic structures, see Alishahi and Chrupala (2009, 2012) and Alishahi and Stevenson (2008). Similarly, Matusevych et al. (2013) have refined artificially generated inputs resembling child-adult interactions, akin to data from the CHILDES database (MacWhinney 2014). However, unlike the MODOMA framework, their language acquisition algorithm does not engage in conversational exchanges. Beekhuizen et al. (2014) offer a different perspective by modeling the acquisition of grammatical constructions using a single-agent system that processes generated representations of utterances and their meanings. Finally, Conner et al. (2008, 2009) advanced the Baby SRL project, which investigates the acquisition of semantic role labeling by applying algorithms to annotated data from the CHILDES corpus or synthetically constructed sentences.
