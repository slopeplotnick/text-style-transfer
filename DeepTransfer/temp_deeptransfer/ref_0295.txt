In the process of editing a video to align with specific requirements, it is imperative to maintain the original motion dynamics. Therefore, every subtle movement, such as blinks or head turns, must occur concurrently with the corresponding actions in the source video. This temporal synchronization is crucial, especially in scenarios involving talking-head videos, as even minor discrepancies can compromise the fidelity of the edited output relative to the source performance. Typically, the desired modifications are minor, such as the addition of a mustache or the alteration of a facial expression. The objective is to effectuate these changes while keeping the remainder of the content unchanged. However, achieving this level of precision and synchronization remains a challenge for the majority of contemporary video editing tools. To address this, we propose a novel framework, Sync-LoRA, designed to generate temporally synchronized edited video sequences. The method involves modifying only the initial frame of the source video, with the desired transformation specified via a text prompt. Moreover, to ensure robust synchronization, the model is trained using paired video sequences, wherein one represents the original content and the other its edited counterpart. These pairs are meticulously curated to maintain frame-level temporal alignment, ensuring that corresponding frames exhibit identical motion trajectories while differing solely in the visual attributes modified by the edit. The process of generating these pairs involves initial creation followed by rigorous synchronization verification. We employ a model capable of interpreting both visual and textual inputs to conceptualize various transformation ideas, subsequently generating corresponding images which are then synthesized into video sequences. However, discrepancies often arise between these videos, necessitating verification through human motion data encompassing facial expressions and body movements. We assess their alignment in terms of speech, ocular motion, blinking, and bodily posture, retaining only the most congruent examples for training purposes. By leveraging these well-aligned instances, our model acquires the capability to preserve the original video's motion dynamics while implementing the desired modifications, thus maintaining synchronization with the source video.
