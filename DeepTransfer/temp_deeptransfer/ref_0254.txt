In our analysis, we examine a category of attacks where a backdoor is probabilistically triggered during user interactions, making the vulnerability present from the outset. However, a more sophisticated variant of this attack involves the tool initially exhibiting benign behavior, with adversaries deliberately refraining from executing malicious actions during the initial deployment phase. Once the tool achieves widespread utilization, the attacker can remotely initiate the hijacking. This strategy of deferred activation, akin to supply chain attacks, significantly enhances the threat's severity and complicates attribution efforts. Moreover, the attack's stealth can be augmented through selective targeting of specific entities. For instance, adversaries may scrutinize user identifiers and selectively target individuals who frequently utilize the tool, particularly those who successfully navigate security evaluations. This strategy renders the attack more elusive during the testing phase. In this paper, we delineate a category of attacks termed implicit toxicity, which exploits authorized tool functionalities to surreptitiously misuse granted permissions, thereby inflicting harm. To demonstrate this, we developed a method termed LeechHijack, which facilitates the clandestine appropriation of computational resources through legitimate tool usage. LeechHijack can be deployed by any MCP tool provider and infiltrates the system without necessitating elevated privileges, reallocating system resources for unintended operations. This analysis highlights a significant vulnerability in the dissemination of MCP tools, where malicious tools can compromise system integrity without requiring additional access privileges. Extensive testing across various systems has demonstrated the efficacy of LeechHijack, which remains impervious to existing defense mechanisms. Furthermore, we have explored strategies to mitigate this risk and established a foundation for the development of more robust defenses in the future. Our findings reveal a critical security vulnerability inherent in systems leveraging large language models (LLMs), underscoring the necessity of rigorous scrutiny when integrating third-party tools. All experimental procedures adhered strictly to established ethical and security protocols. The Leech-Hijack attack presented herein serves solely as a controlled proof of concept to illuminate this systemic issue, rather than to advocate or facilitate malicious activities. All experiments were conducted within secure, controlled environments that were independently established, ensuring no interaction with actual systems, platforms, or services. Network communication with external servers was restricted to simulated settings to prevent the exfiltration of authentic data.
