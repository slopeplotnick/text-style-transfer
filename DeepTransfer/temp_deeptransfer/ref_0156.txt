Transitioning from deterministic to stochastic inversion necessitates the incorporation of a falsification step to eliminate erroneous modeling assumptions, as these may induce false positives. Geological realism is integrated from the outset, avoiding the subsequent addition typical of deterministic inversion; thus, the focus remains on geology without resorting to vague terminology. Moreover, drilling strategies are proposed through collaboration with experts, employing methodologies that account for uncertainty quantification. These strategies are devised with specific objectives, such as maximizing the targeted area or volume, and quantitative analyses are employed to determine the most effective drilling methodologies. Importantly, these modifications are not arbitrary; rather, they are informed by a deliberate and systematic approach to data, models, objectives, and decision-making processes, rooted in a longstanding tradition of scientific inquiry. In the subsequent section, we will further elucidate this framework through the lens of the philosophy of science, with particular emphasis on two principal paradigms: Falsification and Bayesianism. The pursuit of scientific inquiry necessitates not only the execution of scientific practices but also a reflective consideration of the methodologies employed therein, prompting an examination of the principles that constitute a robust scientific method. The evolution of scientific inquiry has transitioned from mere observation to the examination of the interplay between theoretical models and empirical data. Furthermore, the collaborative dynamics within the scientific community significantly influence the establishment of norms and methodologies, which, despite their acceptance, may later be revealed as erroneous. However, the insular nature of the scientific community can obscure the recognition of these inaccuracies, as prevailing practices are often internalized as standard. Scientists may exhibit excessive confidence in the validity of their knowledge claims (Bond et al., 2007). Thomas Kuhn examined such collective frameworks within scientific communities, referring to them as "paradigms." According to Kuhn, paradigms encompass a set of shared beliefs, methodologies, rules, and applications that are collectively endorsed by members of a scientific discipline, exemplified by foundational concepts such as evolutionary theory or plate tectonics.
