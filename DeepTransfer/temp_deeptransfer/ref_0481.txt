To better interpret the trends suggested by the accuracy and false positive rate (FPR) results, we examine the verifier gain presented in the final row. Verifier gain quantifies the expected benefit of using the verifier during rejection sampling, i.e., repeatedly sampling until the verifier accepts a solution—a common solver-verification interaction setting (Song et al., 2025). This analysis provides a clearer perspective on verification quality: self-verification yields the smallest gains, and more accurate solvers do not exhibit greater self-improvement. Moreover, gains increase modestly in intra-family verification, while cross-family verification delivers the most substantial benefits. To evaluate verifier performance across different model families and sizes, we replicate the experiments from Figure 2 and plot each verifier metric as a function of model size within each model family in Figure 3. We observe that verification accuracy and the false negative rate (FNR) consistently improve as model size increases, whereas the FPR behaves less predictably, often rising with model size—for instance, in intra-family verification for DeepSeek. For state-of-the-art post-trained models such as Qwen3 and DeepSeek, verifier gains are most pronounced in the cross-family setting, less significant in the intra-family setting, and minimal during self-verification. At first glance, this seems to contradict Song et al. (2025), who report that self-verification GV-Gaps increase with additional pretraining FLOPs. However, their analysis primarily focuses on older model families, and Figure 2 similarly shows larger verifier gains for older models such as Qwen2.5 and Llama3. We hypothesize that stronger post-trained models like DeepSeek and Qwen3 exhibit negligible gains in self-verification and limited gains in intra-family verification for two reasons: (a) they may already perform spontaneous self-verification when used as solvers, thereby reducing the benefit of an additional forced verification round, and (b) their distributions are significantly sharpened through post-training (Huang et al., 2024a), which constrains the improvement achievable via rejection sampling.
