Models perform differently across topics. As shown in Table 2, their performance tends to be slightly lower in Religion and Sport, with scores typically ranging from 7.8 to 8.2, whereas performance in Game and Lifestyle is noticeably stronger, often exceeding 8.4. This pattern suggests that models adopt a more cautious and reserved approach when addressing culturally or ethically sensitive subjects like religion and sports. In contrast, topics such as gaming and lifestyle, being more neutral and better represented in training data, enable models to produce smoother and more natural mediation. Moreover, steering and judgment performances are strongly correlated. Fig. 3 reveals a clear positive relationship between average steering and judgment scores across all models, showing that those excelling in judgment also tend to perform well in steering. This implies that both tasks rely on shared underlying reasoning and alignment abilities. Therefore, improvements in core mediation skills, such as contextual understanding and balanced decision-making, could generalize across multiple evaluation tasks. Future research could explore strategies to further unify these capabilities, enhancing both interpretability and control in large language models. Additionally, the results in Table 3 demonstrate that mediation has varying effectiveness across different linguistic aspects. For instance, the reduction in toxicity and exclamation usage indicates that the mediation process successfully fosters more neutral and composed responses. This adjustment can improve conversational tone, making model interactions feel more balanced and less emotionally charged. However, the argumentativeness scores show only slight changes, suggesting that mediation has a weaker influence on managing disagreements within discourse. Interestingly, trends differ between the user simulation setting and the main experiment, as models behave differently when generating both sides of an interaction versus responding to real user input. This underscores the importance of evaluating mediation not only as a procedural application but also in terms of its tangible communicative outcomes in realistic contexts.
