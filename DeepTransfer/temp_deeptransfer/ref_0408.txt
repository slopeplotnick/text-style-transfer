The system we developed demonstrates robust performance across various experimental conditions, indicating its reliability and consistency. This reliability is crucial for interpretability; inconsistent or erratic results would impede our understanding of interconnections. Instead, we observe distinct groupings, stable patterns, and coherent characteristics that are logically aligned. These findings confirm that our methodology effectively identifies training images that are semantically and stylistically congruent with the generated outputs, beyond mere visual or textual resemblance. Fundamentally, the statistical evidence indicates that the automated construction of knowledge graphs enhances the transparency and interpretability of the influence of training data on model outputs. This advancement supports critical objectives such as model explainability, copyright verification, and dataset transparency. For instance, when instructing a generative model to produce an image in a specific artistic style, such as "Ghibli-style," the model does not replicate exact images from its training dataset. Rather, it assimilates the overarching visual characteristics of the style from a curated collection of representative images during the training process. These stylistic attributes are encoded within the model's parameters and subsequently utilized to synthesize novel images that adhere to the learned aesthetic, without directly reproducing any individual training example. Consequently, the attribution of specific training images to generated outputs becomes intricate. The influence manifests at a higher level of abstraction, characterized by shared motifs, textures, themes, and compositional structures. Therefore, to discern the stylistic impact on the model, it is imperative to examine patterns and semantic interpretations rather than merely aligning specific objects. Our experimental methodology builds upon prior approaches to automated knowledge graph (KG) construction. Specifically, the process commenced with the extraction of frames from renowned Studio Ghibli films. Each frame was systematically annotated using a domain-specific ontology, facilitating the decomposition of visual content into structured semantic representations. These components were utilized to construct a "Ghibli World" knowledge graph that encapsulates the principal elements of the studio's visual style. Although the dataset is derived from frames of only ten films, it nonetheless provides a comprehensive representation of their stylistic characteristics.
