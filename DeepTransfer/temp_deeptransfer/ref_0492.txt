Because concepts are not explicitly defined in the data features, probing is commonly used to detect neurons, or combinations of neurons, that best represent target concepts. These probes are often linear; however, they may also be non-linear when the concept is not linearly separable. Moreover, probes can be trained using both supervised and unsupervised methods. The supervised approach requires labeled data of the human-specified concept, whereas the unsupervised approach identifies concepts important to the model but not necessarily interpretable to humans. The present study extends the modeling of structural equations beyond mere observational analysis, incorporating various levels of modeling complexity. These range from Fully Specified Causal Models (FSCMs), which are readily interpretable with domain-specific knowledge, to Partial Structural Causal Models (PSCMs) that encompass all FSCMs compatible with the empirical data. This underscores the critical importance of selecting appropriate modeling strategies and highlights the utility of partial information, such as counterfactual intervals, in scenarios where comprehensive FSCMs are unattainable. Galhotra et al. [2021] propose an explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) approach that leverages FSCMs to compute probabilities of sufficiency and necessity. Their methodology is specifically tailored to tabular data, incorporating causal modeling at the level of data features, which are presumed to represent high-level concepts. Additionally, the framework accommodates the inclusion of concepts beyond those explicitly present in the model input. While this approach assumes that the features correspond to meaningful concepts, it obviates the need for a causal generator. Furthermore, it assumes a preference for explanations that elucidate which input values must be altered to achieve a more favorable prediction or, conversely, which values must be preserved to maintain a desirable outcome when the current prediction is preferred unchanged.
