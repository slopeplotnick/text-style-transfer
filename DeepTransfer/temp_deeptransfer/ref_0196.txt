Ensuring that devices like smartphones, laptops, intelligent personal assistants, autonomous drones, and automotive systems can independently manage malicious prompts and harmful responses without relying on cloud-based solutions is crucial. Such on-device safety mechanisms enhance real-time latency, privacy, and deployability, particularly in constrained environments. While recent research efforts have focused on developing multilingual safety models, these approaches often struggle to generalize effectively across diverse languages, especially underrepresented ones, highlighting a significant gap in current AI safety research. A common strategy to address cross-lingual performance involves fine-tuning a pretrained model in English or another high-resource language and evaluating its performance in a different language by leveraging shared linguistic features. This approach generally benefits languages that are either high-resource or linguistically similar to the training set. However, it remains less effective for low-resource languages due to limited overlap and reduced representation during pretraining. To overcome these limitations, we introduce CREST, a parameter-efficient, safety-aligned binary classification model supporting 100 languages with only 0.5 billion parameters, trained on 13 high-resource languages. Leveraging the multilingual transformer XLM-R, we cluster semantically and structurally similar languages within its representation space. This clustering enables the model to generalize performance across languages within the same group, allowing training on a well-resourced language to benefit less prevalent ones in the cluster. This approach is particularly advantageous for safety-critical applications where annotated data is scarce or unavailable in many target languages. Despite its compact size, CREST consistently outperforms existing models, achieving robust and balanced performance across both high-resource and low-resource languages. By effectively utilizing shared lexical and scriptural features, it demonstrates strong cross-lingual generalization. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first instance of a safety model achieving such extensive multilingual applicability. In this study, we outline the methodologies implemented to address these challenges.
