GRAPHECTORY facilitates the computation of metrics and analyses for evaluating agent systems. As delineated in Table 1, six metrics are supported by GRAPHECTORY, along with methodologies for their determination. These metrics illustrate the degree of effort an agent exerts to complete a task. However, while these indicators do not independently determine the usefulness of the work, they can assess whether the work aligns with the problem's complexity or if the agents successfully resolve it. The metric known as Temporal Edge Count (TEC) quantifies the frequency of action transitions occurring over time, with an increase in TEC indicative of an extended trajectory to completion. Despite the ultimate outcome, Figure 2 illustrates a more extended trajectory for SWE-agent Dev, which necessitates 14 steps, as compared to SWE-agent DSK-V3, which completes the path in 8 steps. Similar to Node Count (NC), TEC values increase in alignment with problem difficulty, reflecting the relationship between GRAPHENCY complexity and task demands. Moreover, the Loop Count (LC) metric quantifies the frequency with which the agent engages in identical actions. As demonstrated subsequently (§3.2), an increased LC may represent an ineffective strategy, causing the agent to reiterate unsuccessful actions, akin to the loop at edge 9 in Figure 2-a, or necessitating reevaluation following actions that fail to resolve the issue, comparable to back edge 10 in Figure 2-a (§3.3.2). Alternatively, the agent may iteratively address the challenging problem by systematically re-evaluating and adjusting previous actions. LC is analogous to Cyclomatic Complexity [35] in software engineering, as it evaluates the branching structures and alternative execution paths. In the context of GRAPHENCY, the concept of branching elucidates how local cognitive shifts can alter the trajectory of development. Furthermore, the Average Loop Length (ALL) metric evaluates the average number of actions undertaken prior to the repetition of a previously executed action, suggesting that longer intervals between action repetitions may indicate weaker reasoning or an inability to gather proper contexts for reasoning. As demonstrated by our results, agents that invest in collecting a richer context during localization are less likely to experience long loops, thereby enabling them to quickly determine reasoning inefficiency and adapt their strategies accordingly (§3.2.1 and §3.3.2).

The efficiency of an agent's decision-making process is compromised when there is a significant delay in recognizing the ineffectiveness of its actions. This situation indicates deficiencies in cognitive processing or contextual analysis. However, the process of localization can be enhanced through the acquisition of additional contextual information by agents. Such acquisition results in abbreviated feedback loops, which therefore facilitate rapid identification of ineffective cognitive strategies and subsequent modification of plans (§3.2.1 and §3.3.2).
