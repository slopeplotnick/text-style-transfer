Recent advancements in Large Language Models (LLMs) have predominantly focused on harnessing increased computational resources during inference. Specifically, models such as OpenAI's o1 and DeepSeek-R1 engage in extended cognitive processing, systematically exploring concepts, formulating hypotheses, and rectifying errors prior to delivering a conclusive response. This trend of amplifying reasoning during test-time computation has emerged as a dominant approach, as it frequently enhances model accuracy and problem-solving capabilities. However, it is important to note that simply prolonging the reasoning process does not always lead to improved performance and may indeed exacerbate issues, particularly when models engage in excessive deliberation. Empirical findings suggest that accurate responses often result from reasoning processes involving more concise deductive steps. Consequently, an alternative proposition involves executing concurrent reasoning processes and subsequently synthesizing their outcomes, typically by selecting the most frequently occurring response when the task presents a definitive resolution. This methodology proves effective for mathematical problems or formulating responses to multiple-choice inquiries. Nevertheless, while many real-world tasks lack a singular correct solution, some tasks inherently admit multiple valid solutions. In the context of tool development, coding utilities must ensure functionality in their outputs, whereas research utilities frequently necessitate the integration of auxiliary instruments, comprehensive strategizing, and extensive elucidation. In these instances, it is not feasible to simply select the most prevalent answer due to the absence of a definitive option, despite the efficacy of this approach in more straightforward scenarios. This prompts a significant inquiry: How might we leverage concurrent reasoning processes for tasks lacking definitive answers, while eschewing reliance on selecting the most prevalent outcome? To address this question, the present study introduces THINKMERGE, a methodology that synthesizes the outputs of multiple reasoning processes at the testing phase to yield a singular optimal response. Despite occasional discrepancies even in completed iterations, these remain relatively minor.
