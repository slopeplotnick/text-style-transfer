In addressing the issue of belief entrenchment in reasoning models, we propose a statistical measure to quantify the extent of a model's adherence to its established beliefs. Specifically, a model is considered entrenched if it violates the Martingale property, a fundamental concept in Bayesian reasoning which posits that belief updates should not be predictable in advance. This violation serves as an indicator of irrational belief entrenchment. Therefore, if a model's future predictions can be reliably inferred from its prior beliefs, it suggests a deviation from the Martingale property, indicating an undue fixation on initial beliefs. To demonstrate this, we establish that such a deviation results in decreased model accuracy. Our investigation includes empirical examinations in three domains: forecasting occurrences, providing subjective responses to inquiries, and critiquing scholarly articles. This tendency to forecast prospective beliefs based on antecedent ones contravenes the Martingale property and hinders the adaptability of models on complex reasoning tasks, as they frequently exhibit an excessive adherence to prior beliefs. Consequently, we employ the Martingale Score to quantify the extent to which a model's belief updates can be anticipated solely based on its prior beliefs. Our evaluation of prevalent models using this score reveals a frequent occurrence of excessive adherence to beliefs across various domains, prompts, and models. Critically, the Martingale Score strongly correlates with a drop in ground-truth accuracy, as evidenced by the Brier Score, in domains where such labels are available.
