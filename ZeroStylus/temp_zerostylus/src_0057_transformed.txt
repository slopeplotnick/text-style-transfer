An examination involving both CNNs and ViTs, all trained under uniform adversarial protocols, aims to elucidate the impact of adversarial training on the transferability of adversarial attacks. Contrary to initial expectations that robust models would produce less transferable attacks, our findings demonstrate a paradoxical outcome; robust models indeed generate attacks with heightened transferability. This phenomenon indicates that adversarial training fundamentally alters the feature spaces of models, inadvertently facilitating transfer across diverse architectures, as depicted in Figure 1. Consequently, the features acquired by these models may introduce cross-model vulnerabilities, suggesting a systemic rather than incidental effect of model architecture. Our comprehensive study includes extensive adversarial training across 36 instances of CNN and ViT models, underscoring a thorough approach to understanding the implications of adversarial methodologies on model architecture. Additionally, all trained models and the associated code will be made available on GitHub, thereby supporting reproducibility and further research. Furthermore, the increase in attack transferability due to adversarial training challenges prevailing notions of model robustness. Building upon the framework of Integrated Gradients (IG), our work introduces a novel attack methodology termed Momentum Integrated Gradient (MIG). MIG leverages a momentum-based approach to iteratively modify attacks, prioritizing critical regions identified by IG, thus enhancing the likelihood of misclassifications. A pivotal characteristic of IG, implementation invariance, ensures that MIG attacks function effectively across diverse model architectures, including both CNNs and ViTs, as well as between these two frameworks. As illustrated in Figure 2, recent advancements have surpassed the limitations of previous methodologies, such as PGD. The significance of IG lies in its capacity to identify the features most critical for a model's predictions, thereby aiding in the evaluation of the model's suitability for specific tasks. The approach involves accumulating gradients from a baseline input to the actual input, where the baseline serves as a reference point for analysis.
