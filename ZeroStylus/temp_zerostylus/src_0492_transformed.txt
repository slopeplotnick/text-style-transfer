Because concepts are not explicitly defined within data features, it is a common practice to employ probing techniques to detect neurons or combinations of neurons that optimally represent target concepts. These probes are predominantly linear; however, they can also exhibit non-linear characteristics in cases where the concept is not linearly separable. Moreover, probing methods can be categorized into two types: supervised approaches, which rely on labeled data to identify human-specified concepts, and unsupervised approaches, which uncover concepts that are relevant to the model but may not be interpretable by humans. Building on this, the present study extends the analysis of structural equations beyond observation-based estimation by incorporating modeling under general assumptions. Specifically, it examines various levels of modeling, ranging from functional structural causal models (FSCMs), which are straightforward to interpret using domain knowledge, to partial structural causal models (PSCMs), which encompass all FSCMs consistent with the observed data. This distinction underscores the critical importance of selecting an appropriate modeling strategy and highlights the utility of partial information, such as counterfactual intervals, when complete FSCMs are inaccessible. Furthermore, Galhotra et al. (2021) introduce an explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) method that employs FSCMs to compute probabilities of sufficiency and necessity. Their approach focuses on tabular data and integrates causal modeling into the data features, operating under the assumption that these features correspond to high-level concepts. This framework also allows for the inclusion of additional concepts that may not be explicitly present in the model input. However, the effectiveness of this method is contingent on the interpretability of the features as meaningful concepts, and it does not require the presence of a causal generative model. Finally, the method assumes a preference regarding the model’s output, framing explanations in terms of which subsets of input values are sufficient to shift a prediction toward a more desirable outcome or which values are necessary to preserve an optimal prediction when the preferred outcome remains unchanged.
