Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly employed as automated judges for evaluating the output of other generative systems, a paradigm known as "Thinking-LLM-as-a-Judge." This approach offers a scalable and cost-effective alternative to human evaluation, which is often both slow and expensive. To mitigate the inherent stochasticity and noise associated with single-pass judgments, it is common to leverage inference-time compute (ITC) by generating multiple independent reasoning and rating samples for each item under evaluation. However, the reliability of the final judgment critically depends on how these multiple outputs are aggregated. Current aggregation methods, such as majority voting or heuristics based on model confidence scores, are often brittle and statistically suboptimal. These methods are particularly fragile in the presence of ties. For example, a simple majority vote fails to distinguish between a narrow 5-to-4 decision and a decisive 9-to-0 consensus, thus discarding valuable information about the strength of evidence contained within the full distribution of votes. This insensitivity to evidential strength results in less reliable and robust evaluations. Therefore, we argue that the aggregation step should not be an afterthought but rather a critical component for effectively utilizing ITC. We propose a principled Distribution-Calibrated Aggregation (DCA) scheme that moves beyond simple vote-counting. Our method operates directly on the full counts of positive, negative, and tie votes, thereby preserving the full signal in the sample distribution. Specifically, we model the three-way preference outcomes using a Bradley-Terry-Davidson formulation, which explicitly parametrizes both the preference margin and the global propensity for ties. By estimating parameters via maximum likelihood on a small calibration set and employing the Mean Absolute Error (MAE) Bayes action at inference, our approach remains aligned with the evaluation metric while leveraging a well-behaved probabilistic fit. This avoids loss-metric mismatch and yields more accurate judgments.
