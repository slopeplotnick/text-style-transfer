The methodology described is universally applicable across various scientific disciplines. For instance, geologists represent conceptual models through diagrams in academic publications and reports, mathematicians refine these into constraints within prior probability models, and geophysicists apply such constraints to perform Bayesian inversion processes. Furthermore, alternative datasets may occasionally be required, as the initial hypothesis might prove to be erroneous. This challenge exemplifies a broader scientific problem: how can researchers substantiate the validity of their assumptions? The Austrian philosopher Karl Popper devoted his lifetime to addressing philosophical questions of this nature. He argued that scientific inquiry should avoid reliance on induction, where theories are derived directly from empirical observations. Instead, Popper viewed theories as speculative constructs formulated by individuals to address deficiencies in preceding theoretical frameworks. Once formulated, these theories must undergo rigorous testing against empirical observations. If a theory fails to align with observed data, it must be reconsidered or discarded—a principle known as falsification. Conversely, theories that withstand empirical scrutiny are considered the most robust. In the context of uncertainty quantification, models are regarded not as definitive representations of reality but rather as hypotheses subject to testing. The number of hypotheses available corresponds precisely to the number of models proposed. While prior knowledge can constrain these hypotheses, empirical data should serve not to confirm a model but to falsify one that does not align with observed results. Popper provides a framework to address the inherent subjectivity in Bayesian reasoning, which is fundamentally inductive, by contrasting it with the deductive methodology of falsification. If the prior distribution fails to account for current empirical data—within the bounds of measurement error—the Bayesian prior is effectively falsified. This implies that no matter how many models are sampled from the prior, none adequately conform to the data. Although Popper's concept of falsification may reduce the subjectivity of Bayesian reasoning after empirical testing, his framework does not provide a mechanism for revising the prior distribution, which remains contingent upon human judgment.
