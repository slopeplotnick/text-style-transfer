Algorithm 1, R-Few, involves the Challenger Role in Few-Shot Grounded Generation, wherein the Challenger computes rewards by engaging in few-shot grounded generation. Conversely, the Solver Role in Online Curriculum Learning entails computing the Solver reward and updating the Solver, which ensures a focus on tasks that balance complexity and feasibility, aligning with robust learning theory. This process is crucial as it ensures that the Solver concentrates on challenges that are difficult yet achievable, thus aligning well with established learning theory. Within the Solver Role, the Solver reward is computed by integrating both synthetic and authentic data, adjusted according to curriculum complexity. In contrast, R-Few retains the iterative structure of R-ZERO but introduces a data setup that is both grounded and attuned to the curriculum. Algorithm 2, C-Gen, involves the Challenger Role in Few-Shot Task Generation by formulating tasks using limited examples derived from human input; otherwise, the Challenger is adjusted. Meanwhile, the Solver Role follows an Adaptive Learning Pathway, which evaluates the Solver's efficacy and modifies it accordingly. This ensures the Challenger creates tasks that are instructive and attainable, aligning with optimal educational paradigms. Subsequently, the Solver attempts to resolve these tasks and evaluates the frequency of success. This indicates that relying solely on predetermined difficulty metrics is inadequate. Instead, selectively filtering tasks of moderate complexity from both synthetic and authentic data sources creates a more effective selection process. In our primary model deployment, we employed the Qwen3-4B-Base and Qwen3-8B-Base models, which align with the configuration utilized in this study. Moreover, to facilitate a comprehensive comparison, we evaluate our framework against several baseline models: (1) the Base Model, which is the unmodified original model accessible for download; (2) R-Zero, the pioneering self-play approach wherein the model autonomously generates and responds to its own inquiries for self-improvement; (3) Absolute Zero, a self-play configuration restricted to tasks involving code generation, with verification via Python; and (4) SPICE, a self-play strategy that employs extensive documents to formulate reasoning tasks with responses derived from the documents. We establish comparable baselines for models (1) and (2) utilizing the same training configurations, whereas the performance metrics for models (3) and (4) are derived from the findings reported by Liu et al.
