Position Bias. Despite the advanced capabilities of large language models (LLMs) in Natural Language Processing (NLP), recent research has demonstrated their susceptibility to the structural arrangement of input data, a phenomenon commonly referred to as position bias. Position bias describes the tendency of models to disproportionately prioritize information presented at specific positions within the input sequence, often resulting in the neglect of critical details located elsewhere [Jung et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2023a; Zheng et al., 2023; Chhabra et al., 2024]. This issue is particularly prominent in multi-document summarization (MDS), where models process multiple long documents concatenated into a single context. In such scenarios, the likelihood of information being included in the summary is significantly influenced by the document's placement within the context window. Consequently, LLM-generated summaries may exhibit imbalanced content coverage or skewed perspectives depending on the order of input. One notable manifestation of this phenomenon is the Lost in the Middle problem. For example, Liu et al. [2023] examined LLM performance in multi-document question answering and key-value pair retrieval tasks, revealing a characteristic U-shaped performance curve: accuracy is highest when relevant information appears at the beginning (primacy effect) or end (recency effect) of the context, but degrades when this information is located in the middle. While this analysis primarily focused on tasks requiring short, limited-token outputs, related work by Koo et al. [2023] has shown that similar structural biases emerge in annotation tasks. Specifically, when LLMs are tasked with evaluation, they exhibit a systematic preference for the first or last option presented, even if the optimal response resides elsewhere in the sequence. Moreover, position bias is often reflected as lead bias, where models disproportionately emphasize initial segments of input data. Chhabra et al. [2024] observed that while some models, such as GPT-3.5-Turbo, exhibit relatively mild lead bias, others display substantial sensitivity to early-positioned content, particularly in extreme summarization tasks. Beyond compromising performance, such positional biases raise significant concerns regarding fairness. For instance, Olabisi and Agrawal [2024] demonstrated that in the context of social multi-document summarization, arranging tweets by dialect could result in summaries that disproportionately favor the group presented first. Therefore, these findings highlight the profound impact of input ordering on both the quality and fairness of model outputs.
