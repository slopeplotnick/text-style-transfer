The PF model serves as a continuum mechanics framework designed to simulate the kinetic evolution of a multiphase, multimaterial system toward thermodynamic equilibrium. Notably, the model is particularly effective for systems with constrained spatial dimensions and temporal scales, such as those relevant to the fabrication of solution-processed solar cells. In this context, the thermodynamic properties of the system are encapsulated within a Gibbs free energy functional, which governs the forces driving the system's progression toward equilibrium. Specifically, our methodology focuses on key processes involved in the formation of solar cells, including solvent evaporation, phase separation, and crystallization. These phase transitions are described using Allen-Cahn equations for the kinetics of phase changes, coupled with Cahn-Hilliard equations to account for diffusive mass transport. To ensure consistency in the simulation framework, the PF model is integrated with a fluid dynamics solver, which incorporates advective mass transport and ensures an accurate representation of mass movement dynamics. Further details of the simulation methodology can be found in Section S2. The material parameters derived from these simulations are subsequently employed for optical analyses of light propagation within solar cells. Depending on the complexity of the solar cell under investigation, either numerical optical simulations (e.g., COMSOL Multiphysics, JCMsuite) or more analytically tractable methods, such as the transfer matrix method, are applied. The solar cell is modeled as a stratified stack of isotropic media to enable a comprehensive understanding of the interaction between light and the cell's structure. Moreover, to facilitate the prediction of charge generation within the solar cell, a surrogate machine learning (ML) model is employed. In particular, a neural network is trained to predict the charge generation rate as a function of key variables, such as the angle of incidence of light, the photoactive layer thickness, and the spectral properties of the incident light. Details regarding the neural network model and its implementation are provided in Section S3.
