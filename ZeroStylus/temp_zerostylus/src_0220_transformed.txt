Effective analysis demands tools capable of capturing the propagation, evolution, and topology of reasoning over time, rather than merely providing snapshot assessments of immediate correctness. To address this need, we introduce the concept of the Emergent Systemic Risk Horizon (ESRH), which serves as a pivotal threshold marking the point beyond which the assumptions of separability and reversibility of systems and their components are no longer valid. Beyond this horizon, it becomes essential to evaluate safety as a property of the entire interconnected system, emphasizing its ability to sustain stability, coherence, and responsiveness amidst continuous interaction. Consequently, advancing this perspective necessitates the design and execution of novel experiments, the collection of longitudinal datasets, and the development of systematic methodologies to observe and analyze the dynamic behaviors of collectives over time. Moreover, governance frameworks must be scaled to match the extensive complexity of these networks, a topic that will be explored in greater detail in the subsequent section through the introduction of Institutional AI as a framework for embedding oversight and norm-enforcement mechanisms directly within multi-agent systems. The increasing complexity of language model ecosystems further underscores the need for governance approaches capable of evolving in tandem with these systems. As models transition toward semi-autonomous operation—engaging in reasoning, negotiation, and collaboration without continuous human intervention—traditional approaches, such as centralized oversight, static safety protocols, or periodic audits, become increasingly insufficient. These conventional methods often presuppose conditions of stability, observability, and clear accountability, which are inherently absent in the dynamic and decentralized nature of multi-agent environments. Therefore, we propose Institutional AI as a framework for system-level safety, wherein mechanisms for rule enforcement, evaluation, and oversight are inherently integrated into the architecture of these systems, rather than being externally imposed. The objective is not to replicate human institutions but rather to formalize and embed key functional principles, such as distributed authority, procedural transparency, and adaptive norm revision, within networks of interacting models. Within this context, the concept of *constitutional AI* emerges as a complementary approach, wherein models are governed by a predefined set of normative principles codified during their training or fine-tuning. While such constitutions can enhance single-agent alignment and consistency in isolated interactions, they remain insufficient to address the broader systemic risks that arise from interactions among multiple agents. These challenges highlight the need to move beyond dyadic considerations and toward governance architectures that are capable of addressing the emergent risks inherent in complex, interconnected systems.
