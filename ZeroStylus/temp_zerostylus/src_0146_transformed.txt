As established in prior discussion, unguided self-evolving systems tend to plateau rapidly, exhibiting instability that frequently culminates in diversity collapse and reward exploitation. Our empirical findings corroborate these patterns. As illustrated in the left panel, R-Zero demonstrates a precipitous decline in question diversity during training, where the number of distinct questions diminishes sharply from 35 to fewer than 20 within the initial 50 training steps. While the 2-gram diversity score shows an increase post-50 steps, this rise coincides with a pronounced escalation in question length, as depicted in the middle panel. This increase in diversity is largely superficial, as it stems from prolonged outputs generating unique n-gram combinations, thereby inflating the diversity metric without contributing to substantive semantic exploration. Furthermore, a parallel trend is evident in question length itself, wherein R-Zero progressively elongates its questions, eventually producing overly verbose queries. This phenomenon suggests reward hacking, whereby the model manipulates superficial attributes such as verbosity to enhance perceived diversity or difficulty, rather than achieving genuine cognitive improvements. In contrast, R-Few maintains stable diversity throughout the training process, coupled with consistent question length, indicating that minimal human input effectively mitigates diversity collapse. Turning to the right panel, both approaches exhibit a gradual increase in question difficulty over time. However, this apparent similarity masks significant qualitative differences. To quantify difficulty, we systematically relabeled questions generated at different training stages using Gemini-2.5-Pro and subsequently assessed these questions by evaluating whether Qwen3-8B-Base could correctly address them. Here, difficulty is operationalized as the proportion of questions the model fails to answer accurately. While both methods display an upward trajectory in difficulty, the underlying sources diverge. R-Zero increases difficulty primarily through extended question length, which inflates perceived complexity without requiring deeper cognitive engagement. This constitutes another form of reward exploitation, as length-induced complexity undermines the integrity of difficulty progression. Conversely, R-Few maintains consistent question length and fosters self-evolution toward genuinely more challenging questions, thereby preserving diversity and structural coherence while promoting meaningful cognitive advancement.
