Ensuring that devices such as smartphones, laptops, smart assistants, drones, and automotive systems can autonomously manage erroneous prompts and responses without reliance on cloud-based solutions is imperative for real-time safety enforcement. This approach enhances speed and privacy while enabling functionality in constrained environments. However, recent investigations into multilingual safety models have sought to improve their applicability across diverse linguistic contexts, yet these models often exhibit suboptimal performance, particularly when addressing low-resource languages lacking adequate computational support. A prevalent strategy involves training a model in English or another resource-rich language and subsequently evaluating its performance in a different linguistic context, leveraging shared structural and semantic features across languages. Consequently, languages that are either well-supported or closely related benefit more significantly, whereas less common languages continue to encounter difficulties due to minimal commonality and limited usage during training. To address this challenge, we developed CREST, a multilingual model encompassing 100 languages, constructed with only 0.5 billion parameters and trained on a foundation of 13 high-resource languages. Moreover, to examine the grouping of languages with similar semantic and structural characteristics, we employ a multilingual transformer model. Thus, training the model on a high-resource language within the group enhances its performance across less-resourced counterparts in the same linguistic family, as the shared structure and overlap provide indirect benefits. This holds substantial importance as it assists in addressing the paucity of data for safety applications across numerous languages. Despite its relatively modest size, CREST demonstrates superior performance compared to other models and effectively accommodates both high-resource and low-resource languages. Furthermore, to evaluate the capability of utilizing shared lexical items and scripts for linguistic generalization, the model employs these components across various languages. To the best of our knowledge, existing research has not demonstrated a safety model capable of performing effectively across such a diverse range of languages.
