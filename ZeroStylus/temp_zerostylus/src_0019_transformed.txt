Our approach utilizes a hierarchical framework that integrates distinct statistical distributions to independently analyze various components of the statistical process. This framework facilitates the demonstration of independence among certain elements. For instance, although variations in energy usage exist among CPUs with identical specifications and different instruction sets, these discrepancies are independent of one another. Our methodology, as depicted in Figure 1, constructs a Worst-Case Energy Consumption (WCEC) model to assess a device's total energy usage, comprising two main components: (1) the measurement component, which evaluates the energy consumption of the benchmark executed on the target hardware, and (2) the modeling component, which employs a Bayesian model to characterize the energy consumption associated with the programming language. Moreover, there are several statically typed programming languages compatible with the Java Virtual Machine (JVM). To model these languages, we adopt a two-pronged approach: (1) the measurement component quantifies the energy of bytecode patterns, a process in which the compiler translates a programming language's source code into bytecode, as illustrated in Figure 2; and (2) the modeling component applies statistical methods to analyze the energy consumption of the resultant bytecode. Each microbenchmark includes a version of a source code statement for every possible data type and size relevant to a specific operation, as shown in Figure 3. The characteristics of each microbenchmark are defined by its measurement cycle, which assesses current, voltage, and the duration required to complete a single microbenchmark operation. The procedure initiates by configuring the multimeter to ensure readiness for measurement during the cycle. Subsequently, the garbage collector is executed to minimize memory noise during the operational cycle. Each microbenchmark cycle is repeated ten times to accommodate measurement variability, employing a split plot design to mitigate order-induced bias by randomizing the cycle execution sequence.
