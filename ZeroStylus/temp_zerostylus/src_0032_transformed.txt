Training robust reward models presents significant challenges, primarily due to their subjective and context-dependent nature (Chakraborty et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2023). Moreover, learned proxies are vulnerable to exploitation via reward hacking (Casper et al., 2023). Furthermore, the sparsity of annotations exacerbates the difficulty in managing detailed behaviors effectively (Wu et al., 2023). Consequently, these challenges have led to various approaches aimed at reducing reliance on annotations. Notably, Direct Preference Optimization (Rafailov et al., 2023) circumvents explicit reward modeling by optimizing policies based on curated preference data, albeit still necessitating preference datasets. Verifiable rewards (DeepSeek-AI, 2025; Lambert et al., 2024) offer a potential solution by leveraging observable outcomes; however, their utility is limited to scenarios where automatic validation is feasible. Self-assessment techniques (Yuan et al., 2024; Lee et al., 2024; Garg et al., 2025) remain susceptible to contextual cues and inherent biases (Zheng et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024), thereby undermining their reliability across diverse applications. Importantly, these methodologies persist in assessing quality through external indicators, thereby neglecting the intrinsic representations within the model. Ideally, the validity of a generation should be mirrored in its internal computations. For instance, when a model generates "The capital of France is Paris," its hidden states encode activated factual knowledge (Smith et al., 2023); conversely, when fabrication occurs, distinct activation patterns are observable (Lee et al., 2024). This observation prompts the question: Can text quality be evaluated solely through internal signals, thus eliminating the need for external supervision in aligning large language models (LLMs)? Our analysis indicates that examining a straightforward geometric characteristic of LLM hidden states, termed effective dimensionality, provides a reliable measure of LLM response quality. We propose stable rank (Rudelson & Vershynin, 2007), derived from matrix theory, as an unsupervised metric for evaluating generation quality. Stable rank assesses the distribution of information by measuring the ratio of total variance to the primary direction variance in the hidden-state representation, thus quantifying the number of independent semantic directions employed by a response. This measure balances representational richness with coherence, where a high stable rank signifies that information is distributed across multiple dimensions rather than concentrated in a few. The theoretical foundation of this approach is informed by two distinct lines of research. The softmax bottleneck analysis (Yang et al., 2018) underscores the necessity for high-dimensional semantic representations in accurate language modeling, as the inherent complexity of natural language probability distributions precludes effective modeling in low-rank spaces.
