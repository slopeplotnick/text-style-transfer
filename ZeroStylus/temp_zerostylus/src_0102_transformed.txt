The origin of individuals is not considered in our evaluation process. However, Hallucination Detection (HD) methods rely on internal model states, which Fact Verification (FV) configurations cannot provide. Efforts to address this discrepancy have primarily involved constructing static benchmarks of large language model (LLM) outputs, such as HELM [60]. Yet, such benchmarks remain limited as they are unable to effectively evaluate novel or out-of-distribution models and fail to capture dynamic generative signals. Consequently, this methodological incompatibility has led to the development of two parallel research trajectories. These separate paths have resulted in redundant efforts and left fundamental questions unanswered. Specifically, it remains unclear whether one paradigm demonstrates inherent superiority, how their respective strengths and weaknesses diverge, or whether a synergistic integration of their capabilities is feasible. Moreover, the continued isolation of these approaches prevents the exploitation of their complementary advantages: for instance, HD's internal model signals could enhance FV's evidence-based reasoning, while FV's retrieved contextual grounding could refine HD's uncertainty assessments. To address this longstanding divide, we take a decisive step forward by presenting the first large-scale and systematic empirical study to directly compare Hallucination Detection and Fact Verification within a unified framework. Unlike previous efforts that primarily focused on benchmarking, our work provides a deeper investigation into the fundamental relationship between these paradigms. This unified approach enables us, for the first time, to explore three critical research questions (RQs) that were previously inaccessible due to the historical separation of these fields. First, regarding overall performance (RQ1), we ask: how do HD and FV methods differ in their ability to detect factual inaccuracies when evaluating the same LLM-generated outputs? Second, in terms of complementary strengths (RQ2), we investigate whether HD and FV target distinct aspects of factual inaccuracies—thereby demonstrating complementary capabilities—or whether they share overlapping weaknesses. Third, concerning hybrid performance (RQ3), we examine whether signals from both paradigms can be effectively integrated into a hybrid system that surpasses the performance of either approach when applied independently. This direct comparison, previously deemed infeasible due to the paradigms' fundamental incompatibility, becomes possible through the introduction of UniFact—a unified evaluation framework designed specifically to resolve this incompatibility. Unlike static benchmarks that depend on pre-generated, fixed outputs from LLMs, UniFact employs a dynamic evaluation process. It queries any LLM in real time to generate responses to factual questions and utilizes an automated labeling system to determine the correctness of these responses. This is achieved by comparing the generated outputs against ground-truth answers and their corresponding pre-labeled reference knowledge. Crucially, this dynamic process simultaneously fulfills the requirements of both approaches: it provides the textual outputs necessary for FV methods while also supplying the internal model signals required for HD methods. By enabling direct, instance-level, head-to-head comparisons of HD and FV on the same generated content, UniFact represents a significant advancement in bridging the divide between these two paradigms.

The methodology elucidated in this analysis facilitates a direct juxtaposition of the two approaches, thereby enabling a comparative evaluation on identical content. This method contrasts with existing works that emphasize generator refinement through techniques such as Mirror-Consistency (Li et al., 2024), Self-Contrast (Zhang et al., 2024), and Step-Back Prompting (Zheng et al., 2023a), which utilize iterative reflection or diverse perspectives to generate higher-quality samples. Furthermore, Self-Check (Miao et al., 2023) employs step-wise verification to filter errors. These approaches, while enhancing the generator, often incur sequential computational costs. However, our focus diverges by concentrating on the aggregator; we accept the noisy distribution of parallel samples and apply a distribution-calibrated layer to robustly estimate the ground truth. A pivotal decision in designing an LLM-as-a-Judge for pairwise comparisons (Zheng et al., 2023b) is whether to permit the judge to declare a tie or compel it to select a preference. Initially, we demonstrate that compelling the model to break ties may induce biases in LLMs. Subsequently, we illustrate that tie decisions are highly sensitive to judge parameters, necessitating a more robust aggregation method to mitigate these effects. Importantly, ties serve to reduce LLM biases, as LLM-as-a-Judge displays various systematic biases (Ye et al., 2024). A prevalent issue is positional bias (Shi et al., 2025), where the model's preference is influenced by the order of presented responses. As shown in Table 1, the results from the same experiment, with a prompt allowing ties, significantly reduce positional bias for both models. This finding demonstrates that incorporating a tie option is not merely a feature for recognizing equivalence but is potentially a crucial mechanism for debiasing the evaluation process. Nevertheless, tie decisions lack stability, as evidenced by the fact that in three-way preference tasks, the vote distribution of an LLM-as-a-judge is highly sensitive to variations in the evaluation setup.
