The MODOMA system is composed of two primary components: the mother agent and the daughter agent. Delilah, a tool specifically designed for the generation and analysis of the Dutch language, functions as the mother agent (Cremers and Hijzelendoorn 1995/2024a). Notably, Delilah’s grammatical knowledge does not rely on a corpus; instead, it operates using a predefined grammar encoded in graph structures comprised of attribute-value matrices. These graphs serve as explicit representations of words and grammatical constructions, detailing properties such as phonological form, conceptual meaning, logical semantic representation (cf. Cremers and Reckman 2008), grammatical number, person, and syntactic category (e.g., determiner, noun, or verb). For instance, Delilah’s lexical and grammatical knowledge of the Dutch article *de* ('the.MASC/FEM') is specified in a template, as illustrated in Figure 1. The detailed specifications of these templates are derived from linguistic analyses of the Dutch lexicon and grammar, thereby ensuring that Delilah’s grammar is closely aligned with linguistic insights into Dutch. This explicit encoding of linguistic knowledge enables Delilah to function as both a parser and generator, systematically combining templates to construct more complex utterances such as noun phrases and sentences. Through the process of unification—a mathematical procedure that verifies the compatibility of templates by detecting and resolving conflicting features—Delilah integrates information from multiple templates into a unified representation. If no conflicting values are found, the unification process generates a composite template containing all relevant information from the original templates (Schieber 2003). For example, the complement position associated with the article *de* ('the.MASC/FEM') is constrained to combine only with noun phrases, as specified by the feature ARG|SYNSEM|CAT:n. This constraint allows Delilah to generate phrases such as *de vrouw* ('the woman') while maintaining syntactic and grammatical consistency. Given the detailed and intricate nature of Delilah’s representations, a comprehensive account of all its features lies beyond the scope of this discussion. However, prior research provides extensive insights into unification as applied to Delilah, including Cremers et al. (2014, 259–265). Furthermore, the operational methodology employed by Delilah shares significant similarities with head-driven phrase structure grammar (HPSG; Pollard and Sag 1986, 1994). In this framework, the distinction between lexicon and grammar is effectively neutralized, as both lexical items and grammatical rules are treated as unified entities within the same formal system. Delilah’s grammar also incorporates principles from combinatory list grammar, a framework related to combinatory categorial grammar (CCG; Cremers 2002, pp. 378–386; Cremers et al. 2014, pp. 115–137; cf. Baldridge and Kruijff 2003, Moortgat 1997, Steedman 1996). The theoretical foundations and implementation of this grammar model have been extensively analyzed in prior studies, including those by Cremers et al. (2014) and Reckman (2009, pp. 25–75). For the purposes of the MODOMA system, Delilah is utilized in its original configuration, adhering to its initial design without modifications. Consequently, Delilah provides a linguistically informed, template-driven approach to language processing, offering a contrast to data-driven approaches such as those employed by large language models.
