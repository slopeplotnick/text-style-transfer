Our method enhances action precision, consistent with the outcomes observed in simulation tests. Moreover, it significantly addresses policy failures resulting from inadequate teleoperation data. Specifically, during the analysis of pen grasping techniques, variations in grasping methods and timing contribute to significant differences in states across grasping modes, thereby resulting in a sparse distribution of observation-action pairs within the expert dataset. Consequently, when executing a grasping task, the base policy often becomes unstable, failing to close the gripper properly, reopening after grasping, or oscillating between two grasping modes. However, our test-time scaling framework effectively addresses these issues: the gripper operates as intended, and the robot reliably avoids unfavorable modes of operation. This improvement is attributed to the scaling method, which systematically eliminates erroneous paths introduced by noise within the teleoperated dataset, thereby enhancing the overall framework's effectiveness. To address this, the challenge of mapping from features to action errors complicates the learning process, necessitating the model's comprehension of both effective and ineffective features. During test-time scaling, prioritizing the selection of the optimal action is crucial, as it plays a critical role in achieving robust decision-making. The observed decline in performance is attributed to overfitting on the training dataset, which adversely impacts the model's ability to generalize effectively. Furthermore, the use of separate encoders, rather than relying on internal features, and their joint training with the CFN pseudo-count network result in highly similar feature representations, which hinders accurate pseudo-counting and significantly degrades overall performance. Additionally, the method in question diminishes operational efficiency, specifically because it fails to leverage the VLA's inherent capability to comprehend scenes and actions effectively. In response, our test-time scaling framework, TACO, employs a straightforward pseudo-count estimator as a robust mechanism for verifying action chunks. Notably, TACO demonstrates the capacity to interface with various VLA models, consistently refining the underlying policy and yielding measurable improvements across a range of real-world scenarios and simulated evaluations.

In our approach, we address the challenges of exploration and instability by employing test-time scaling as an alternative to relying solely on the representational capabilities of the underlying VLA. Specifically, this method mitigates the limitations associated with evaluating novel actions via perturbation, which is inherently constrained and may lead to performance degradation caused by distributional shifts in action prediction. Consequently, by integrating test-time scaling, we effectively curtail these shifts, ensuring more stable and reliable performance in downstream tasks.
