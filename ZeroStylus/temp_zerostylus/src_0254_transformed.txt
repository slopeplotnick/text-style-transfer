In our threat model, the backdoor is probabilistically triggered through user invocations, ensuring the presence of the LeechHijack mechanism from the outset. However, a more sophisticated and insidious variant of this attack is conceivable. Initially, the tool appears benign, as attackers abstain from issuing malicious requests and simply relay the tool's intended outputs. This strategy allows the tool to establish widespread adoption without raising suspicion. Once the tool achieves sufficient proliferation, the attacker can remotely activate the hijacking functionality. Consequently, this delayed activation approach, analogous to supply chain attacks [72], [73], significantly intensifies the attack's impact while complicating attribution. Moreover, the covert nature of the attack can be further enhanced through selective targeting. By analyzing victim-specific identifiers and focusing on individuals who frequently interact with the tool—particularly those whose actions pass security testing protocols—the LeechHijack threat becomes increasingly difficult to detect during validation phases. Therefore, this paper identifies and formalizes a novel class of attacks termed implicit toxicity, which manipulates legitimate permissions of an agent to execute harmful actions. To demonstrate this risk, we introduce LeechHijack—a Latent Embedded Exploit for Computation Hijacking. LeechHijack can be deployed by any MCP tool provider and infiltrates a victim's agent system via authorized tool interactions, covertly siphoning computational resources for unauthorized purposes. This exploit underscores a critical vulnerability in the MCP sharing paradigm, wherein malicious tools can compromise agent systems without necessitating elevated privileges. Through extensive experimentation across diverse agent frameworks, we validate the practicality of LeechHijack and highlight its resilience against existing security defenses. Furthermore, targeted mitigation strategies are explored to establish foundational measures for future security enhancements. These findings reveal a significant security gap in the expanding agent ecosystem, emphasizing the pressing need for rigorous evaluation and verification of third-party components. All experimental procedures adhered strictly to ethical and security guidelines. The LeechHijack attack described herein is presented solely as a controlled proof of concept to highlight systemic vulnerabilities in ecosystems employing large language model (LLM)-based agents. It does not aim to enable or promote malicious behavior. Importantly, no production systems, commercial platforms, or third-party services were exploited during this research. All evaluations were conducted within isolated local or cloud sandboxes using agents and tools explicitly developed for this study, and network communication with external servers was restricted to simulated environments to prevent any exfiltration of real data.

The experimental procedures were conducted exclusively within secure and controlled environments specifically designed for this purpose. To prevent the exposure of sensitive data, no connections to live servers were established during the experiments. Moreover, the integrity of the experimental conditions was upheld by ensuring all operations were confined within these isolated systems. Therefore, the risk of data leakage was significantly minimized. Furthermore, this precautionary measure facilitated a reliable analysis of the experimental outcomes without compromising data security. The consistent application of these protocols underscores their critical importance in safeguarding sensitive information during experimental investigations.
