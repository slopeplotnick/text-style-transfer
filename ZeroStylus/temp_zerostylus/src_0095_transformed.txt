The paper proposes a methodology for constructing an energy model that provides a distribution of energy consumption for statically typed programming languages executed on the JVM. The investigation focuses on the effects of factors such as primitive data types, data sizes, computational operations, and hardware devices on energy consumption. The evidence indicates two critical findings: firstly, devices with identical hardware configurations can have disparate levels of energy consumption; secondly, this variability poses challenges for the accurate prediction of overall energy usage. The paper is organized as follows: Section 2 offers background information on Bayesian statistics, a framework for making probabilistic inferences using prior knowledge and observed data. Section 3 introduces our methodology, while Section 4 elaborates on its practical implementation. In Section 5, the evaluation methodology is presented, including any issues encountered during the analysis. The results of this evaluation are detailed in Section 6, with a subsequent discussion in Section 7. Related work is examined in Section 8, and the paper concludes in Section 9. Bayesian statistics serves as a methodological framework that utilizes prior information to formulate probability statements regarding unobserved phenomena. Although this objective aligns broadly with frequentist statistics, which is prevalent in computer science, the two frameworks differ fundamentally. Frequentist statistics aims to construct models exclusively from observed data, minimizing assumptions. In contrast, Bayesian statistics explicitly incorporates assumptions, allowing for their scrutiny and validation. We employ Bayesian statistics for two main reasons: firstly, it compels us to articulate our assumptions clearly, thereby enhancing modularity and explainability; secondly, it enables the prediction of energy consumption through posterior distributions, offering a more detailed comparison than the single value estimates typical of frequentist methods. Building on prior research, we model energy usage as following a normal distribution, allowing us to define its range and examine its statistical properties. Subsequently, this range is refined using a Bayesian inference algorithm, which aligns the data more closely with empirical measurements and facilitates a more accurate representation of the system state. However, limited data availability can magnify the influence of initial assumptions, potentially leading to suboptimal outcomes. This consideration is crucial, as it allows for the correction of initial assumptions and improves the model's alignment with empirical data.
