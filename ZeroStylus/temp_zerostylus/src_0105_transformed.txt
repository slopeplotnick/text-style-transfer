Psychometric scales and questionnaires are indispensable tools in quantitative research across various fields, including psychology, medicine and health sciences, social sciences, education, and human-computer interaction studies. When a novel construct or theoretical framework is introduced and becomes the focus of empirical research, developing corresponding psychometric tools is essential. However, this development process is resource-intensive, necessitating careful planning, significant time, materials, and substantial funding (Stefana et al., 2025). In this study, we investigate the potential of reducing resource demands in the scale development process through in silico prototyping of psychometric scales, utilizing simulated psychometric data from representative samples generated by Large Language Models (LLMs). In the following sections, we will first elucidate the rationale and hypotheses underpinning this research, followed by a comprehensive description of four studies designed to empirically test this method's feasibility. Finally, we will discuss the results and provide recommendations for future research endeavors. Obtaining a representative participant sample is costly and time-intensive, leading researchers to often rely on convenience samples of volunteering university students, which significantly limits the external validity of the findings (Hanel & Vione, 2016; Wild et al., 2022). Recently, online platforms like Prolific.co and Amazon MTurk have become prevalent in participant recruitment for psychometric validation studies (Buhrmester et al., 2018; Douglas et al., 2023; Huang et al., 2015). These platforms, contingent upon available funding to compensate participants, have notably reduced recruitment time and may enhance external validity by enabling the assembly of representative samples for specific nationalities, such as those in the UK and US. However, ethical concerns have arisen regarding online studies recruiting participants from these platforms, as they may include individuals from disadvantaged backgrounds (Huang et al., 2015; Huff & Tingley, 2015; Moss et al., 2023). Furthermore, when exploratory studies investigate constructs lacking validated scales, often due to their novelty, or when conducting a validation study is impractical, researchers typically resort to ad hoc questionnaires. These scales, grounded solely in theoretical frameworks, allow researchers to circumvent the expenses associated with scale development. Nevertheless, this approach compromises the validity of the findings, which frequently fail to generalize beyond the study sample (M. K. Lee et al., 2020).
