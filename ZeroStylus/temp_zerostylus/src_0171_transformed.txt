The MODOMA system offers a computational laboratory framework for studying language acquisition, simulating real-world learning processes. While the mechanisms through which MODOMA mirrors these processes remain to be elucidated, the system provides a robust foundation for investigating intricate aspects of grammar acquisition. Both adult and child agents function as integral components of the system, interacting dynamically within its framework. Moreover, the system is fully parameterized, allowing researchers to modify various settings while transparently displaying the grammar rules it acquires. This transparency facilitates the straightforward evaluation of the linguistic knowledge and processing capabilities exhibited by the system. Crucially, our study validates the feasibility of employing a multi-agent computational setup to model first language acquisition. However, the specific mechanisms by which these agents enhance the acquisition process remain an open question, necessitating further investigation. An important aspect of this research is the application of Zipf's law as a fundamental principle for assessing the system’s efficacy. However, while Zipf's law serves as an essential evaluative tool, its specific contributions to system performance remain underexplored. The experiments conducted in this study incorporate a hybrid methodology combining statistical analyses to identify distinct word categories, including function and content words. These experiments integrate all components necessary for modeling more complex acquisition processes, thus providing a solid foundation for future research initiatives. For example, subsequent studies could explore the impact of varying environmental parameters on the grammatical acquisition of the child agent, the communicative output it generates, and its interaction with the adult agent. Relevant parameters for investigation include the frequency with which the lexical acquisition process is executed and whether previously acquired lexical categories should be updated in response to new data. Additionally, the role of feedback from the adult agent in shaping the language acquisition and communicative development of the child agent represents a critical avenue for further research. The findings of this study also open new opportunities for exploring advanced learning methodologies within this framework. For instance, the MODOMA system could be extended to model the acquisition of additional and more complex grammatical phenomena. Shakouri et al. (2025) demonstrate that grammatical categories such as nouns, verbs, and prepositions can be acquired under conditions similar to those used in function and content word experiments. Their approach employs sophisticated statistical techniques, such as hierarchical agglomerative clustering, to autonomously identify discrete grammatical categories without explicit instruction. Thus, these results represent a significant contribution to the field, providing a foundational basis for the continued exploration and refinement of computational models of language acquisition.
