Question Formulation. Following the categorization detailed in Section 2.1, eight graduate students with expertise in specific domains identified a selection of categories and independently generated 20 prompts for each. Each of these prompts was crafted to necessitate engagement with at least two scientific ideas, thus presenting a considerable challenge. The initiative drew inspiration from examples of public educational experiments, aiming to ensure their scientific validity and adaptability to a concise video format of 10 seconds or less. Furthermore, two graduate students developed a set of 40 I2V questions, each accompanied by a video demonstrating the experimental procedure as intended. These videos were sourced from datasets and online platforms, with a key frame from the onset serving as the primary image to illustrate the entire experimental setup. Panel Review and Quality Check. Once the complete test set had been finalized, the graduate student team convened to conduct a comprehensive review of all materials. Prior to finalizing their respective sets of prompts, each participant undertook a thorough review of their peers' 20 contributions to ensure specificity, accuracy in procedural steps, and clarity in intended outcomes. In addition, for the purpose of conducting a final quality evaluation, video-generation systems, namely Sora-2 and Wan-2.5-T2V-Preview, were employed to generate the initial segments of each prompt, thereby ensuring that the critical components of the experimental procedures were adequately represented. VLM-as-a-Judge. Recent investigations have demonstrated that robust VLMs are capable of generating human-aligned judgments when appropriately prompted and configured. In the VideoScience-Bench framework, we implement a VLM-as-a-Judge configuration to examine the disparity in performance between visual language models that emphasize reasoning and those that do not, such as GPT-4o. Notably, prompting VLMs, particularly those lacking reasoning capabilities, to evaluate video creations frequently leads to insufficiently justified evaluations and occasional false-positive judgments. Consequently, this approach employs a quantitative self-evaluation framework utilizing a checklist-based grading methodology, wherein each assigned score is substantiated by key video frames and a corresponding evidence table derived from video analysis.
