The cognitive framework for parsing user queries and item attributes necessitates an initial infusion of foundational domain knowledge to interpret query intent and item-specific details effectively. Building upon this knowledge, the system conceptualizes user intent as a process of discerning salient item attributes and aligning them with user requirements. In certain instances, visual representations of items encapsulate intricate details, such as their appearance or texture, which are challenging to articulate solely through textual descriptions. Therefore, this approach integrates information from item images, wherein the visual data is contextualized alongside textual features to enhance understanding. By combining both types of information, the system constructs a more comprehensive and nuanced understanding of the item's characteristics. To validate and align item attributes with user requirements, the system adheres to predefined rules for detail verification and matching. At this juncture, it systematically applies these principles to ensure coherence and functionality, facilitating an objective and consistent operational framework. The subsequent phase of the process involves knowledge acquisition and reasoning. It is essential to distinguish between general world knowledge and specific e-commerce knowledge, as the latter is crucial for understanding diverse expressions and phrases. The extensive training of large language models endows them with a comprehensive repository of general knowledge, enabling them to grasp common concepts and relationships effectively. However, the realm of e-commerce encompasses a multitude of phrases and terminologies that prove challenging to interpret based solely on their intrinsic semantic content. The challenges are predominantly categorized into two distinct types: Firstly, rare queries represent highly specific inquiries regarding atypical items, wherein models frequently lack the requisite background knowledge to accurately associate the terminology with its intended meaning. For instance, when the query term is "strange things are also loved by someone," and the product title is "novel and interesting toys," the search pertains to a book title. This lack of prior knowledge undermines the system's ability to align the query and item accurately. Secondly, in e-commerce environments, there exist brand-specific terminology, product nicknames, and marketing language that are challenging to interpret based solely on their standalone semantic content. Conceptual ambiguities also arise with certain items that are so rare that the system lacks sufficient conceptual understanding of them. Consequently, these contents require supplementary domain-specific e-commerce knowledge for accurate interpretation.

The term "lying gate" cannot be fully comprehended or classified solely based on its nomenclature without additional context regarding its nature. Therefore, within e-commerce scenarios, supplementary domain-specific knowledge becomes essential to accurately interpret and resolve these cases. Moreover, higher-level semantic understanding and domain knowledge must interact cohesively to address these challenges effectively. Treating the problem as pure semantic parsing is insufficient. For instance, domain-specific jargon, shop/brand taxonomies, model coding rules, and category-specific descriptions of functions or effects, such as mouthfeel descriptions for tea or wine, exemplify the complexity of these needs. Additionally, users may articulate their needs through misspellings, aliases, homophones, or industry slang, necessitating a robust integration of general and domain knowledge. Consequently, the model must possess strong capabilities in spelling correction, variant normalization, and alias alignment. Intent recognition, or entity-relation recognition, heavily depends on reasoning. Its objective is to ascertain whether the query seeks items themselves or item-related information, such as reviews, comparisons, or usage guides, and to differentiate the main target entities from modifiers or constraints. This process relies significantly on reasoning at both methodological and implementation levels. Accurately identifying the purchase purpose and item usage is pivotal in locating the true target of the need, distinguishing whether the query describes derived attributes or directly refers to specific items. For example, in "charger/headphones for iPhone 17," the actual target items are the charger or headphones, while "iPhone 17" imposes compatibility or model constraints, representing a compositional need over functionality/effect and model. Furthermore, abstract needs that do not point to specific items but to categories or domains, such as "phones with the best battery life," pose a ranking or filtering request over the entire item space rather than specifying a concrete item. The system must precisely capture the semantic meaning of the derived attribute "battery life" within the phone domain and its operational measurement, constructing reasonable ranking or filtering rules to support inference and comparison over candidate sets. Finally, ambiguity arising from missing context must be mitigated, as users frequently omit constraints or preferences within a single query. Therefore, the system must exploit dialogue history, user profiles, and default preferences to comprehend the user's intent comprehensively.
