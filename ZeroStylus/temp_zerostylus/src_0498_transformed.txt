The anonymity afforded by digital platforms often diminishes social inhibitions and exacerbates anger; moreover, collective dynamics can intensify disputes. Consequently, while computational systems can identify inappropriate conduct online, they predominantly respond by removing content or penalizing individuals, rather than addressing the root causes of the issue. As a result, online platforms can temporarily suppress harmful discourse but fail to address the underlying social dynamics, allowing such hostility to reemerge. Rather than simply moderating interactions, we propose exploring whether adopting mediation can address conflicts more effectively. Efforts are directed toward assessing whether a language model can effectively track the progression of a conflict and contribute to its de-escalation. We explore the potential for these models to comprehend dialogues, assess the fairness of arguments, and provide responses that de-escalate tensions and enhance the constructiveness of discussions. To achieve this, we must engage in reflective analysis concerning the current dynamics, emotional responses, the progression of deterioration, and prevailing communication patterns. To this end, we take a decisive step toward dividing the task into two distinct components: judgment and steering. The judgment component involves identifying elements of unfairness or factors that cause discontent among individuals, while the steering component focuses on articulating responses that facilitate de-escalation and encourage dialogue centered on underlying issues. To evaluate the potential efficacy of these models, we constructed an extensive dataset comprising real-world online conflicts sourced from Reddit and implemented a systematic framework for their assessment. Our examination assesses three key aspects: firstly, the adherence of the model to established conversational norms; secondly, the behavioral response of individuals post-intervention to ascertain the model's efficacy; and thirdly, the comparative analysis of the model's assistance against human input. The implications of these findings on model performance are yet to be fully explored. Through extensive testing, we observed that paid models outperform free ones in both identifying and de-escalating conflicts, demonstrating greater adherence to the discussion topic. As illustrated in prior studies, the models effectively mitigate hostile language and heightened emotions in simulated conflicts. Nevertheless, while they exhibit strong neutrality and clarity, their readability and level of engagement remain less comparable to that of human counterparts.
