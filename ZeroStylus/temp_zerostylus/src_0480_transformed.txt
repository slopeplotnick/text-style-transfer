Software Product Lines (SPLs) have emerged as a widely recognized method for the efficient development of highly configurable software systems tailored to meet diverse requirements. An SPL can be conceptualized as a software supply chain comprising various components. Specifically, variability models delineate potential product features and guide their permissible combinations within product configurations. Furthermore, an asset base provides the tangible software components necessary for implementing these features. The tools within an SPL facilitate the selection of features to configure a specific system and subsequently enable the construction of the final product by assembling components from the available repository. This approach is particularly advantageous when a considerable number of configurations are required. However, companies frequently encounter challenges when initiating a plan for a novel product type. To address this, many firms initially establish a collection of related software systems, which aids in transitioning their development processes to a product line framework. Despite documented instances of successful transformations in the literature, the evolution of an existing software family into a unified product line platform remains complex, and organizations may exhibit reluctance to undertake such a conversion. This hesitancy often stems from the absence of explicit procedures and uncertainty regarding the cost-benefit ratio. Consequently, although organizations possess substantial amounts of well-documented code, they continue to manually develop custom applications from the foundational codebase to satisfy specific client needs, often employing a clone-and-own strategy. Our partner enterprise, ITK - Predict and Decide, exemplifies a small to medium-sized enterprise with a workforce of 120 individuals, including 19 agronomists, 29 developers, 2 UI/UX designers, and 4 system administrators, thus ensuring a balanced integration of specialized expertise essential for operational efficacy. Notably, approximately one-third of the employees specialize in technology-related roles. The company employs an agile methodology in its system development and delivery processes, which is perceived as effective. Developers utilize advanced programming languages to create applications that assist farmers in decision-making by enabling the prediction of plant and animal diseases as well as crop outcomes. The agronomists develop the core simulators that form the foundation of these applications. Subsequently, IT teams develop additional code that collects data from IoT devices or online services to feed the simulators and display their output data in dashboards. The levels of the Architecture Dimension, as described in the FEF framework [vdLSR07], address the technical methodologies for constructing software and dictate the technical realization of products within the software product line. At Level 1, architectures are confined to individual systems, lacking an evident focus on reuse. Transitioning to Level 2, there is a movement towards the reuse of third-party infrastructure, with defined common software components such as middleware; however, there is no formal integration of domain-specific assets. At Level 3, domain commonality is captured and implemented into a software platform, providing a reference architecture for all applications, primarily centered around platform utilization. This configurable platform, however, does not support handling configuration variability. Progressing to Level 4, both domain commonality and variability are systematically addressed, with a reference architecture specified to encompass the entire software product line. Domain assets are designed to support product derivation, with explicit attention to variability management within the software product line architecture. Finally, at Level 5, the reference architecture assumes a dominant role, with application architectures only marginally diverging from it. Automated product derivation becomes feasible through scripts, tools, and high-level languages, while application development primarily revolves around configuration within the reference architecture's boundaries, thereby enabling the possibility of automated product configuration.
