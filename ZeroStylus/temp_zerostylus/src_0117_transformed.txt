The emergence of multi-agent autonomy is facilitated by the integration of large language models (LLMs) with orchestration layers, shared memory mechanisms, and external tools. Frameworks such as AutoGen [11] and CAMEL [7] exemplify this by enabling role-based dialogues—planner, critic, and executor—within a unified operational environment. Furthermore, more advanced systems, including SWE-agent [12] and Voyager [10], extend these dynamics through continuous learning loops, where agents iteratively generate tasks, evaluate outcomes, and adapt their strategies. Consequently, the resulting systems are not static pipelines but rather adaptive ecologies, wherein the output of one model reshapes the context for subsequent interactions, creating a feedback loop that accumulates over time. While human oversight remains nominally present, it becomes increasingly delayed, indirect, and constrained in practice. As the scale of multi-agent systems expands, traditional assumptions of behavioral observability and interpretability begin to deteriorate. Specifically, communication within these systems often lacks explicit structure, appearing in forms such as free-form natural language, JSON payloads, or API calls that lack semantic guarantees. This unstructured communication introduces fragility in meaning, as interpretation becomes endogenous to the network itself. As a result, traceability degrades not due to missing data but rather due to the inherent instability of interpretation. Consequently, technical methodologies have been proposed to address this growing opacity. For example, multi-agent sandboxes now facilitate the logging of interaction graphs and the computation of metrics such as divergence, semantic coherence, influence centrality, and consensus rate, all of which contribute to characterizing system-wide behavior. Similarly, the Inspect framework, developed by the UK AI Security Institute [9], enhances message traceability by appending provenance metadata to communications, thereby enabling the post hoc reconstruction of causal chains. Together, these advancements signal a transition from merely verifying outputs to auditing the flow of information through network topologies. This shift involves moving beyond evaluating whether a model violates predefined rules to examining how information propagates within multi-agent systems. Moreover, as these systems scale, the conceptualization of autonomy itself evolves. In supervised settings, autonomy has traditionally been defined as the capacity to act independently of explicit human directives. However, in multi-agent ecosystems, autonomy takes on a relational dimension, characterized by agents dynamically coordinating, competing, and inferring each other's intentions. Notably, even when agents are initialized with identical objectives, minor asymmetries in incentives, environmental conditions, or context windows can lead to significant outcomes, such as polarization, cooperative manipulation, or runaway escalation [1]. Thus, autonomy transitions from being a property of individual components to an emergent feature of the network as a whole.
