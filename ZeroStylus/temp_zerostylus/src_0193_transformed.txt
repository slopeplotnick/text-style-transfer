The issue cannot be observed when examining a single model; however, it arises from the configuration and interaction dynamics, ultimately diminishing safety, reliability, or accountability at the system level. To elucidate the origins of this issue, three initial indicators can be identified. These indicators serve as a preliminary framework rather than an exhaustive set, and additional aspects may emerge upon further testing. The first consideration involves the manner in which agents communicate and exert influence on one another. Notably, the cognitive opacity presents a challenge, where discerning their thoughts or communications becomes difficult. Moreover, these differences encapsulate the extent to which their objectives begin to diverge. Collectively, these factors facilitate the anticipation of the timing and manifestation of group risks. While each element can be quantified and evaluated independently in controlled environments (see §3.1), experimental setups involving multiple interacting agents enable systematic testing and validation of their collective behavior (see §3.5). The dynamics of information propagation through the network underscore its critical significance. Tightly interconnected systems enable rapid collaboration, yet simultaneously amplify the propagation of errors across the network. Conversely, the limitation arises from a lack of connectivity, which mitigates the propagation of errors but concurrently impedes collaborative resolution efforts. Furthermore, the organization of information influences the extent to which a single error propagates into widely accepted beliefs. This enables the assessment of factors such as the rate at which errors propagate and the proportion of agents adopting risky behaviors, thereby facilitating a deeper understanding of these dynamics. In systems characterized by multiple interacting agents, the traditional paradigm of evaluating safety must account for the configuration of information. Cognitive opacity refers to the extent to which an agent's cognitive processes remain unseen or incomprehensible. This occurs either inadvertently or intentionally when the pursuit of specific objectives conceals genuine cognitive processes. Consequently, one model may accept the assertions of another without scrutiny, even in instances of inaccuracy. Finally, the intent-opacity rate refers to the extent to which the reasoning and underlying logic behind outputs become inaccessible or untraceable, providing a crucial metric for evaluating transparency.
