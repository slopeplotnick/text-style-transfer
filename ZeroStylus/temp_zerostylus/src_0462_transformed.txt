Language Model Systems (LMS), encompassing Large Language Models (LLMs) [Brown et al., 2020; Gemini Team, 2024; Liu et al., 2023, 2024; Touvron et al., 2023], Vision-Language Models (VLMs) [Liu et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2023], and agentic systems, exhibit considerable versatility across various applications. Notable examples include domain-specific chatbots, such as medical conversational agents, autonomous driving assistants, and programming support tools [Liang and Tong, 2025; Zhang et al., 2024; Zheng et al., 2024]. Given their deployment in safety-critical and user-facing contexts [Nie et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2025b], ensuring the correctness of these systems and their alignment with human expectations of appropriate behavior is essential. Consequently, substantial research efforts have been dedicated to identifying desirable safety properties and incorporating them into LMS frameworks [Anwar et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2023]. However, the current state of the art in evaluating and enforcing these behaviors remains inadequate, as it lacks the ability to provide generalizable methodologies with formal guarantees of effectiveness. These formal guarantees are indispensable for fostering user safety and cultivating trust [Russell et al., 2016; Singh and Chawla, 2025; United Nations Scientific Advisory Board, 2025]. In response to this need, recent studies have introduced the first formal certification approaches for LMS, targeting specific desirable behaviors such as fairness, accurate question answering, and operational safety, which are encoded as formal mathematical specifications. These specifications constrain LMS input scenarios and ensure that their outputs adhere to the desired behaviors. For example, [Chaudhary et al., 2024] proposed counterfactual fairness specifications for LLM responses, while [Chaudhary et al., 2025] developed specifications for reading comprehension across general knowledge and medical domains. Similarly, [Wang et al., 2025a] introduced specifications addressing catastrophic risks in multi-turn conversations with LLMs, and [Yeon et al., 2025] provided specifications for correct tool usage by agentic systems. Despite these advancements, existing certification frameworks are predominantly domain-specific and tailored to particular properties, which limits their adaptability to diverse requirements or modalities. This rigidity arises because the desirable behaviors are often defined informally or, at best, algorithmically, with a primary focus on conversational applications. Consequently, even minor modifications to the specifications necessitate substantial reformulation. Therefore, the formal certification of LMS is currently approached in a bespoke manner for each property, domain, and system type, a limitation that this study seeks to address.
