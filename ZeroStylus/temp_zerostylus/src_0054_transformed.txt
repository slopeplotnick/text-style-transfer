The system known as Udio possesses the ability to transform prompts expressed in natural language into complete musical compositions spanning various genres and languages, inclusive of lyrical content. Within the realm of AI-generated music perception, research in computational creativity and machine learning has scrutinized the creativity of these models through listener evaluations of audio samples. Specifically, the assessment of music produced by these models, relative to other models or human-composed music, often considers technical and musical attributes, originality, and the capacity to evoke emotions. Moreover, evaluators frequently examine music for its technical quality, originality, and particular musical characteristics, such as melody, harmony, and rhythm. Furthermore, the degree to which the music exhibits naturalness or human-like qualities is frequently evaluated. Participants assess compositions by employing rating scales or a musical Turing Test, whereby they ascertain whether a piece was created by a human or an AI, with varying levels of awareness or intentional misinformation regarding the originator. In particular, evaluations of AI-generated Bach chorales have revealed that early models such as Deepbach achieved results indistinguishable in technical and musical quality from genuine human compositions. The emotional aspect of AI-generated music is often assessed by listeners who categorize intended emotions as "happy" or "sad" or employ models that quantify emotion dimensions. Additionally, researchers have explored whether perceptions of AI-generated music fluctuate based on individuals' knowledge or beliefs about its creator. Initial investigations that manipulated individuals' awareness of the composers revealed no significant bias in evaluations of live recordings of string-quartet compositions by humans versus computers; however, the findings remain inconsistent. For instance, Zlatkov et al. reported no discernible bias against AI-generated pop music samples. Furthermore, Moura and Maw's inquiry into consumer marketing demonstrated that individuals do not exhibit a distinct preference for music based on its origin, whether classical or pop, irrespective of the composer.
