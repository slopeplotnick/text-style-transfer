Agentic systems are akin to contemporary software architectures: they comprise integrated components, present interfaces, and are configured within software workflows. Unlike conventional programs, probabilistic programming languages employ stochastic and adaptable methodologies for problem-solving. Therefore, evaluations of these systems often hinge on their ultimate success or failure, thereby overlooking the processes of cognition, strategy formulation, action implementation, and method adaptation that occur over time. To address this limitation, we formally introduce GRAPHECTORY, a framework designed to delineate the temporal and semantic interconnections within these systems, facilitating the development of methodologies for assessing and analyzing their efficacy beyond mere success evaluation. Utilizing GRAPHECTORY, we systematically examine 4000 pathways across two primary agentic programming frameworks, SWE-agent and OpenHands, leveraging four prominent Large Language Models (LLMs) to address SWE-bench Verified issues. Our findings reveal several insights: Firstly, agents equipped with more effective prompts or advanced language models exhibit increased GRAPHECTORY complexity, indicative of enhanced cognitive processes, broader contextual comprehension, and meticulous verification prior to submitting corrections. Secondly, the methodologies employed by agents in problem-solving evolve in response to the difficulty of the problem and the language model deployed. Successfully resolved issues typically adhere to a sequence of identifying, rectifying, and verifying, whereas unresolved issues manifest as disorganized, repetitive, or regressive actions. However, despite their efficacy, agentic programming systems frequently experience protracted operations, resulting in unnecessarily extended procedural pathways. Nevertheless, agentic systems utilizing large language models (LLMs) represent a promising approach to addressing complex, multi-step tasks across various domains, including software engineering, web browsing, virtual assistance, scientific discovery, and robotic navigation. These systems are contemporary software architectures, comprising components such as LLMs, tools, APIs, and memory, and featuring inputs, internal states, operational mechanisms, and outputs. This underscores the importance of dynamic pathway development—comprising sequences of cognition, action, and observation—that collectively establish the rationale for task completion.

The primary focus in evaluating agentic software systems is on the outcomes they produce, particularly whether their methodologies can effectively address a given problem. This evaluation necessitates a detailed examination of the system's processes and methodologies. For instance, file editing tools such as create, str_replace, and insert are utilized to develop or alter a test file for reproducing an issue (Localization), an application file for addressing a bug (Patching), or a test file for validating a generated patch (Validation). These tools are employed during debugging to either localize bugs or validate patches. Agents typically use Python to execute newly generated reproduction or regression tests, or pytest for executing existing regression tests. It is important to note that depending on the context, the same tool or command can serve multiple purposes and be assigned to different phases. In the context of the GRAPHECTORY of the SWE-agent Dev, potential inefficiencies in agent reasoning or tool usage are indicated by self-back edge 9 and back edge 10, which may result in repeated failed edits or necessitate refining the localization plan (as discussed in §1). GRAPHECTORY facilitates the computation of process-centric metrics for analyzing agentic systems. Table 1 delineates six metrics supported by the current GRAPHECTORY implementation, along with formal definitions illustrating their computation. These metrics quantify the effort an agent expends to resolve a task. While their standalone values do not inherently indicate the usefulness of the effort, they offer insights into how well the effort aligns with the problem's difficulty and whether agents successfully solve these problems. Each metric's significance is explained further: Temporal Edge Count (TEC) measures the total number of temporal transitions between actions. A higher TEC suggests a longer execution path to termination. As shown in Figure 2, SWE-agent Dev exhibits a longer execution chain (14) compared to SWE-agent DSK-V3 (8). Thus, similar to the Node Count (NC), TEC values are positively correlated with problem difficulty, aligning the overall GRAPHENCY complexity with problem difficulty. Moreover, Loop Count (LC) quantifies the frequency with which the agent repeats a previously executed action. Larger LC values, as demonstrated later (§3.2), may indicate a non-optimized strategy, causing the agent to become ensnared by unsuccessful actions, such as the self-loop of edge 9 in Figure 2-a, or necessitating refinement of reasoning and planning after actions fail to solve the problem, exemplified by back edge 10 in Figure 2-a (§3.3.2). Alternatively, the agent may need to re-execute a series of actions to gradually solve a complex problem. LC is akin to Cyclomatic Complexity in classic software, which measures branching and unique execution paths. In GRAPHENCY, branching implies that local reasoning redirects the execution flow to a different path. Additionally, Average Loop Length (ALL) measures the average number of actions performed before an agent repeats a previously executed action. Intuitively, the longer it takes for an agent to realize its reasoning and actions are ineffective, the weaker its reasoning or ability to gather proper contexts for reasoning. Our results indicate that agents investing in richer context collection during localization are less likely to encounter long loops, enabling them to rapidly identify reasoning inefficiencies and adjust their strategies accordingly (§3.2.1 and §3.3.2).
