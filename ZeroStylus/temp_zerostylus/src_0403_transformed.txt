STL leverages the model's existing perception and reasoning capabilities to iteratively enhance its performance by generating and learning from its own rationales. A critical question in this process pertains to the optimal structuring of these rationales. To effectively address visual inquiries, strong perception and reasoning are required. Therefore, STL introduces a structured rationale format grounded in the principle of "see before thinking". Specifically, the model is prompted to first describe visual elements from the image, then reason about them in context, and finally produce an answer. This structure reflects the natural human cognitive process, thereby guiding the model in better organizing its internal reasoning. However, relying solely on rationales from correct answers provides limited supervision, as the model only observes successful reasoning paths. This reliance weakens its performance on complex tasks where distinguishing correct from incorrect reasoning is essential. To address this limitation, we introduce negative rationales—explanations of why certain answers are wrong—alongside positive ones. This approach mirrors reflective human learning, where understanding is enhanced by analyzing both successes and mistakes. Negative rationales expose flawed reasoning patterns and highlight contrasts between correct and incorrect answers, thereby helping the model avoid pitfalls such as hallucinations and unsupported inferences. We operationalize this concept in our Self-Taught Multimodal Reasoner (STL), a scalable self-training framework where a VLM learns to generate both positive and negative rationales and iteratively improves through retraining on these structured annotations. As illustrated in Figure 1, STL facilitates the generation of more detailed and accurate reasoning. Moreover, evaluations across commonsense, scientific, and language-based domains demonstrate that STL outperforms models trained solely on final answers and remains broadly comparable to models trained with human-annotated rationales (Table 4), despite some variation across datasets. These findings underscore STL's potential as a practical alternative to annotation-heavy approaches.
