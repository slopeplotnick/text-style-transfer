Hallucinations in large language models (LLMs) typically arise from fabrications, as described in [16]. In certain cases, LLMs exhibit the ability to monitor their internal processes to assess whether they are generating unreliable information. This capability suggests the potential for developing self-aware AI systems that can identify and mitigate instances of disseminating erroneous outputs. Furthermore, [22] proposes the use of unlabeled text generated by LLMs, as opposed to traditional labeled datasets, to improve model adaptability and efficiency. This approach supports the creation of hallucination detection models, ultimately simplifying and expediting the training process by reducing the reliance on extensive manual annotation. Additionally, [12] underscores the importance of establishing robust question-answering systems grounded in extensive research, which serve to automatically reduce the propagation of false information by ensuring accuracy and reliability. Such advancements are particularly significant for end-users of LLMs, as these systems possess generative capabilities and engage interactively with users. Collectively, these studies focus on identifying strategies to mitigate hallucinations in text generation, thereby enhancing the credibility and trustworthiness of content produced by LLMs. To address these challenges, techniques such as zero-resource detection and self-assessment have been identified as effective methods for combating hallucinations, given the difficulties posed by their unpredictable nature. Understanding the relationship between LLMs and hallucinations is critical, as it informs the selection of models that demonstrate superior capacity for managing this phenomenon. Notably, the variability in hallucination levels across different LLMs is often attributable to variations in model architecture and training methodologies. For instance, [1] offers a comprehensive analysis of hallucinations in language models, highlighting the discomfort experienced by models when generating illogical or false outputs. This discussion emphasizes the importance of investigating the underlying causes and classifications of hallucinations, including the fabrication of facts and nonsensical content. Similarly, [5] attributes the generation of hallucinations to factors such as the inherent size and complexity of training datasets, which occasionally lead to the presentation of inaccurate information or the fabrication of non-existent content.
