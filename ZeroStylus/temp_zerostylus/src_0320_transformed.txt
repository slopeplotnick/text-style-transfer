Enhanced taxonomic frameworks recognize that practical deployment scenarios often involve robots with specialized capabilities designed to execute complex tasks. However, construction environments present unique challenges for multi-robot task allocation (MRTA), distinguishing themselves from controlled settings where traditional approaches have historically proven effective. Specifically, construction sites are characterized by unstructured layouts, dynamic material flows, weather dependencies, and complex interdependencies among trades (Yu et al., 2013). Moreover, these environments demand allocation frameworks capable of addressing spatial constraints, adhering to temporal dependencies, and ensuring safety-critical operations (Garcia de Soto et al., 2023; Chakraa et al., 2023). In addition, construction processes require human oversight mechanisms that cannot be predetermined or easily encoded into mathematical formulations, further complicating the operational landscape. Consequently, these construction-specific challenges necessitate the development of adaptive coordination approaches capable of responding to dynamic constraints and unstructured environments. Classical optimization methods have long served as the foundation for multi-robot coordination strategies, offering mathematical rigor and performance guarantees through a variety of computational techniques. Kuhn (1955) established the theoretical basis for optimal assignment problems through the seminal Hungarian method, which demonstrated the efficient resolution of bipartite task-resource matching via linear programming with polynomial time complexity guarantees. However, the Hungarian method is limited to simple one-to-one assignments and cannot accommodate heterogeneous robot capabilities or task dependencies. In construction contexts, robotic systems are often equipped with specialized capabilities—such as heavy lifting or precision assembly—that necessitate the coordination of task sequences with explicit dependencies. These limitations spurred the development of more sophisticated optimization approaches. Atay and Bayazit (2006) advanced the field by introducing mixed-integer linear programming (MILP) formulations for multi-robot task allocation, which incorporate constraints related to robot capabilities, task precedence, and communication limitations. Military and aerospace applications further refined MILP-based coordination by addressing scenarios with heterogeneous robot capabilities and stringent timing constraints (Darrah et al., 2005; Schumacher et al., 2004). The use of MILP offers significant advantages in modeling complex multi-robot scenarios, ensuring explicit satisfaction of intricate constraints. Nevertheless, MILP approaches rely on centralized optimization architectures, which face scalability challenges when coordinating large teams of robots. This is especially problematic in construction projects, where multiple robots operate across expansive, unstructured sites with limited communication infrastructure. Therefore, the scalability limitations of centralized optimization have motivated the exploration of distributed coordination approaches better suited to the demands of such environments.

Inherent challenges with scalability in centralized optimization systems, particularly those employed in mixed-integer linear programming (MILP) methodologies, become evident when coordinating large teams of robots. This underscores the necessity for an equitable distribution of computational resources to maintain long-term system efficacy. Construction environments, specifically, require this equitable distribution of robotic resources due to the expansive and disordered nature of sites, which also have restricted communication capabilities. Here, sustained system efficiency hinges on effective coordination, given the dispersion of numerous robots. Consequently, these scalability challenges have necessitated the development of distributed coordination methods.
