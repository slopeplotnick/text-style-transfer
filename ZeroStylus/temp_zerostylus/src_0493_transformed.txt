Current evaluations of video models predominantly focus on basic physical principles, targeting routine scenarios that necessitate a fundamental understanding of physical mechanics. However, these benchmarks provide limited insight into the capacity of video models to engage in scientific reasoning. To address this gap, we introduce *VideoScience-Bench*, a benchmark designed to evaluate video models' abilities to comprehend and reason about scientific concepts. Each test scenario is constructed to require an understanding of multiple scientific domains, demanding an undergraduate-level proficiency, and incorporates 103 topics spanning physics and chemistry. This benchmark enables the assessment of whether video models can discern scientific setups and predict outcomes without external guidance, a crucial step toward advancing predictive modeling grounded in scientific understanding. The evaluation framework focuses on five critical dimensions: alignment with the given prompt, accurate prediction of expected outcomes, effective handling of movement, adherence to invariant scientific rules, and the overall coherence of the generated outputs. To facilitate this process, we propose *VideoScience-Judge*, a systematic evaluation tool that employs a checklist-based methodology. This framework systematically generates evaluation criteria, extracts key video frames, and identifies pivotal moments where significant events occur, thereby enabling a detailed analysis and deeper understanding of the physical phenomena represented in the videos. The resulting scores are then synthesized into a single metric that reflects the model's proficiency in scientific reasoning. Moreover, recent findings indicate that evaluations conducted using *VideoScience-Judge* align more closely with expert assessments compared to other benchmarks that focus primarily on elementary physical knowledge or the visual realism of video representations. Notably, this study also evaluates the ability of video models to simultaneously process multiple scientific concepts embedded within video scenarios. Our contributions are threefold. First, the development of *VideoScience-Bench* demonstrates the feasibility of creating high-quality benchmarks that facilitate video models in engaging with scientific reasoning, even in the absence of additional external assistance. Furthermore, the inclusion of authentic, real-world examples drawn from 103 physics and chemistry topics ensures that the evaluation benchmarks are firmly grounded in genuine scientific contexts. Second, the creation of *VideoScience-Judge* introduces a robust evaluation framework that identifies key video frames and utilizes computational tools to detect critical elements, such as motion and object interactions. This system assigns scores that reflect both the scientific validity and the visual quality of the videos. Third, we conducted a comprehensive performance analysis using *VideoScience-Bench*, providing an in-depth evaluation of contemporary video models. Despite recent advances, where video models exhibit the ability to generate highly realistic and seamless videos, *VideoScience-Bench* continues to present substantial challenges for these models. This underscores the need for further research and development to enhance video models' capacities for scientific reasoning and accurate representation of complex phenomena.

Numerous models exhibit limitations in processing intricate scientific concepts and frequently fail to adhere to fundamental principles of physical laws. However, among the models evaluated, Sora-2 and Veo-3 demonstrated superior performance. They exhibited preliminary capabilities in scientific reasoning, which marks a significant development in the field. Therefore, while many models struggle to integrate complex scientific principles effectively, Sora-2 and Veo-3 offer promising insights into the potential for models to not only generate visually accurate outputs but also to engage in scientific reasoning. This advancement underscores the evolving expectations of video models to move beyond basic visual fidelity toward a more comprehensive understanding of scientific phenomena.
