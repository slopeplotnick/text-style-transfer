The transition to a Software Product Line (SPL) approach entails substantial organizational changes, particularly given that Software Product Management itself remains challenging, as evidenced by the survey conducted by Springer and Miler [SM22]. In adopting an SPL, teams increasingly become cross-functional, thereby collaborating on a shared set of core assets that are reusable across multiple projects. This shift, therefore, allows for optimized resource utilization, shorter time-to-market, and a concentrated focus on innovation. Moreover, the emphasis on component reusability, configuration-based customization, and unified documentation collectively streamlines processes and enhances efficiency. Although this transition necessitates careful planning and change management, it ultimately fosters a culture of collaboration, efficiency, and adaptability within the organization. In the context of ITK, valuable insights into outcomes and guidelines were obtained through discussions with the development team. They expressed a desire for comprehensive involvement and information, prompting us to maintain continuous collaboration and progression alongside them throughout the migration process. These findings at ITK can be contextualized with several reports on SPL adoption, strategies, and experiences in the literature. Furthermore, ITK's strategic framework adheres to the IDEAL improvement model [McF96]. The project commenced with a collective determination to initiate the migration, during which we assessed the methodology and perception of project execution as follows. Subsequently, the next steps in ITK's adoption involve formulating a strategy and action plan, executing it, and, in alignment with typical agile methodologies, reflecting on both successful and unsuccessful outcomes to drive continuous improvement. Our guidelines serve as a crucial function in facilitating and directing the establishment of the action plan. However, the challenges encountered differed from those documented in prior reports, such as a lack of skilled personnel or insufficient management support, since these factors were not perceived as significant issues within the company's prevailing mindset, as noted in [NJ13].
