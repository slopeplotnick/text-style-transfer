Legal and ethical concerns surrounding generative models are intensifying, particularly with regard to the incorporation of copyrighted material in their training processes. At the core of these concerns is the issue of authorship and moral rights, which involves determining the rightful creator and the associated entitlements. Claims have arisen asserting that the unauthorized use of copyrighted content infringes upon established property rights. For instance, the New York Times has initiated legal proceedings against OpenAI for the unapproved use of its news archives, while Getty Images has similarly taken legal action against Stability AI for generating content derived from its proprietary database. In addition to legal actions, artists are increasingly vocalizing their concerns. Notably, Elton John, Dua Lipa, and approximately 200 other artists expressed their apprehensions in a letter, urging technology companies to safeguard human creativity against artificial intelligence capable of mimicking their vocal characteristics and artistic styles without consent. Consequently, several organizations have proposed new regulations and guidelines to be considered. For example, the European Union's AI Act emphasizes transparency in the deployment and use of AI systems. Meanwhile, the United Kingdom and UNESCO have established frameworks for ethical AI governance prioritizing privacy and intellectual property protection. In recent developments, Denmark has proposed an amendment to its copyright legislation aimed at conferring individuals with proprietary rights over their voice, face, and bodily likeness, marking a novel legal precedent within the European context. Such modifications intend to address the legal complexities arising from the replication of personal characteristics by AI systems. The Danish culture minister underscores the importance of safeguarding personal identity, emphasizing the need to protect individual rights to bodily autonomy, vocal identity, and facial representation against the encroachments of generative AI. Despite these advancements, most generative models remain opaque, complicating efforts to discern the influence of training data on their outputs. Furthermore, the replication of open-source models is constrained by their substantial size, rendering exact duplication impractical and resource-intensive. To investigate the necessity of accessing internal mechanisms, we evaluated current methods such as influence functions and retraining-based attribution.
