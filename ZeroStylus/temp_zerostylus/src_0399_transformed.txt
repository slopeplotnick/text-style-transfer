In the context of unsupervised fraud detection within multi-type journal entry data, the challenge of identifying fraudulent activity without labeled instances underscores the necessity for sophisticated detection methodologies. The scarcity and inaccessibility of labels render supervised learning techniques impractical on a large scale. Consequently, it becomes essential to identify anomalous entries by modeling irregular patterns across both structured and unstructured fields, thereby reflecting real-world auditing conditions. The simulated accounting dataset developed by Gronewald et al. [2024] serves as an exemplary reproduction of the financial record-keeping practices characteristic of a medium-sized enterprise. Each journal entry within this dataset is defined by features including a unique posting identifier, the date and time of both the posting and transaction, credit or debit classification, tax rate, and associated account identifier. Moreover, our methodology is evaluated against real-world anonymized accounting data obtained from an industry partner. This dataset, however, lacks the conventional associations between debit and credit entries typical of standard double-entry bookkeeping systems due to its anonymized nature, thus necessitating a transaction-level approach to fraud detection. To mitigate the absence of specific contextual details, we enrich the large language model (LLM) prompts with bookkeeping heuristics, structured guidelines, and summary statistics, which proves instrumental in surpassing traditional machine learning baselines. Recognizing the importance of privacy when handling sensitive accounting data, we opted for open-source LLMs, such as LLaMa AI [2025] and Gemma Team [2024], rather than proprietary alternatives like OpenAI's GPT-4 [2024], to conduct our testing. Subsequently, we establish that our method outperforms baseline techniques when applied to both the synthetic dataset derived from Gronewald et al. [2024] and the real-world anonymized dataset. The official baselines, including methods such as Isolation Forest as introduced by Liu et al. [2008], are replicated due to their efficacy with tabular data and their suitability for unsupervised anomaly detection, aligning well with the objectives of this study.
