Stock price prediction remains a fundamental and persistent challenge in the field of quantitative finance. This difficulty arises primarily due to the pronounced volatility of stock price movements and their acute sensitivity to real-time information. These characteristics render traditional numerical prediction models ineffective in capturing unstructured textual signals, such as breaking news and market sentiment dynamics. Consequently, such methods often fail to identify the underlying factors driving these fluctuations. However, the rapid proliferation of online textual data, combined with significant advancements in the natural language processing capabilities of Large Language Models (LLMs), has catalyzed progress in text-based stock price forecasting. Despite these advancements, most existing research employing LLMs focuses on shallow text analysis and immediate predictions, thereby neglecting the incorporation of systematic knowledge integration and long-term memory capabilities. This limitation undermines their ability to adapt to the highly dynamic and multifaceted nature of financial markets. Moreover, the inherent complexity of financial markets exacerbates the inadequacy of general-purpose memory architectures. Price fluctuations are rarely attributable to isolated events; rather, they emerge from the sequential interplay of interrelated events over time. Thus, models must not only comprehend the immediate semantics of textual data but also retain, associate, and retrieve critical information across extended temporal horizons. Furthermore, the paradigm of predicting stock prices based on news articles is fundamentally distinct from conventional question-answering tasks. Unlike queries with explicit answers, the prediction of future stock trajectories (e.g., "tomorrow’s stock price movement") is not directly stated within the textual content. Therefore, it necessitates that models actively construct a dynamic understanding of market contexts by synthesizing heterogeneous information, identifying analogous historical patterns, and engaging in deep analogical reasoning. This shift from basic "text alignment" to genuine "market pattern discovery" is essential for advancing predictive accuracy. Additionally, financial texts are inherently noisy, often containing redundant or irrelevant information that can obscure critical signals with predictive value. As a result, sophisticated content filtering and matching techniques are required to extract pertinent information effectively. Addressing these challenges is imperative to developing robust models capable of navigating the complexities of financial markets and enhancing the accuracy of stock price prediction.
