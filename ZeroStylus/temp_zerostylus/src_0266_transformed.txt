Advanced reinforcement learning (RL) techniques have integrated domain randomization and reward shaping to address the gap between simulated environments and real-world applications. For instance, Tobin et al. (2017) demonstrated that domain randomization enables successful sim-to-real transfer for robotic tasks. Similarly, Grzes and Kudenko (2010) developed reward shaping approaches that enhance learning efficiency in multi-agent scenarios, allowing for accelerated adaptation to dynamic environments. These advancements have proven particularly beneficial in construction robotics, where robustness to environmental variability is crucial. However, these techniques still demand significant computational resources and meticulous hyperparameter tuning. In highly dynamic construction settings, where conditions frequently fluctuate, the associated computational overhead and tuning complexity become impractical for real-time coordination. Furthermore, despite their effectiveness, learned policies often suffer from limited interpretability, complicating both their validation and their application in safety-critical contexts. To address this, game-theoretic approaches have emerged as promising alternatives, offering sophisticated mathematical frameworks for modeling strategic interactions among autonomous agents. For example, Martin et al. (2023) employed game theory to develop a multi-robot task allocation framework, using Shapley values to quantify individual robot contributions. This approach demonstrated superior performance while enabling a formal analysis of equilibrium solutions and stability properties. Nevertheless, game-theoretic methods are not without limitations. Their reliance on assumptions of rational agent behavior often conflicts with the unpredictability of construction environments, where agents may be influenced by factors beyond purely logical considerations. Moreover, defining precise utility functions for complex construction tasks remains a significant challenge. Despite these advances, learning-based and AI-driven approaches continue to face critical limitations in construction coordination scenarios that require explainable decision-making processes. The inherent black-box nature of deep learning and game-theoretic models hinders the clear understanding of coordination decisions, as highlighted by You et al. (2023). This lack of transparency necessitates human oversight in construction management, where decision rationales must be thoroughly understood and validated to ensure safety and effective project execution. These challenges have motivated the exploration of Large Language Model (LLM)-based coordination approaches, which offer a transformative paradigm for multi-robot coordination. LLMs demonstrate capabilities in natural language understanding, contextual reasoning, and adaptive decision-making. Unlike optimization-based methods that require complete problem specifications, LLM-based approaches facilitate intuitive human-robot interactions and address the explainability limitations of traditional learning-based methods by providing transparent, natural language reasoning. Consequently, they hold significant potential for advancing coordination in complex and dynamic construction environments.

The integration of Large Language Models (LLMs) into robotic systems reveals their capacity to comprehend language, reason within contextual frameworks, and adapt their decision-making processes based on evolving information. However, their impact on enabling collaborative functionality across multiple robots introduces significant shifts in coordination dynamics. In contrast to traditional approaches that necessitate comprehensive problem specifications, LLMs offer a more intuitive mode of human-robot interaction, thereby enhancing the clarity of decision-making processes. This shift could potentially streamline the collaborative efforts among robotic entities, promoting more efficient and effective task execution.
