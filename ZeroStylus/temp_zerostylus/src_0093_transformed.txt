A growing body of research aims to elucidate the mechanisms underlying diffusion models, particularly focusing on how these models navigate the trade-off between memorization (i.e., the tendency to replicate training samples) and generalization (i.e., the ability to generate novel outputs). Earlier studies have approached this phenomenon by analyzing model behaviors and investigating the factors that drive their tendencies toward either memorization or novel generation. Specifically, much of the existing work examines model performance when sampling from an initial state and evaluates these hypotheses on smaller-scale datasets such as FFHQ and CIFAR-10. While these insights are informative in low-data regimes, the likelihood of memorization diminishes as dataset sizes scale up to levels such as ImageNet. Song et al. observed that, for large-scale datasets, initiating the sampling process from an earlier stage tends to produce novel outputs, whereas commencing from a later stage is more likely to replicate training samples. This raises a critical question: what underlying principles regulate the balance between memorization and generalization in models trained on extensive datasets? To address this, we analyze the training objectives of such models to gain deeper insights into their underlying mechanisms. In particular, flow matching (FM) and its gradient-equivalent counterpart, conditional flow matching (CFM), highlight the challenges associated with these approaches, as the unavailability of ground-truth velocity fields renders FM intractable. However, under a Gaussian prior and a finite training set, the marginal velocity field of rectified flow admits a closed-form expression, allowing for the computation of the oracle velocity (defined by the FM objective) at any location in the sample space. This enables the identification of distinct stages in the training process. In the early stage, closer to the prior (referred to as the navigation stage), the oracle velocity incorporates information from multiple data points, guiding the model toward a mixture of relevant data modes. Conversely, in the later stage (termed the refinement stage), the velocity field becomes dominated by a single data point. Furthermore, the timing of this transition is influenced by two key factors: data dimensionality and sample size, which collectively determine the trajectory of the model over time.
