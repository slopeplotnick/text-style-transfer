When performing a series of measurements, such as planning the locations for drilling, artificial intelligence (AI) techniques, including Monte Carlo tree search, can be employed to identify the optimal sequential drilling strategy (Mern and Caers, 2023). However, the properties of interest evolve throughout the process, necessitating a hierarchical approach: first, drilling to test hypotheses; next, assessing the volume of the mineral system; and finally, evaluating the grade. Once all but one hypothesis has been falsified, attention should shift primarily to the volume of the mineral system, followed by its grade. Alternatively, these factors can be combined into a grade-tonnage curve, depending on the desired approach. Despite the potential of a rigorous scientific method to enhance the efficiency and cost-effectiveness of exploration, several non-scientific barriers persist within the industry. These include entrenched practices and organizational structures, the broader organization of the exploration industry, and the absence of comprehensive software solutions. In many companies, organizational frameworks assign specialized experts to specific tasks aligned with their training and expertise. Consequently, these individuals often focus on intricate details without fully considering whether those details significantly influence the final decision-making process. To leverage the full potential of AI, a fundamental reorganization of large corporations is required, shifting from a task-oriented mindset to one centered around solving decision-making problems. This transition entails not only structural changes but also educational efforts. While geologists do not need to become data scientists, fostering a shared understanding between disciplines is critical—a topic that will be revisited in the education section. Moreover, socialization around a single model, often facilitated by software systems, perpetuates biased and deterministic interpretations. Over time, these interpretations are attributed a misplaced sense of truth. As personnel move on, the rationale underlying the original interpretations is frequently forgotten, leading to a perception of the resultant model geometry as objective data rather than a conceptual framework. Within many organizations, uncertainty is often regarded as a failure, and a lack of knowledge is mistakenly attributed to individual shortcomings. This perspective assumes that greater effort and time investment would naturally reduce uncertainty, overlooking the inherent complexity and limitations of the problem at hand.
