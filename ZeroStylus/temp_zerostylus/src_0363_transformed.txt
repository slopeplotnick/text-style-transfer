AMP exhibits commendable proficiency in replicating motions; however, it necessitates retaining reference motion data during the training phase. In contrast, SMP demonstrates the capability to replicate a diverse range of motions, including complex ones, with an imitation accuracy and sample efficiency comparable to AMP, while notably eliminating the need for reference data during training. Moreover, SMP consistently surpasses the performance of both SMILING and AMP-Frozen across all evaluated skills. AMP-Frozen attempts to remove AMP's dependency on reference motion data by utilizing a pre-trained discriminator; however, this approach proves inadequate, resulting in severely degraded motion imitation. Although DeepMimic benefits from explicit phase synchronization, thereby reducing tracking errors for certain motions, SMP still performs competitively. Furthermore, when attempting more complex maneuvers such as spinkicks and cartwheels, DeepMimic, particularly when pose termination is disabled, frequently converges to suboptimal solutions instead of successfully executing the motions. Our system distinguishes itself by being among the first to employ score-matching in the construction of a reusable motion model that achieves effectiveness comparable to conventional adversarial imitation learning techniques. This section elucidates the stabilizing effects of ensemble score-matching on training procedures and the consequent enhancement of outcomes. For example, when assessing ensemble score-matching, the technique, which averages several SDS evaluations across set diffusion timesteps, demonstrates improved outcomes compared to traditional methods relying on SDS evaluation at a singular, random timestep. The comparison evaluates single-clip imitation tasks of varying difficulty, including Run, Cartwheel, and Backflip. As demonstrated in Table 5, policies relying on a single timestep of SDS evaluation perform adequately with straightforward motions, yet they tend to encounter difficulties with more complex ones. For instance, when attempting to replicate a backflip, policies trained with a single SDS evaluation frequently result in stationary standing or falling backward rather than performing a complete flip. Conversely, training policies for complex motions such as acrobatics benefits from using multiple SDS evaluations over fixed timesteps, as this approach provides a more consistent reward signal compared to relying on a single evaluation.
