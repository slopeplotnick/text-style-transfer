To improve our understanding of the trends observed in the accuracy and false positive rate (FPR) results, we analyze verifier gain, as presented in the final row. Verifier gain quantifies the expected benefit of employing a verifier during rejection sampling, which involves repeated sampling until the verifier accepts a solution. This measure provides a clearer perspective on verification performance: self-verification yields the smallest gains, and more accurate solvers do not consistently exhibit greater self-improvement. However, gains increase slightly in intra-family verification and are most pronounced in cross-family verification, which offers the greatest potential benefits. To further investigate verifier performance, we assess its effectiveness across different model families and sizes. In Figure 3, we extend the experiments from Figure 2 by plotting each verifier metric as a function of model size within each model family. From this analysis, we observe that both verification accuracy and the false negative rate (FNR) improve steadily as model size increases. In contrast, the false positive rate behaves unpredictably, often increasing with model size—an effect particularly evident in intra-family verification for DeepSeek. Notably, for state-of-the-art post-trained models, such as Qwen3 and DeepSeek, verifier gain is most substantial in the cross-family setting, less significant in the intra-family setting, and negligible during self-verification. At first glance, these findings appear to challenge the conclusions of Song et al. (2025), who report that self-verification GV-Gaps expand with greater pretraining FLOPs. However, a closer examination reveals that their analysis predominantly focuses on older model families. Consistent with this, Figure 2 highlights that earlier models, such as Qwen2.5 and Llama3, exhibit larger verifier gains compared to newer models. We hypothesize that the limited gains observed for advanced post-trained models, such as DeepSeek and Qwen3, in self-verification or intra-family verification can be attributed to two factors. First, these models may already engage in implicit self-verification when used as solvers, thereby reducing the incremental benefit of an explicit verification phase. Second, their distributions are significantly refined through post-training (Huang et al., 2024a), which constrains the efficacy of rejection sampling in improving their performance.
