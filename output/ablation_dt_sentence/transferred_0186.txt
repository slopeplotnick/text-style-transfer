To overcome these limitations, model-agnostic methods have been proposed to compare generated outputs with training data in a shared embedding space, typically using features from pre-trained encoders. While these approaches are effective and scalable, they rely on latent-space analyses and vector representations that are inherently difficult to interpret. Recent work, such as LatentExplainer, seeks to address this challenge by perturbing latent variables and leveraging language models to describe their semantic effects. However, these methods still operate within an opaque space, limiting transparency for end-users. In contrast to latent or parametric approaches, we utilize automatically generated Knowledge Graphs (KGs) to trace the influence of training data on downstream behaviors. By comparing generated and training images via shared triples that capture objects, attributes, and stylistic elements, we enable scalable, interpretable, and human-aligned attribution. Furthermore, Large Language Models (LLMs) have become central to modern KG workflows, enabling structured reasoning over unstructured input data. Their contributions span three primary areas: (1) generating subject-predicate-object triples from natural text, (2) assisting in ontology creation, and (3) translating natural language inquiries into graph queries. For triple extraction, prompt-based methods like AutoKG can convert textual descriptions into ontology-aligned triples. Beyond extraction, LLMs also facilitate ontology learning and extension, using techniques such as prompt engineering, ontology reuse, and reasoning over specific concepts. In question answering, LLMs function as semantic parsers, generating SPARQL queries from user input, a process that is further enhanced by incorporating contextual KGs or using controlled natural languages to reduce ambiguity. Recent surveys on generative KG construction highlight a shift from traditional pipeline architectures to unified, LLM-driven approaches, which outperform traditional pipelines in entity recognition, relation extraction, and graph population. These advancements provide a promising foundation for addressing data attribution in generative models. Instead of relying on opaque embeddings or inaccessible parameters, structured outputs from multimodal LLMs can be used to build interpretable KGs that encapsulate key elements of both generated and training data while tracing their influences. Although most work so far has focused on text, these methods show considerable potential for extension to images, despite the added complexity of visual content.
