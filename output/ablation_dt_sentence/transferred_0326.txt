A journal entry, in accounting terms, represents the fundamental record of a financial transaction, typically involving both a debit and a credit entry across one or more accounts. Each entry is characterized by a set of attributes—including transaction date, posting ID, currency, responsible user, tax rates, amounts, and, where applicable, a free-text description. These heterogeneous features—spanning numerical, categorical, and textual data—make journal entries a natural testbed for evaluating anomaly detection systems in multi-type data environments. Both synthetic and anonymized datasets retain a shared feature space encompassing these core attributes, thereby enabling consistent model development and evaluation across different accounting domains. In response, researchers have increasingly adopted machine learning (ML) methods to enhance the detection of anomalies in accounting data. Unsupervised techniques, such as clustering and Isolation Forest, can identify entries that deviate from expected transactional patterns Herreros-Martinez et al. [2025]. Notably, recent work on synthetic general ledger datasets has shown that such methods can outperform traditional rule-based approaches Gronewald et al. [2024]. However, conventional ML models often operate strictly on structured inputs and may struggle to capture the contextual nuances that distinguish malicious anomalies from harmless outliers. In contrast, the advent of Large Language Models (LLMs), such as GPT-4, has introduced promising new methodologies for auditing. These models exhibit advanced capabilities in natural language understanding and reasoning, positioning them as potential AI co-pilots in financial analysis tasks Gu et al. [2023]. Moreover, LLMs have recently been explored for anomaly detection as well—for instance, SigLLM for time-series data Alnegheimish et al. [2024] and AnoLLM for tabular formats Tsai et al. [2025]—demonstrating strong performance with minimal feature engineering. That said, applying LLMs to high-stakes tasks like auditing requires careful consideration. By design, these models can generate outputs that are inconsistent, biased, or fabricated. To address this, researchers have begun to develop oversight tools, such as AuditLLM Amirizaniani et al. [2024a] and LLMAuditor Amirizaniani et al. [2024b], aimed at verifying and contextualizing LLM outputs. These efforts underscore the need for robust governance when deploying LLMs as decision-support systems in critical domains like financial auditing.
