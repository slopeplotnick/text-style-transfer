We will begin by discussing the early foundational contributions of Bayesian and n-gram-based language models, which were instrumental in establishing probabilistic frameworks for speech recognition. By effectively modeling word sequence patterns and addressing the inherent uncertainties in spoken language, these models laid the groundwork for the systems in use today. Building on these foundations, we will next explore the development of contemporary end-to-end speech architectures that incorporate contextual information directly within the modeling pipeline. These models go beyond linguistic content to capture paralinguistic features such as prosody, emotion, and speaker characteristics, thereby enabling more nuanced and expressive speech understanding and generation. Furthermore, we will examine the role of large language models (LLMs) in post-ASR error correction, where they are employed to identify and rectify recognition errors produced by conventional ASR systems. Particular emphasis will be placed on issues of fairness and bias, especially as they relate to speaker variability—including accent, dialect, and sociolect—and their impact on system performance. We will highlight recent advancements aimed at mitigating these challenges and discuss the ethical imperatives for ensuring equitable outcomes across diverse user populations. In addition, we will review theoretical frameworks for adapting LLMs to speech input, focusing on population risk assessment and model transferability across demographic groups, which inform various spoken agent design pipelines (Radhakrishnan et al., 2023; Hu et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2023). Building on this, we will investigate joint text-speech training methods that enhance multimodal understanding by aligning learning objectives across both modalities. Finally, we will consider the application of LLMs to voice quality estimation, demonstrating how these models can assess and adapt to speaker-specific characteristics in real time.
