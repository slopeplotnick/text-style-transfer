To ensure data quality, the survey incorporated an attention check similar to the procedure used by Shank et al. (2023), asking participants, "Who composed the music you just heard?" before the music rating. Participants were then asked to rate their honesty and attentiveness on a 5-point scale, as well as to indicate whether they had listened using headphones or speakers. They were informed that their responses would not affect the feedback or compensation provided. An overview of the study design is presented in Figure 1. All participants were asked to rate their familiarity with the music excerpt and their enjoyment on a scale from 1 (not at all) to 5 (very). If participants rated their familiarity as 4 or 5, they were presented with an additional question asking whether the music elicited associations with a specific individual or experience. Participants in the "AI or human" condition were further asked whether they believed the music was generated by AI or a human, and to rate their confidence in this attribution on a 6-point scale ranging from 1 (definitely AI) to 6 (definitely human). Emotional reactions to the music excerpts were assessed using the Geneva Emotion Music Scale (GEMS; Zentner et al., 2008), which includes nine emotion dimensions categorized into three main factors: (1) Sublimity (Wonder, Tenderness, and Nostalgia), (2) Vitality (Joy and Power), and (3) Unease (Sadness and Tension). A condensed version of the GEMS was used, featuring a single label for each emotion along with a select set of associated terms. Participants provided ratings for each emotion using visual analog scales from 0 to 100, with the initial slider positions set to 0. If no selection was made, the value remained at 0. Additionally, participants could include up to three additional emotions not captured by the GEMS, along with corresponding intensity ratings in a text box.
