Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly employed in applications such as Google's AI Overviews, where they condense multiple lengthy documents into succinct summaries. However, it remains unclear whether all inputs are treated equally. To explore this, researchers focused on abortion-related news, constructing 40 triplets of articles—each consisting of a pro, a neutral, and a con article. These triplets were permuted into six distinct orders, and Gemini 2.5 Flash was prompted to generate a neutral summary based on each permutation. The generated summaries were then compared to the original articles using various evaluation metrics, including lexical overlap, semantic similarity, and factual consistency. These metrics assessed the degree to which the summaries preserved the original content in terms of vocabulary, meaning, and factual accuracy. The results revealed a systematic bias toward the first article in the input sequence, as indicated by higher similarity scores under the BERTScore evaluation metric. Specifically, the initial article exerted a stronger influence on the summary than the second and third articles, which had comparable effects. This suggests that the first document may disproportionately shape the generated summary, which could pose risks for systems that rely on these summaries, as well as for AI systems that base decisions on them. The rapid development of generative AI, a core component of such systems, has already facilitated the deployment of agentic AI across various sectors. These systems are designed to autonomously manage complex tasks with minimal oversight, demonstrating considerable operational independence. By decomposing larger objectives into smaller, more manageable tasks, they enhance the likelihood of successful completion. To achieve this, agentic AI systems engage in systematic planning, considering multiple stages and incorporating external information as needed to inform their decision-making and guide subsequent actions. Ultimately, the success of these systems hinges on the ability of the underlying LLMs to accurately retrieve, organize, and integrate information from both provided instructions and external sources.
