Using scikit-learn, we trained baseline models including Majority, Random, n-gram SVM, Logistic Regression, Random Forest, and Decision Tree classifiers. Features were extracted using TF-IDF and count-vector representations. Baseline models were trained on CPU; however, all experiments involving transformer models were conducted in a GPU-enabled environment (Google Colab T4). The micro-averaged F1-score was primarily used as the evaluation metric. Additionally, accuracy, precision, and recall were calculated for a fair comparison. Our study evaluated a wide range of models—such as m-BERT cased, XLM-RoBERTa base, BanglaBERT, DistilBERT cased, and baseline models—aiming to classify hate speech within the BLP2025 dataset. Table 2 displays the classification report for subtask 1A. BanglaBERT achieved the highest accuracy of 0.71, outperforming all other models evaluated. Moreover, BanglaBERT surpassed other models with precision and recall scores of 0.70 and 0.71, respectively. The official scoring metric for shared task 1a was the micro-F1 score, with BanglaBERT achieving a metric of 0.71 in our study. The BERT multilingual base model (m-BERT) achieved an F1 score of 0.66, while XLM-RoBERTa base model attained a slightly higher F1 score of 0.67, both surpassing the baseline models. On the other hand, DistilBERT achieved an F1 score of 0.57, which fell short of the baseline methods SVM and LR. Among the baseline models, SVM produced the best result with an F1 score of 0.61, whereas the random baseline classifier exhibited the worst performance with an F1 score of 0.16. DistilBERT also underperformed with an F1 score of 0.57. Finally, among the baseline models, both SVM and LR demonstrated strong performance, achieving F1 scores of 0.63 and 0.62, respectively.
