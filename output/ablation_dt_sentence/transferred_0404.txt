Hate speech recognition in low-resource languages remains a challenging problem due to the lack of sufficient datasets, orthographic variation, and linguistic diversity. Bangla, spoken by over 230 million people in Bangladesh and India (West Bengal), is a language significantly underrepresented in computational resources, despite the increasing demand for automated social media moderation. In this study, we focus on Subtask 1A and Subtask 1B of the BLP 2025 Shared Task on hate speech detection. We replicate the official baselines (e.g., Majority, Random, Support Vector Machine) and introduce additional baseline methods such as Logistic Regression, Random Forest, and Decision Tree. Furthermore, we explore transformer-based models, including DistilBERT, BanglaBERT, m-BERT, and XLM-RoBERTa, for hate speech classification. All transformer-based models outperformed the baseline methods for the subtasks, with the exception of DistilBERT. Among the transformer-based models, BanglaBERT achieved the highest performance across both subtasks. Despite being smaller in size, BanglaBERT outperformed both m-BERT and XLM-RoBERTa, highlighting the critical role of language-specific pre-training. Our results underscore the need for leveraging pretrained language models tailored to low-resource languages like Bangla. The internet has experienced remarkable growth in recent decades, with social media platforms gaining widespread popularity globally. Enhanced internet access has facilitated the sharing of thoughts and information online, and social media usage continues to rise rapidly, showing no signs of slowing down. This pervasive presence of social media in public discourse, however, has also provided a platform for spreading hate speech. The rapid increase in harmful content, such as hate speech, has raised significant safety concerns within online spaces. Hate speech is defined as language that is harmful, offensive, or derogatory, targeting individuals or groups based on identity factors like religion, ethnicity, nationality, race, or gender. Consequently, automatic hate speech detection has become a crucial task within Natural Language Processing (NLP). While substantial progress has been made in detecting hate speech in high-resource languages like English, advancements in low-resource languages, including Bangla, remain limited. This gap is primarily due to the lack of high-quality annotated datasets and the complex linguistic features of the language.
