Recent advances in reasoning techniques have significantly improved the performance of large language models (LLMs), raising expectations for their ability to provide accurate, truthful, and reliable information. However, emerging evidence suggests that iterative reasoning may inadvertently promote belief entrenchment, rather than fostering truth-seeking behavior. In this study, we propose a systematic framework to evaluate belief entrenchment in LLM reasoning, drawing on the Martingale property from Bayesian statistics. This property implies that, under rational belief updating, the expected value of future beliefs should align with the current belief, meaning belief updates cannot be predicted from the current belief alone. To measure violations of this property, we introduce the unsupervised, regression-based Martingale Score, which signals a departure from the Bayesian ideal of updating beliefs based on new evidence. In open-ended problem domains, such as event forecasting, value-laden questions, and academic paper review, we observe widespread violations across models, reasoning paradigms, and system prompts. Specifically, future beliefs are often predictable from the model's current beliefs, a phenomenon we refer to as belief entrenchment. Through comprehensive experiments, we identify which models (e.g., GPT-4o), reasoning techniques (e.g., chain of thought), and domains (e.g., forecasting) are particularly susceptible to belief entrenchment. Moreover, we validate the Martingale Score by demonstrating its ability to predict ground-truth accuracy in problem domains where ground truth labels are available. This suggests that, while designed as an unsupervised metric that operates even in the absence of ground truth, the Martingale Score serves as a valuable proxy for assessing the truth-seeking capacity of LLM reasoning processes. Consider a stubborn individual who refuses to acknowledge their mistakes, or a king surrounded by sycophants. In their pursuit of truth, humans often become entrenched in their pre-existing beliefs, either due to their own confirmation bias or through external influences.
