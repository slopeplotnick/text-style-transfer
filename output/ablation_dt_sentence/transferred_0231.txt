The evaluation of outlines follows a principle-based approach, eliminating the need for direct comparison to human-produced examples. Since outlines are significantly shorter and primarily encode structural intent, comparing them to full surveys would offer limited utility and rigid constraints. Instead, we assess the outlines based on key criteria such as organization, logical progression, and clarity. This method aligns more closely with the essential elements of a well-constructed outline, emphasizing adherence to academic norms and structural coherence. We use the original survey title as the query to elicit a generated survey from each system. Given that all systems have access to external information through web retrieval, the evaluation ensures equitable data access across the systems. Three categories of automated writing systems are evaluated: general-purpose long-text generation systems, paper-writing systems designed for full manuscript development, and survey-generation systems tailored specifically for academic surveys. General long-text models demonstrate proficiency in language fluency and topic relevance; however, their surveys often lack depth, exhibiting weaknesses in originality, synthesis, and critical analysis. Paper-writing systems offer better structural control, yet their outputs tend to be rigid, relying on templates that limit analytical depth and reduce diversity of perspectives. Survey-generation systems outperform the others, showcasing superior organization, a more coherent integration of ideas, and a clearer distinction between factual information and subjective opinions. Their effectiveness is particularly pronounced in scientific and technical fields, where accurate information and structured organization are crucial. These findings underscore that high-quality survey generation requires more than general LLM capabilities; it demands specialized methodologies that prioritize literature grounding and conceptual coherence.
