Energy efficiency is a concern in various fields, such as mobile software design and software operating in data centers. Understanding and reducing the energy consumption of even simple software systems presents significant challenges, with modern language run-time systems incorporating dynamic optimizations that further complicate the issue. For instance, consider statically typed JVM-based programming languages like Java, Scala, or Kotlin, which rely on a platform-independent intermediate representation—Java bytecode—executed on a virtual machine. The energy consumption of such software depends on various factors, including the compiler optimizations, the Java Virtual Machine (JVM), the garbage collector, the operating system, and the target hardware. To truly grasp the energy impact of software design decisions, a software engineer must have a deep understanding of these abstraction layers and the specific characteristics of the intended execution platform(s). Previous research has explored techniques like workload-based energy profiling, in which developers execute software with predefined workloads in specialized environments that measure energy consumption and attribute it to specific code regions. However, this approach requires custom hardware, limiting its applicability in routine software engineering. While some hardware platforms expose performance counters for specific components, such as the CPU, few allow for the measurement of the total energy consumption of the entire platform. In response, the proposed model constructs a Gaussian distribution for each quadruple representing a piece of code, grouping its elements to assess their individual impacts on energy consumption, including the effects of operations, data types, data sizes, and devices. This model offers several advantages, such as explaining how changing data types in an algorithm can influence energy consumption. It focuses on predicting the worst-case energy consumption (WCEC) of individual code statements and employs the convolution of individual prediction distributions to estimate energy consumption for functions and entire programs. Distributions are used to represent WCEC because a single point estimate fails to capture the range of possible consumption values, especially considering stochastic variations in energy, such as those arising from temperature fluctuations (Johnson-Nyquist noise) or external interferences (electromagnetic interference). Moreover, the distributions preserve important statistical properties, such as the mean, skewness, and mode, while also allowing the calculation of metrics like entropy, cumulative distribution, and probability density functions—information that point estimates cannot provide. By utilizing WCEC estimates, this methodology ensures that software systems can meet energy specifications, which is crucial for battery-powered devices and can lead to significant cost savings in data centers. With this foundation, program analysis or software verification tools can adopt this approach to improve their energy consumption predictions.
