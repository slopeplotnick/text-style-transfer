The study utilized five dataset configurations: one with the complete set of foundational units, and four with individual components removed—namely events, relationships, entities, and the story outline. Both qualitative and quantitative metrics were employed for evaluation. Specifically, all fine-tuned models were assessed using the same test dataset consisting of 196 samples. For qualitative evaluation, we examined various aspects of the generated stories, including their functional utility and artistic appeal. To enable a consistent basis for comparison, we used the stories selected in the preference dataset (“Chosen”) as a baseline, allowing for a structured analysis of the similarity across outputs from different model variants. Therefore, we used similarity metrics to evaluate the effect of excluding each of the four foundational units. **Quantitative Evaluation.** We primarily measured the similarity between stories generated by different fine-tuned models—given the same input—and the original reference story. Each model generated three distinct stories per JSON input. The original text served as the benchmark, and similarity was computed using three metrics: ROUGE-L, BERTScore, and METEOR Score. ROUGE-L captures lexical similarity by computing the longest common subsequence (LCS) between texts. BERTScore assesses semantic similarity, reducing the limitations of purely surface-level comparisons. METEOR Score incorporates diverse matching strategies, offering greater flexibility and robustness in evaluation. As illustrated in Figure 7, the comparative performance of each configuration is presented relative to the Chosen baseline. The Full Units configuration exhibited the strongest performance in terms of content control, semantic consistency, and linguistic naturalness in story generation. **Participants & Task.** To evaluate the usability of TaleFrame, we re-invited the same 2 domain experts and 17 volunteers referenced in Section 3. To support their creative process and reduce potential inspiration barriers, we provided a diverse selection of images. Volunteers were encouraged to choose topics based on personal interest and use TaleFrame to generate narrative content. If the generated story did not meet their preferences, they were able to modify the attributes of the four foundational units to tailor the narrative. Overall, participants were required to interact with all the features offered by the TaleFrame system.
