In our threat model, the backdoor is probabilistically triggered by user interactions, rendering LeechHijack present from the outset. However, a more insidious variant of the attack is possible. In this case, the tool initially behaves benignly, with the attacker refraining from any malicious activity and simply allowing the tool to function as intended. Once the tool gains widespread adoption, the attacker can remotely activate the exploit. This delayed activation strategy, analogous to supply chain attacks [72], [73], significantly amplifies the attack’s impact while complicating attribution. Moreover, the attack's stealth can be enhanced through selective targeting. By analyzing user profiles and focusing only on users who frequently engage with the tool—particularly those whose behavior consistently passes security assessments—LeechHijack becomes even harder to detect during testing. In this paper, we define and formalize a previously underexplored class of threats, termed implicit toxicity, which leverages ostensibly legitimate tools to perform harmful actions using authorized capabilities. To exemplify this threat, we introduce LeechHijack, a mechanism for covert computation hijacking via routine invocations of seemingly benign tools. LeechHijack is compatible with any MCP tool provider and infiltrates a system without requiring elevated permissions, covertly repurposing the host's resources for unauthorized tasks. This reveals a systemic vulnerability in the MCP tool-sharing ecosystem, wherein malicious tools can compromise downstream systems without privilege escalation. We conducted extensive testing across diverse environments, confirming both the feasibility of LeechHijack and its resistance to current defensive measures. Furthermore, we explore targeted mitigation strategies to establish a foundational framework for future defenses. Our results underscore a critical security concern within LLM-integrated systems and the necessity of rigorous evaluation of third-party tools. All experiments in this study were performed in strict adherence to ethical and security protocols. The LeechHijack attack presented here serves solely as a proof of concept to illuminate latent vulnerabilities, not to endorse or facilitate malicious use. No live systems, platforms, or services were affected during this research. All testing occurred within secure, isolated environments, with no connections to production servers, ensuring the protection of sensitive data.
