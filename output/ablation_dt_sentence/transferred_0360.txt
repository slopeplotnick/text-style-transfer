A distinct body of research focuses on enhancing the content generation process. Methods such as Generator Refinement and Verification (Li et al., 2024), Self-Contrast (Zhang et al., 2024), and Step-Back Prompting (Zheng et al., 2023a) employ iterative techniques and diverse perspectives to optimize the output of generative models. Similarly, Mirror-Consistency (Li et al., 2024), Self-Contrast (Zhang et al., 2024), and Step-Back Prompting (Zheng et al., 2023a) utilize iterative reflection or varied perspectives to enhance the quality of the generated outputs. In contrast, Self-Check systematically identifies errors by progressing through each step. While these approaches aim to enhance the generator's performance, they often require additional time and computational resources. Our work, however, distinguishes itself by focusing on the integration of results: rather than attempting to correct the disordered outputs generated by the model, we retain them in their original form and apply a specialized layer to determine the most precise response. A critical consideration when using a language model as an evaluator for comparing two entities is whether to allow the model to declare both as equivalent or to constrain it to select one over the other. We first demonstrate that compelling the model to make a selection can induce biases. In particular, the decision to classify a result as a "tie" is highly contingent upon the configuration of the judge, which necessitates the development of a more effective method for addressing this issue. Allowing ties, by contrast, mitigates the introduction of unintended biases by avoiding arbitrary resolution of otherwise equivalent alternatives. Moreover, when language models are used as evaluative agents, they may exhibit systematic biases, including positional bias, where the relative ordering of candidate options influences the resulting judgment. In a controlled experiment, we modified the setup to allow the model to output the response "tie," thereby reducing positional bias for both models under evaluation. This highlights that incorporating a "tie" option is not merely a means of indicating equivalence but also serves as a crucial mechanism for promoting fairness in the evaluation process. However, tie decisions may exhibit inconsistencies across different instances. In tasks where the model is required to select from three options, the voting behavior can vary significantly depending on the configuration of the task. This variability further underscores the need for a more robust method of aggregating the results.
