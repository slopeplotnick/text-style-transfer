We propose SR-GRPO, which leverages stable rank as a dense reward signal in reinforcement learning, thereby eliminating the dependency on preference datasets or external verifiers. We demonstrate that SR-GRPO improves reasoning capabilities across multiple benchmarks, establishing intrinsic geometric signals as a robust foundation for aligning language models. Moreover, beyond reinforcement learning, we show that stable rank provides an effective zero-shot reward proxy for evaluating language model responses, seamlessly integrating into best-of-N decoding. In this context, we introduce stable rank (Rudelson & Vershynin, 2007) as an unsupervised quality signal derived from language model representations. This section defines the metric, explains its geometric significance, and validates its effectiveness as a reward proxy. The theoretical motivation arises from two distinct lines of research. The softmax bottleneck (Yang et al., 2018; Godey et al., 2024) suggests that expressive language modeling necessitates high-rank representations, with representation collapse being a well-documented issue in degraded generation (Gao et al., 2019). Stable rank directly quantifies this effective dimensionality, making it a natural candidate for quality assessment. We validate this connection both empirically and through correlation analysis, as detailed in Section 5. Furthermore, we evaluate stable rank as a zero-shot proxy for human preference by testing its ability to predict the preferred response within a pair. This assessment ensures that stable rank can serve as a reliable reward signal without model-specific training. For this purpose, we use RewardBench (Lambert et al., 2025), a benchmark consisting of 2,985 preference pairs across five categories: Chat, Chat-Hard, Safety, Code, and Math. For each pair, we compute the stable rank of both responses and predict the one with the higher stable rank as the preferred option. We compare this approach against three baseline methods: (1) Pointwise Scoring (Point.) (Kim et al., 2024), which prompts the language model to score each response on a 1-5 scale; (2) Pairwise Comparison (Pair.) (Kim et al., 2024), which prompts the language model to directly compare two responses; and (3) IPO (Garg et al., 2025), which uses implicit preference scores derived from Yes/No token probabilities. We evaluate five models: Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct (Yang et al., 2024), Qwen3-0.6B (Yang et al., 2025), Qwen3-8B (Yang et al., 2025), Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct (Dubey et al., 2024), and Phi-3.5-mini-Instruct (Abdin et al., 2024). For stable rank, we structure inputs as prompt-response pairs, utilizing each model's native chat template to extract final-layer outputs.
