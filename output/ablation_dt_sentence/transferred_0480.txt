Software Product Lines are a well-established methodology for efficiently developing highly-configurable software products. They can be conceptualized as a software supply chain composed of multiple components. Variability models outline the available product features and specify the methods for integrating these features within product configurations. Furthermore, an asset base encompasses the concrete software elements necessary for implementing these features. Tools within the software product line facilitate the selection of features to formulate a specific configuration and subsequently generate the finished product by assembling components from the repository. Such an approach is beneficial when numerous configurations are needed. However, devising strategic plans for initiating the development of a new product category is not straightforward for companies. Consequently, many enterprises initially develop a collection of related software systems before transitioning their software development infrastructure to a Software Product Line (SPL). Despite successful experiences documented in the literature, transitioning an existing software family into a unified SPL platform remains a challenging task, and organizations may hesitate to adopt this approach. This reluctance often stems from the absence of clearly defined procedures and uncertainty regarding the cost-benefit balance. Therefore, even when possessing extensive repositories of well-documented code, companies continue to manually construct customized applications from the base code to meet client-specific requirements, often employing a strategy known as clone-and-own. Our partner, ITK - Predict and Decide, is a small to medium-sized enterprise with 120 collaborators at the time of writing, including 19 agronomists and 29 developers, among whom are 2 UI/UX designers and 4 system administrators. Approximately one-third of the company's workforce is tech-oriented. The company employs an agile methodology in system development, resulting in a well-functioning development and delivery process. The developed applications assist farmers in decision-making by enabling them to predict diseases in crops and livestock, as well as forecast agricultural outcomes. These applications are grounded in a set of simulators built by agronomists, while information technology teams develop additional code to gather data from IoT devices or online services. This data is utilized to supply the simulators, displaying the output on dashboards. Kotlin is used to construct the software's back-end, whereas the front-end is developed using Angular, with Typescript being prominently employed.
