Drilling plans are proposed as a collaborative effort between experts, underpinned by metrics of uncertainty quantification. Each drilling plan is designed with a clear objective in mind—whether maximizing the intersection or the volume of the targeted intrusion—while being based on quantitative metrics that assess the optimality of the proposed plan. These modifications are not arbitrary; rather, they stem from a well-defined framework for understanding data, models, objectives, and decision-making, a framework that has evolved through a long tradition of scientific inquiry. In the following section, we will delve into this concept from the perspective of the philosophy of science, particularly focusing on two key philosophies: Falsification and Bayesianism. While humans engage in scientific inquiry, they also reflect on the methodologies used to conduct that inquiry. This dual process raises philosophical questions about the appropriate methodology for scientific inquiry. What constitutes a good scientific method? The practice of science has evolved over time, beginning with empirical observation and progressing to questions about the relationship between models and data. It also considers how human collaboration shapes the practice of science, as social interactions define the standards and norms of scientific inquiry—even those that may later be proven incorrect. Insiders may fail to recognize errors or epistemic shortcomings, as the scientific community often accepts them as conventional wisdom. Moreover, scientists may exhibit unwarranted confidence in the extent of their knowledge (Bond et al., 2007). Thomas Kuhn extensively studied these scientific organizations, categorizing them as "paradigms." He viewed paradigms as comprising specific theoretical assumptions, laws, methodologies, and applications collectively adopted by members of a scientific community, such as those found in the fields of evolution or plate tectonics.
