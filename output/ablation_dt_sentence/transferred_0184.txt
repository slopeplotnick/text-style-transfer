When examining these differences, it is important to approach their interpretation with caution, particularly in relation to prompt steerability. Detailed data is provided in Tables 8 and 9. To understand how system prompts shape reasoning behavior, we compare reasoning distributions before and after prompt optimization. As shown in Figure 9 and detailed in Table 10, a notable reduction in the overall frequency of reasoning components is observed after prompt optimization. This shift is primarily due to optimized system prompts being shorter and cleaner, with fewer redundant instructions, such as "answer like Shakespeare," which often inflate the length and diversity of the reasoning chain. Consequently, the proportion of "Others" decreases substantially, indicating that optimized prompts lead to more structured and interpretable model behavior. Furthermore, the frequency of reasoning components such as Logical reasoning and Reframing also changes dramatically, highlighting the sensitivity of these behaviors to prompt formulation. The effect of optimization, however, is highly task-dependent. For mathematical tasks, optimized prompts lead to a clear increase in both Calculation and Logical reasoning, which aligns well with the task's inherent demands. In contrast, these optimized prompts do not elicit the same reasoning components for other tasks. For moral reasoning tasks, we observe a sharp decline in the absolute frequency of nearly all reasoning components, although Logical reasoning continues to dominate relative to other behaviors. Moreover, certain components remain consistently rare across all settings; in particular, Backtracking and Backward chaining are infrequent, likely due to the absence of reflection mechanisms similar to those in reinforcement learning-based systems. Finally, we consider whether the structure of reasoning chains alone can predict prompt performance.
