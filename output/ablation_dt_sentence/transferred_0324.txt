Natural Language Processing (NLP) [1] was initially conceptualized by Swiss linguist Ferdinand de Saussure, who introduced the idea that language meaning arises from internal relationships and contrasts among signs. This insight laid the foundation for communication, which relies on shared linguistic structures. In 1950, Alan Turing further advanced the concept by proposing the idea of a "thinking machine," suggesting that a machine capable of engaging in communication with humans through a teleprinter could be considered to exhibit cognitive functions. Today, NLP plays a crucial role in understanding human language and generating contextually appropriate responses. This is particularly evident in intelligent assistants like Apple's Siri and Amazon's Alexa, which autonomously process user requests and provide personalized assistance. Large Language Models (LLMs) [2], which are advanced AI systems built using deep learning methods, are trained on vast datasets and are capable of generating text that closely mimics human language. These models, grounded in transformer architecture, are designed to capture intricate linguistic nuances and long-range dependencies within text, thereby enabling sophisticated capabilities such as machine translation, extended conversational interaction, and content generation. Moreover, LLMs have proven valuable not only in understanding human language but also in a wide range of research and industrial applications. A prominent example of LLM technology is OpenAI's ChatGPT [3], which has become widely integrated into everyday use. However, one challenge associated with LLMs is the phenomenon of hallucination [4], where the model generates plausible-sounding yet factually incorrect information. This occurs due to the model's reliance on learned patterns from training data, particularly when confronted with unfamiliar inputs. The consequences of hallucinations can vary, ranging from minor inaccuracies to more significant errors in sensitive areas like translation, software development, or autonomous systems. Therefore, addressing hallucinations is crucial to ensuring the reliability and safety of these models, especially in contexts where precision is vital. One effective solution to mitigate this issue is the use of fine-tuned models [5]. These models are pre-trained on general data but are then optimized for specific tasks through further training on smaller, domain-specific datasets. Fine-tuning involves adjusting the model's parameters to enhance its precision and generalization across tasks, leveraging the foundational language capabilities developed during the initial large-scale training.
