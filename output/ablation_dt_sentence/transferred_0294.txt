We propose a method to extract semantically rich triples from images using multimodal large language models, aligning them with domain-specific ontologies to construct interpretable knowledge graphs. Building on this, we introduce a graph-based analytical framework that compares model-generated and training images by constructing and evaluating their corresponding graph representations—thereby enabling interpretable assessments of visual similarity. We demonstrate the utility of our approach in scenarios involving ownership attribution, interpretability, and provenance tracking, highlighting how this form of reasoning can enhance traceability and accountability in generative modeling workflows. We evaluate our method on both locally trained models and large-scale generative models through a style-specific experiment centered on “Ghibli-style” image generation. This experiment is designed to assess the method’s capacity to identify stylistic influences, even in black-box settings where internal model parameters are inaccessible. Section 2 reviews related work on content-generating models and the construction of knowledge graphs using advanced language models. Section 3 introduces our methodology. Section 4 details the experimental setup and presents results from both local and large-scale model evaluations. Section 5 concludes with a summary of key findings and outlines directions for future work. As generative models continue to evolve, interpretability has become increasingly critical across technical, ethical, and policy domains. General-purpose methods such as t-SNE, LIME, and attention visualization offer valuable insights into model behavior; however, they fall short in addressing the provenance of training data—a growing concern in applications involving authorship and intellectual property. To address this, various attribution techniques have been developed. Early methods include influence functions [8], retraining-based attribution approaches [3], and Shapley value approximations [15]. While effective in well-understood settings like classification, these techniques are often impractical for large-scale models due to their reliance on gradients, retraining access, or high computational cost. Consequently, they are rarely applied in modern deep learning pipelines, particularly in the context of generative models with opaque internal mechanisms.
