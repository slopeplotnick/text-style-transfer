We present Menta, a fine-tuned SLM designed for multi-task mental health prediction from social media text, optimized for both accuracy and deployability. Menta employs a weighted-loss, LoRA-based multi-task training framework, specifically developed to address data imbalance and maximize generalizability across diverse clinical classification tasks. We demonstrate that Menta offers a compelling balance between predictive accuracy and computational efficiency, outperforming several SLMs and competing effectively with LLMs on six distinct mental health tasks. To facilitate practical application, we provide an open-source deployment pipeline and model implementation for on-device inference, ensuring privacy-preserving and resource-efficient mental health solutions. Early approaches to analyzing social media text for mental health relied on traditional machine learning and feature-engineered models. For example, Jiang et al. (2020) utilized contextualized word embeddings such as ELMo and BERT to detect mental health conditions in Reddit posts, showing that deep contextualized models significantly outperform traditional bag-of-words or static embeddings in the context of mental health classification. Similarly, Sarkar et al. (2022) developed a multi-task learning model that jointly predicts depression and anxiety from Reddit posts, demonstrating superior performance over single-task baselines by leveraging shared features across related conditions. Other studies have focused on scalable deep-learning screening tools for suicide risk, with potential clinical applications for early intervention, including CNN (Coppersmith et al., 2018), LSTM (Coppersmith et al., 2018), and SVM (Ji et al., 2018). More recently, LLMs have been adapted to this domain. A prior study employed ChatGPT for zero-shot classification of stress, depression, and suicidality on social media data, validating the potential of LLMs to perform mental health prediction without domain-specific training (Lamichhane, 2023). Another study focused specifically on depression detection from online text, confirming that fine-tuned LSSLMs achieve significant improvements over earlier state-of-the-art approaches (Shah et al., 2025). Moreover, the interpretability of LLMs in prediction tasks related to depressive symptoms (Bolegave & Bhattacharya, 2025; Chen & Lin, 2025; Belcastro et al., 2025) and emotional states (Yang et al., 2023) has been explored, with findings suggesting that prompt engineering incorporating emotional cues and few-shot examples enhances model performance.
