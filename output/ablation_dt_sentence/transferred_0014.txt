**Step 1: Knowledge Injection and Reasoning Foundation** Domain knowledge forms the cognitive foundation required to understand user queries and item attributes, making its integration at the initial stage essential. By establishing this knowledge base, the model can effectively interpret query intent, infer users' underlying needs, and extract relevant item attributes. This enables a deep understanding of both user requirements and item features. **Step 2: Multimodal Information Extraction** Visual representations of items often contain key features that are difficult to convey through text alone, such as design elements, textures, or material properties. Therefore, at this stage, the system incorporates item image data, integrating both textual and visual information to create a richer, more accurate representation of the item. By synthesizing knowledge across modalities, the model enhances its ability to construct comprehensive item profiles. **Step 3: Rule-Oriented Discrimination** Following the identification of user intent and the extraction of item attributes, the system must apply specific rules to verify and align the information. This step involves using these rules to ensure logical consistency and operational correctness, ensuring that the extracted data aligns with predefined standards. In Section 2.3, we categorized knowledge into two distinct types: general world knowledge and e-commerce-specific knowledge. While large language models have already internalized substantial factual information through pretraining, e-commerce scenarios often involve terms and expressions that defy simple semantic interpretation. These challenges can be broadly classified into two categories: **(1) Long-tail Queries**, which involve highly specific or uncommon items that models often lack sufficient knowledge to interpret. For example, a query like "strange things are also loved by someone" could refer to the title of a book, but without prior knowledge, the system might struggle to make the correct connection. Similarly, brand-specific terminology, product nicknames, and marketing jargon pose challenges that require domain expertise. **(2) Semantically Ambiguous Items**: Certain items are so rare or niche that the model cannot process them effectively based on their text alone. For instance, the term "lying gate" cannot be properly classified without additional context regarding its functional or categorical attributes. To address such cases, the system must integrate supplemental e-commerce-specific knowledge, ensuring accurate interpretation and classification.
