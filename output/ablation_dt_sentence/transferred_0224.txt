Proper software for rational decision-making remains underdeveloped. The primary goal of software companies is to create products that consumers desire, with users typically favoring software that meets their specific needs and functions as expected. However, rational decision-making, which relies on rigorous uncertainty quantification, often requires high-performance computing resources, as seen in the implementation of Monte Carlo sampling methods, and this comes at a significant cost. Small exploration companies are unlikely to invest tens of thousands of dollars in cloud computing systems, nor will they hire the specialized expertise needed to implement and maintain such infrastructures. Moreover, most exploration activities are conducted by junior mining companies, typically owning one or two prospects and employing a small number of geoscientists. These companies often secure funding by demonstrating limited success, such as hitting mineralization with a single borehole, and the pursuit of additional drilling funds from investors frequently takes precedence over a rigorous scientific approach to decision-making. Falsifying a geological hypothesis requires a long-term commitment to data collection, whereas junior mining companies focus primarily on short-term outcomes. Therefore, it is evident that new financial vehicles are necessary. Investing in a single drill hole at a time is akin to betting on a single stock. In contrast, the typical investment strategy in stocks involves building a diversified portfolio that balances risk and return. The mining industry should adopt a similar approach, investing across multiple prospects to better understand the trade-off between risk (uncertainty) and return (estimated present value). How this can be achieved, however, remains uncertain.
