This paper addresses the limitations of current safety mechanisms designed for human-model interaction and their failure to scale in environments where large language models (LLMs) interact with each other. Existing governance practices primarily focus on single-agent safety containment, employing prompts, fine-tuning, and moderation layers to constrain individual model behavior. These mechanisms operate under the assumption of a dyadic setting: one model responding to one user with stable oversight. However, with the rapid shift toward LLM-to-LLM ecosystems, where outputs are recursively reused as inputs across chains of agents, the dynamics of multi-model interaction remain largely ungoverned. In such systems, local compliance can aggregate into collective failure, even when each model is individually aligned. To address this, we propose a conceptual shift from model-level safety to system-level safety, introducing the framework of the Emergent Systemic Risk Horizon (ESRH) to formalize how instability arises from the structure of interactions, rather than from isolated misbehavior. This paper contributes (i) a theoretical account of collective risk in interacting LLMs, (ii) a taxonomy linking micro, meso, and macro-level failure modes, and (iii) a design proposal for Institutional AI, an architecture for embedding adaptive oversight within multi-agent systems. Current safety approaches assume a single model operating under direct human supervision, where undesired behavior is mitigated through prompt engineering, reinforcement learning from human feedback, or output moderation. While these techniques have led to measurable improvements in reliability and civility, they remain pointwise—they govern individual model responses to inputs, rather than addressing the behavior of multiple models operating together. As large models are increasingly deployed in tool-using, agentic, and multi-agent settings, this single-agent safety paradigm becomes increasingly inadequate. Even models that are individually well-aligned can collectively generate outcomes that no single instance was trained to avoid, through feedback amplification, imitation, or emergent coordination. Consequently, there is a growing gap between local alignment and systemic safety, where compliance at the component level fails to guarantee control at the network level.
