Verifier Gain is a novel metric devised to better assess the utility of verifiers, addressing the limitations of relying solely on verifier accuracy for test-time evaluations. To provide a more comprehensive perspective, Verifier Gain simulates the improvement a verifier can achieve during rejection sampling. We conducted empirical studies on rejection sampling with verifiers, demonstrating that this theoretical approach closely mirrors actual performance trends. Furthermore, we extensively compared performance improvements across self-verification, intra-family verification, and cross-family verification modalities. Our findings indicate that cross-family verification often yields the most significant benefits, particularly when contrasted with self-verification methods. This difference is linked to the similarities in solution distributions between solver and verifier; as these similarities increase, verifier gain decreases. Our study suggests that as models improve through larger scale, post-training, or enhanced accuracy, their effectiveness diminishes for self-verification but enhances for cross-family verification. Additionally, we investigated the verifiability of various tasks by examining whether easier tasks are inherently easier to verify and whether some tasks are more verifiable than others. Our findings reveal that verification accuracy generally correlates with solver accuracy, although self-verification offers little verifier gain across the board. Notably, tasks involving mathematical or logical reasoning consistently produce higher verifier gains. Verifiers can operate at different levels, either assessing the correctness of the final answer or evaluating intermediate reasoning steps. Recent developments in outcome-level self-verification include methods by Weng et al. (2023), Wu et al. (2024), and Jiang et al. (2024) that predict question parts based on solutions. To minimize hallucinations, Dhuliawala et al. (2024) introduced language models that fact-check their own outputs by generating fact-check questions. Other researchers have trained general-purpose outcome-level verifiers (Hosseini et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025; Cobbe et al., 2021) and value models (Yu et al., 2024), independently or together with solvers (Shen et al., 2021; Sareen et al., 2025). Moreover, process-level verification has focused on deductive verification methodologies.
