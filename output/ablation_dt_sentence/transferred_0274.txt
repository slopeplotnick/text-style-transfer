Micro-level disruptions arise in direct exchanges among a small number of agents, typically two to five, where early signs of systemic drift begin to manifest. These risks reveal the fragility of shared understanding and interpretability, even before large-scale coordination emerges within the network. While initially localized, these issues grow and propagate as interactions deepen, forming the basis for meso- and macro-level pathologies. Once a deviation becomes embedded in an agent’s representation, it is replicated and amplified with each subsequent exchange, resulting in a nonlinear increase in the number of distinct risk events. At the meso level, risks emerge when clusters of agents, ranging from dozens to hundreds, interact within semi-connected subnetworks. Here, no single failure predominates; rather, the interaction patterns themselves give rise to a gradual degradation in accuracy, diversity, or coherence. Though each agent may appear locally correct, the collective behavior deviates from the expected norms. These meso-level risks underscore that systemic degradation can occur even in the absence of a single malfunctioning component. Failures arise from the interference patterns between partially correct agents, not from isolated error sources. When these issues converge and reach a critical threshold, systemic instability takes hold, and the group’s behavior transforms in unanticipated ways. This framework serves as an initial structure for discussing and analyzing these behaviors. In the absence of such a framework, identifying, quantifying, and regulating these patterns within group dynamics becomes exceedingly difficult. Validation of this hypothesis will require a series of controlled experiments, which we plan to conduct in future phases of our research.
