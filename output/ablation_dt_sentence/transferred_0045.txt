Large language models (LLMs) exhibit strong explicit reasoning capabilities; however, their implicit computational mechanisms—particularly in contexts lacking explicit instructions—remain poorly understood. Decades of psychophysics research demonstrate that humans intuitively process and integrate noisy signals using sophisticated strategies to interpret visual information. This raises the question: do LLMs exhibit similar behaviour, and can they perform effective multimodal integration in zero-shot settings? Drawing from psychological methodologies, we systematically analyze model behaviour to infer their cognitive capacities. To this end, we introduce BayesBench, a benchmark comprising four magnitude estimation tasks—spanning dimensions such as size and time—administered over both text and image modalities. Grounded in foundational psychophysical research, BayesBench enables standardized comparisons between nine LLMs and human judgments. Through controlled variations in noise, context, and instructional prompts, we evaluate performance, cue integration, and behavioural efficiency. Beyond traditional metrics of accuracy and efficiency, we incorporate a Bayesian Consistency Score to detect alignment with optimal decision-making, even when accuracy alone appears saturated. Our findings reveal that while certain models exhibit Bayes-consistent adaptation, correctness alone is not a proxy for robustness. For example, although GPT-5 Mini performs strongly on text-based tasks, it fails to integrate visual information effectively. This dissociation between competence and computational strategy suggests that accuracy-focused benchmarks may overlook deficiencies in uncertainty management. These results highlight how models handle uncertainty and how perceived rationality is often conflated with correctness. We present these findings and corresponding scores as tools to guide the development of multimodal systems capable of principled information integration. Estimating physical attributes such as size, duration, or spatial extent constitutes a core computational capacity in both biological and artificial intelligence. Humans accomplish this by integrating noisy cues, weighting them by reliability, and applying prior expectations to reduce error. This strategy arises spontaneously across developmental and cultural contexts, underscoring its role as a foundational solution to processing uncertainty.
