In this paper, we explore the potential parallels between brain function and artificial intelligence (AI) through the lens of the “world model,” a computational framework that enables predictive understanding and processing of external stimuli. Adopting this perspective, we conducted a comparative analysis that revealed a convergence in information-processing strategies between the brain and modern AI systems, most notably large-scale language models. Building on this insight, we propose a novel theory of macro-scale information processing in the brain. Specifically, cortical and cerebellar circuits appear to construct internal world models by predicting future states of the environment from prior sensory inputs, learning through the minimization of prediction errors, and deploying these models to support diverse cognitive and behavioral functions. These predictive processes contribute not only to the interpretation of sensory inputs, such as visual and auditory signals, but also to the coordination of motor actions. This theory integrates and extends earlier frameworks—such as the internal model theory and the mirror neuron system—by positing that a unified world-model-based computation underlies a broad array of functions within a common circuit architecture. Therefore, this framework offers a conceptual bridge between neuroscience and AI, advancing our understanding of the mechanisms supporting human-like intelligence. In particular, the cerebellum functions as a predictive system, receiving inputs from the external world, generating expectations about future states, and refining these predictions through learning driven by prediction errors. Accumulating evidence suggests that the cerebellum primarily learns to predict upcoming sensory input, rather than relying on abstract supervisory signals.
