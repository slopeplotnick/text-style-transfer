This paper examines why safety mechanisms designed for human-model interaction do not scale to environments where large language models (LLMs) interact with each other. Most existing safety protocols still focus on single-agent containment through prompts, fine-tuning, and moderation, which regulate individual model behavior but overlook the dynamics of multi-model interaction. These mechanisms are predicated on a dyadic model, where a single model responds to one user under continuous supervision. However, both research and industry are swiftly moving towards architectures in which models engage in sustained interactions, recursively incorporating outputs from other models into extended computational chains. In such systems, even when each model operates as intended, the collective outcome can still lead to failure. We propose shifting the focus from individual model safety to broader system-level safety, introducing the Emergent Systemic Risk Horizon (ESRH) to elucidate how risks emerge from the structure of interactions among models, rather than from the malfunction or misalignment of any single model. This paper presents (i) a theoretical framework for understanding risks arising from interactions between models, (ii) a conceptual model linking micro, meso, and macro-level failure modes, and (iii) a proposal for Institutional AI, a structural approach designed to enhance oversight within multi-agent systems. Current safety approaches assume the operation of a single model under human supervision, where undesirable behaviors can be mitigated through prompt modifications, reinforcement learning from human feedback, or output regulation. While these techniques have improved the reliability and politeness of models, their effectiveness is still confined to specific instances. They govern the response of individual models to a single input, but do not address the collaborative functioning of multiple models. As large models are increasingly deployed in tool-driven, agentic, and multi-agent settings, the paradigm of single-agent safety begins to unravel. Even when models are well-aligned individually, they may still produce problematic outcomes when working collectively—issues that no single model was trained to mitigate, arising from feedback loops, imitation, or emergent coordination. This results in a gap between ensuring the optimal performance of individual models and maintaining the overall safety of the system, where success at the component level does not necessarily guarantee effective control over the network as a whole.
