Efforts are being made to enhance machine perception of high-resolution images, with a common approach focusing on specific regions to improve the perception of finer details. While training-based methods such as Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) and Reinforcement Learning (RL) have proven effective in guiding model learning, they come with significant drawbacks, including high computational costs, long training cycles, and limited cross-architecture generalizability, which hinder their scalability and broader applicability. In contrast, training-free methods leverage attention mechanisms or tree-based search techniques to automatically identify key image regions without the need for dataset construction or model fine-tuning. These approaches generally start by examining the entire image and progressively zoom in to refine the details. However, they may overlook small features during the initial search, leading to potential inaccuracies. Recently, Retrieval-Augmented Perception (RAP) has emerged as a training-free framework designed to improve the ability of machines to perceive high-resolution images. RAP consists of three key components: the first leverages a pre-trained model to localize image regions with the highest semantic similarity to the target concept, reducing extraneous visual content. The second preserves the original layout of these regions, ensuring that all elements remain properly aligned. Finally, the third component uses a decision tree to determine the optimal number of components, balancing the need for sufficient information with computational efficiency.
