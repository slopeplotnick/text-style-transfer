This work proposes a novel approach to disentangling perceptual processing from higher-level reasoning within artificial intelligence models. To support this goal, we augment the training data with examples of incorrect reasoning, enabling the model to better distinguish between valid and invalid thought processes. In contrast to prior methods that primarily emphasize reasoning, STL introduces a unified framework that enhances both perception and reasoning. We evaluate this approach across multiple domains—including commonsense reasoning, natural language understanding, and scientific inquiry—and observe consistent performance gains without requiring human supervision. In the context of vision–language reasoning, incorporating explicit reasoning mechanisms has proven critical for improving the performance and robustness of Vision–Language Models (VLMs). However, despite recent advances on benchmark tasks, effectively integrating visual information into the reasoning process remains a significant challenge, particularly for open-source models. To address this, some methodologies employ prompts to guide the model through a structured cognitive process, resulting in more coherent and interpretable outputs. One line of work emphasizes context analysis followed by high-level feature generation, while another relies on scene graphs to model object–attribute relationships, enabling a two-stage process of graph construction and reasoning. Other approaches attempt to train models on curated datasets containing reasoning examples; however, the creation of such datasets is often labor-intensive and costly. To overcome this limitation, our method enables models to iteratively refine their reasoning through self-directed learning, without the need for manually annotated datasets. Specifically, self-training offers a semi-supervised paradigm in which a model leverages its own outputs for supervision, aligning its learning trajectory with its evolving capabilities. In large language models, this technique enhances reasoning by systematically generating and reusing intermediate reasoning steps. As a result, model performance improves while minimizing reliance on explicit human guidance. Recent studies have further advanced this process by selecting and refining high-quality reasoning examples. Nevertheless, self-training for vision–language models remains relatively underexplored in current research, underscoring the need for continued investigation in this area.
