Certain models may be more reliable than others. This review addresses the influence of model size, structure, and training data on the occurrence of hallucinations. A study presents these tendencies across different models and offers solutions, such as improving training techniques and imposing limitations on the generated output, to mitigate the issue. However, hallucination remains a fundamental challenge for large language models (LLMs), regardless of their size or training methodology. While it is possible to reduce hallucinations through careful model selection and training, the authors suggest that these models are unlikely to be entirely free from such issues. This is of significant importance as it helps in formulating realistic goals and determining the appropriateness of a given model for specific tasks. When selecting an LLM to reduce hallucinations, factors such as model architecture, the quality and diversity of the training data, and strategies like fine-tuning or retrieval augmentation must be considered. Numerous models are currently available, including GPT, BERT, and T5. Of these, GPT often generates text that is logically consistent and fluently structured, but it is also prone to generating fabricated content (hallucinations). In contrast, BERT tends to experience fewer hallucinations due to its bidirectional training approach for specific tasks, although it can still generate fabricated content in certain contexts. Models such as PaLM and LLaMa have been developed with the aim of reducing hallucinations, though their effectiveness depends on the specific training methodologies and architectural designs employed. Understanding the strengths and limitations of these models is crucial when choosing a tool for tasks where accuracy is critical and minimizing hallucination rates is a priority.
