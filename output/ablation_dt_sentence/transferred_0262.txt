To govern such systems, safety evaluation must evolve from rule-bound compliance to system-level observation. This involves assessing how influence propagates across agents, whether oversight signals penetrate collective behavior, and when feedback mechanisms exceed the threshold of controllability. The central hypothesis of this paper is that while fundamental safety mechanisms are necessary, they are insufficient once models participate in networks of interaction. Even under ideal local conditions, emergent failures remain inevitable when feedback loops, network topologies, and collective incentives dominate the system's behavior. Recent studies have addressed the emergent risks in multi-agent systems, offering insightful frameworks: MAST (Multi-Agent System failure Taxonomy) and MARAI (Multi-Agent Risks from Advanced AI). MAST classifies failures into three categories, focusing on risks arising from the system's design, agent coordination, and verification processes. In contrast, MARAI identifies three failure modes based on the nature of inter-agent interactions—miscoordination, conflict, and collusion—that may lead to misaligned behaviors. Building on these, our contribution is twofold: 1) we integrate both frameworks into a cohesive, system-centered approach that transitions from agent-level failures to risks that cannot be attributed solely to individual components; 2) we formalize this integration through the concept of the Emergent Systemic Risk Horizon (ESRH), a boundary at which localized reliability shifts to collective instability. The ESRH provides both a vocabulary and empirical indicators to study LLM ecosystems as complex adaptive systems rather than isolated agents. This reframing sets the stage for the analysis that follows. Section 2 outlines the empirical and conceptual shift towards multi-agent autonomy and explores the regulatory implications of distributed causality. Section 3 introduces the ESRH taxonomy and the metrics used in this study. Finally, Section 4 proposes the Institutional AI architecture as a design principle for embedding governance mechanisms within interacting model collectives.
