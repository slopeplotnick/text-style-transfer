While large language models (LLMs) generally excel across a wider array of tasks due to their larger capacity and superior instruction-following abilities, smaller language models (SLMs) demonstrate competitive or even superior performance in specific tasks, particularly in predicting depression and stress. For other tasks, although SLMs show significant improvements, their performance still lags behind that of LLMs (Xu et al., 2024). This suggests that SLMs can achieve strong results when the nature of the task and their language processing strengths align. However, a notable limitation persists: SLMs struggle with long input sequences, due to both token-length restrictions and reduced capacity to manage extended contextual dependencies (Kumar, 2025). As a result, their applicability is constrained in scenarios requiring a deep understanding of lengthy or complex narratives, an area where LLMs maintain a distinct advantage (Lu et al., 2024). These findings underscore the need for a shift in focus from merely increasing model size to optimizing models for specific purposes within mental-health AI. As efficiency, transparency, safety, and user privacy (Zhang et al., 2021) become paramount in clinical machine learning, balancing performance with practical applicability will shape the future of digital mental-health tools. SLMs already meet emerging demands in digital mental health for real-time, low-latency, and privacy-preserving detection of mental health conditions (Harari et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2018), while also aligning with calls for greater transparency and accountability in healthcare AI systems (Wies et al., 2021; Smith et al., 2023). By demonstrating that fine-tuned SLMs, optimized with cross-dataset integration strategies, can achieve predictive performance on par with or even surpassing LLMs in mental-health outcome prediction, this study highlights that smaller, well-aligned models may offer a more sustainable and ethically responsible alternative for real-world mental health monitoring. In this context, we show that cross-source training, particularly when integrated with an adaptive loss function, can significantly improve predictions of depression, stress, and suicidal ideation. This aligns with the work of Yao et al. (Benton et al., 2017), who found that sharing latent patterns across related mental health tasks can lead to positive transfer. However, unlike their multi-task approach, our method achieves comparable benefits through a more efficient fine-tuning process (LoRA), reducing both computational cost and memory usage. These results emphasize that compact, parameter-efficient models can effectively leverage heterogeneous data sources when the training objectives are carefully designed.
