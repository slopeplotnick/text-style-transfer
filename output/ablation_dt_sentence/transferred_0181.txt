The framework demonstrates the feasibility of using Large Language Models (LLMs) to solve complex optimization problems, particularly in resource allocation tasks. To assess the computational efficiency of LLM-driven allocation, dynamic prompting techniques are employed, which enhance task performance by adapting the action selection policies within evolving environments. Furthermore, the framework evaluates LLM performance in comparison to traditional algorithmic solutions, such as Deep Q-Networks (DQN) and Q-learning. These objectives are systematically achieved through a structured approach, beginning with task decomposition and progressing to the optimization of balanced resource allocation. Long-horizon construction tasks are initially broken down into modular subtasks through object-centric and skill-centric decomposition strategies derived from the SMART-LLM framework (Kannan et al., 2024). The object-centric approach categorizes tasks based on the physical objects involved and their necessary manipulations, while the skill-centric decomposition organizes tasks based on the specific capabilities required, such as precise handling or force application. These subtasks are then assigned to heterogeneous robot teams through capability-aware matching, ensuring that task requirements align with robot expertise. The LTAA framework incorporates LangGraph orchestration and probabilistic success modeling to maximize task completion likelihood by evaluating robot-specific success rates for each subtask based on feature-capability alignment. However, initial testing revealed significant workload imbalances, as the pure success-probability approach led to the overutilization of high-capability robots and the underutilization of specialized robots. This insight prompted the development of phase-adaptive allocation strategies that dynamically adjust priorities throughout the task's progression, shifting from an initial focus on success rates to an emphasis on equitable workload distribution in later stages. The methodology developed through this process is divided into three stages. First, the implementation of the SMART-LLM framework demonstrated the potential of LLMs for Multi-Robot Task Allocation (MRTA), though it revealed issues with validation rigor and output reliability. Second, these challenges were addressed by the introduction of a Self-Corrective-Agent Architecture, which employed multi-stage validation and hierarchical retry mechanisms. Finally, the LTAA framework integrated these validation principles with phase-adaptive allocation strategies to enable systematic benchmarking against traditional optimization methods. The authors outline the implementation of the classic LLM-based MRTA framework, SMART-LLM (Kannan et al., 2024), and discuss the primary challenges encountered, including validation inconsistencies, output reliability, and coordination issues. These obstacles led to the development of the phase-adaptive, self-corrective LangGraph Architecture, which incorporates multi-stage validation and retry mechanisms to mitigate identified deficiencies. These innovations formed the basis for the LTAA framework. Additionally, the paper presents a comprehensive performance evaluation, comparing the LTAA framework with traditional optimization methods. The evaluation employs the TEACH dataset as a benchmark for direct comparison with Q-learning, DQN, and dynamic programming baselines, assessing criteria such as workload balance, feature-specific task performance, and reasoning quality.
