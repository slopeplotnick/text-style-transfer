Psychometric scales and questionnaires are essential tools in quantitative research across diverse fields, including psychology, medicine, social sciences, education, and human-computer interaction. When a novel construct or theoretical framework is introduced, it typically becomes the focus of empirical investigation, and the development of corresponding psychometric tools is often necessary. However, this process is resource-intensive, requiring substantial investment in time, materials, and funding (Stefana et al., 2025). In this article, we explore the potential to alleviate the resource demands of the scale development process by using computer simulations, specifically through the generation of psychometric data for representative samples via Large Language Models (LLMs). We begin by outlining the rationale behind this approach and the hypotheses driving our research. Next, we present four studies designed to empirically test the feasibility of this method, and conclude with a discussion of the results and recommendations for future research. Recruiting a representative sample of participants is both costly and time-consuming, which often leads researchers to rely on convenience samples of volunteering university students, a practice that limits the external validity of the findings (Hanel & Vione, 2016; Wild et al., 2022). In response to these challenges, recent studies have increasingly utilized online platforms such as Prolific.co and Amazon Mechanical Turk to recruit participants (Buhrmester et al., 2018; Douglas et al., 2023; Huang et al., 2015). These platforms not only reduce time and financial costs but also offer a means to recruit samples that more accurately reflect specific populations, such as those in the United Kingdom or the United States. However, concerns about the ethical implications of recruiting from these platforms have been raised, particularly regarding the potential for participants to come from disadvantaged backgrounds (Huang et al., 2015; Huff & Tingley, 2015; Moss et al., 2023). Furthermore, when exploring novel constructs for which no validated scale exists, or when validating such a scale is not feasible, researchers often create ad hoc questionnaires based solely on theoretical frameworks. While this approach can reduce costs and time, it comes at the expense of the external validity of the findings, which may not generalize to populations outside the study sample (Lee et al., 2020).
