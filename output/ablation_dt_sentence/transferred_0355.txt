We conduct a series of evaluations on diverse benchmarks, including machine translation assessment (WMT23) (Song et al., 2025) and reward model evaluation (Reward Bench 2) (Malik et al., 2025). Our results demonstrate that our proposed distribution-calibrated method significantly outperforms conventional self-consistency-based approaches. By modeling the full distribution of votes, our method transforms noisy individual model outputs into more reliable ratings, matching or surpassing the performance of individual human raters when measured against a consensus-based gold standard. Our key contributions are threefold: (1) We identify that prevailing inference-time aggregation methods for LLM judges are suboptimal and highlight the necessity of a more principled aggregation strategy. (2) We introduce a novel aggregation method based on Expected Risk Minimization (ERM) and the Bradley-Terry-Davidson model, trained on a small calibration set, and show that it consistently outperforms existing methods across reward and translation evaluation tasks. (3) Specifically for machine translation, we employ a consensus-based meta-evaluation to construct higher-fidelity ground truth labels in noisy settings, enabling fairer comparisons to human judgments and uncovering scenarios where LLM judges exceed human-level evaluation quality. LLM-as-a-Judge: Recently, Large Language Models (LLMs) have gained traction as effective "judges" for evaluating generated text, providing a scalable and cost-efficient alternative to traditional quality metrics (Zheng et al., 2023b; Gu et al., 2025). These models have shown strong alignment with human preferences across a wide range of domains. Evaluation approaches vary: some rely on prompting general-purpose LLMs directly—such as G-Eval (Liu et al., 2023) and JudgeLM (Zhu et al., 2025)—while others fine-tune models specifically for evaluation, including Prometheus (Kim et al., 2023) and Auto-J (Li et al., 2023). However, despite their strong performance, these LLM-based evaluators face notable limitations, including sensitivity to prompt design and systemic biases, such as positional bias favoring particular candidate orders or morbidity bias toward longer outputs (Wang et al., 2023a). Moreover, LLM judges exhibit inconsistency in their decision-making, with some models being more assertive than others in distinguishing subtle differences or resolving ties (Zheng et al., 2023b). Our method addresses these challenges by mitigating noise and enhancing decision reliability through improved aggregation.
