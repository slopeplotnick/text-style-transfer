To effectively reason over long videos, retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) methods have been introduced, which retrieve relevant frames or clips instead of relying on uniformly sampled frames. These methods leverage both textual and visual cues to identify the most significant portions of the video, enabling the model to focus on crucial clips rather than randomly selecting frames. Some recent approaches further enhance this by constructing graph-based structures to encode multimodal interactions across video segments. However, these models often rely heavily on textual signals or simplistic visual-textual similarity metrics, which limits their ability to perform holistic understanding and complex reasoning over extended video content. In response to these limitations, memory-based methods have emerged, aiming to structure and retain video information for efficient retrieval during downstream tasks. For instance, EgoRAG organizes hierarchical textual memories from egocentric video streams to facilitate reasoning about day-long activities. Ego-R1 extends this by incorporating vision-centric tools and iterative reasoning processes to support long-horizon tasks. HippoMM employs a dual-memory system that integrates semantic summaries with multimodal cues, while M3-Agent constructs entity-centric long-term memory, adopting step-by-step reasoning to retrieve relevant knowledge from memory. Despite these advancements, current models still face challenges in fully integrating textual and visual modalities and adapting to varying temporal scales, which limits their ability to handle complex, long-form video scenarios. To address these challenges, we propose WorldMM, a novel framework that integrates both textual and visual contexts from video streams to build a multimodal memory, enhancing the system’s understanding and reasoning capabilities over extended video sequences. The system operates in three stages: first, it constructs multiple memory types, including two distinct textual memories and one visual memory. The agent then performs an iterative retrieval process to extract relevant information from these memories across different temporal scales, continuing until enough data is collected to adequately respond to the query. Finally, this information is used to generate the final response.
