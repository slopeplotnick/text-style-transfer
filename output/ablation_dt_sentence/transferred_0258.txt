The traditional approach involves performing a deterministic inversion, followed by a deterministic interpretation, as shown in Figure 2. Subsequently, an expert geologist interprets the inversion in terms of shapes, such as intrusions, which are relevant for exploration. These interpretations are typically conducted using software that focuses on a single deterministic model. In contrast, the proposed method suggests performing the geophysical inversion before the geological interpretation. One might argue that the regularization terms serve to incorporate geological knowledge; however, this argument remains vague and provides limited contribution. Effectively, the current approach results in a redundant application of geological knowledge: it is initially incorporated in the regularization term and later re-applied during the interpretation stage. This creates a self-reinforcing cycle that borders on circular reasoning. In the Bayesian framework, both aspects are tackled simultaneously as a unified problem. Bayes' methodology requires formulating plausible solutions (geometries) before any inversion takes place. Bayesian inversion provides relatively simple solutions that can be easily implemented and are broadly applicable. Let us return to Figure 2 and examine how to establish a prior probability model. We begin with the question: what is the minimum knowledge we possess about the subsurface prior to acquiring any observational data? As we have previously observed, petrophysical properties have characteristic ranges, and by knowing the specific lithologies involved, we can refine these ranges with greater precision. Moreover, it is clear that the spatial variation of these properties is neither random nor constant over large areas. In fact, the formation of deposits leads to specific alterations in these properties. Conceptually, the minimum information we have consists of constraints on histograms and the presence of spatial correlation. Mathematically, this can be expressed as a spatial covariance model, or variogram in geostatistics, though the parameters of this model—such as the mean, ranges, and anisotropy—remain uncertain.
