In the rapidly evolving field of Natural Language Processing (NLP), large language models (LLMs) such as GPT and BERT have emerged as principal agents of transformation. They are capable of generating human-like text, answering complex questions, or engaging in fluent conversations. If a method yields an answer that appears logically consistent beforehand, it can be compared to navigating northward on a compass. However, while the responses generated by these models may exhibit semantic relevance and follow internally consistent reasoning, they lack inherent truth value, as they are not grounded in verifiable facts. This phenomenon, known as hallucination, represents a key challenge limiting the broader adoption of these models. In essence, it is akin to an experienced storyteller, who, while attempting to narrate a truthful account, becomes so immersed in the flow of the story that they introduce details beyond empirical reality. Similarly, LLMs may generate content that appears accurate, yet lacks grounding in the data on which they were trained. A simpler analogy would be a GPS system confidently offering directions, yet occasionally leading you down a road that does not exist. More precisely, hallucinations in LLMs occur when generated content deviates from factual or expected outputs based on the models' training data. This may include false statements, fabricated references, or flawed reasoning. Just as a misguided GPS system may appear to know the way, yet ends up taking the wrong route, LLMs can produce confident yet erroneous results. This challenge is actively being addressed by the AI research community. Even advanced models remain vulnerable to hallucinations. While LLMs are undeniably powerful, they are not consistently reliable, especially when it comes to factual accuracy. This issue is particularly critical in fields such as healthcare, law, and academic research, where the consequences of misinformation can be profound.
