Recent advances in Large Language Models (LLMs) have been driven primarily by the scaling of test-time computational resources. As demonstrated by models like OpenAI’s o1 (OpenAI, 2024) and DeepSeek-R1 (Guo et al., 2025), extended “think” segments—reflecting intermediate hypotheses, inferences, and self-corrections—have become central to improving final answer quality. This emerging paradigm of expanded test-time reasoning has proven effective for boosting model accuracy and robustness, particularly in complex problem-solving settings. However, simply prolonging the reasoning process does not guarantee better outcomes; in fact, excessive cognitive processing can degrade performance, with studies showing that accurate answers often emerge from more concise reasoning traces. To complement sequential scaling, parallel reasoning has gained traction: generating multiple independent reasoning paths and aggregating their outputs, typically via majority voting in tasks with definitive answers. This approach has shown strong performance in constrained domains such as mathematics and multiple-choice questions. Nevertheless, many real-world applications are inherently open-ended. For example, coding assistants must produce executable programs, while research-focused agents often orchestrate tool use, compose detailed procedural plans, or generate long-form explanatory content. In these contexts, majority voting becomes ill-defined, as there may be no singular correct answer despite its success in closed-form tasks. This discrepancy raises a key question: Can the advantages of parallelized reasoning be retained in open-ended settings without defaulting to consensus-based output selection? In this work, we introduce THINKMERGE, a framework that integrates multiple reasoning paths at inference time to synthesize a single optimal output. Departing from prior methods that rely on static representations or isolated predictions, recent approaches have shifted toward dynamic, context-sensitive inference—a trend that reflects growing awareness of the limits of shallow signal extraction and the importance of integrating structural priors and iterative reasoning (McCallum et al., 2018).
