Despite these limitations, the replication study confirmed three key findings: (1) LLM-driven coordination is viable for multi-robot task allocation, (2) structured validation mechanisms significantly improve the reliability of reasoning, and (3) hierarchical retry systems effectively address inconsistencies in LLM-generated outputs. With the feasibility of LLM-based MRTA thus established, the authors proceeded to develop the LTAA framework to further improve the performance and robustness of such systems. The LTAA framework was evaluated using the TEACH dataset, the standard benchmark for direct comparison with Q-learning, DQN, and DP baselines in MRTA research. This dataset transition—from AI2-THOR household tasks to construction robotics scenarios—enables rigorous performance comparison with traditional algorithms under identical evaluation conditions. The LTAA framework specializes the allocation component derived from the Self Corrective-Agent Architecture, accepting pre-decomposed tasks and focusing exclusively on robot–task assignment decisions. This narrowed scope ensures that both LLM-based and algorithm-based methods are evaluated under equivalent problem formulations. Building on the previously introduced self-corrective principles, the LTAA framework implements a nine-node workflow. Unlike the Self Corrective-Agent Architecture, which handled the entire task pipeline, the LTAA framework concentrates solely on the allocation decision process, producing robot–task assignments analogous to those of traditional algorithms. To support competitive performance with conventional optimization methods while maintaining transparency in reasoning, the LTAA framework introduces three core innovations: (1) phase-adaptive allocation strategies that dynamically adjust decision priorities throughout a project, (2) multi-stage validation processes incorporating hierarchical retry and self-correction mechanisms to ensure the quality of LLM reasoning, and (3) context-aware reasoning integration that structures decisions using robots’ quantitative capability priors. These innovations are realized through a structured nine-node LangGraph workflow that guides each task through distinct stages of reasoning.
