4.5.1 Fact Verification. Our analysis indicates that the effectiveness of FV methods is fundamentally contingent on the quality of the retrieval module and the availability of relevant external information. As shown in Table 3, this dependency introduces a key vulnerability: when the retrieval component fails to supply necessary supporting evidence, the overall system performance is significantly degraded. Specifically, if the retriever cannot identify relevant passages—often due to keyword mismatches in the LLM-Q setting—the verification module is forced to operate without sufficient contextual grounding. This absence of evidence leads to erratic model behavior: the system may exhibit unwarranted skepticism, triggering false positives (as seen in Case 1), or mistakenly accept inaccurate assertions without proper validation, thereby overlooking critical errors (as in Case 2). However, when the retrieval step is augmented with the generated answer (LLM-QA), the system is more likely to retrieve relevant documents, thus enabling the FV module to produce more accurate inferences. 4.5.2 Hallucination Detection. In contrast, HD methods often struggle due to the inherent flexibility of language generation, which complicates the task of identifying hallucinations. Table 4 presents a clear contrast: HD methods perform strongly on deterministic responses (Group A) but experience a marked decline in accuracy when applied to more creative or expressive outputs (Group B). In such cases, a model may be confident in the factual accuracy of a claim (e.g., the founding year of a band) while simultaneously expressing lexical uncertainty about its articulation. Existing HD methods lack the capacity to distinguish between legitimate phrasal variation and actual misinformation. Consequently, the presence of multiple correct ways to express the same idea often results in erroneous classification, with valid alternatives being misidentified as errors. 4.5.3 Implications for Hybrid Design. Our analysis reveals that FV and HD exhibit distinct, non-overlapping error boundaries, which provides a rationale for their integration. FV serves as a precision-focused mechanism, offering highly reliable judgments when relevant evidence is successfully retrieved. However, its utility diminishes significantly in the absence of such evidence, highlighting its contextual limitations. Conversely, HD functions as a recall-oriented safeguard, capable of identifying potential errors even without external support, though its reliability suffers under conditions of semantic ambiguity. Therefore, a well-calibrated hybrid strategy should not treat both components as equivalent. Instead, the system should favor FV when corroborating evidence is available, while defaulting to HD’s internal signals when retrieval fails. This conditional complementarity directly informs the hierarchical structure of the hybrid framework proposed in RQ3.
