Sentiment analysis has become a cornerstone of contemporary Natural Language Processing (NLP), facilitating applications in digital governance, public health monitoring, financial inclusion, and the moderation of user-generated content. However, despite significant global advancements in deep learning and multilingual language modeling, South African low-resource languages remain underrepresented in sentiment analysis research. This underrepresentation is largely due to the limited availability of annotated datasets, the lack of comprehensive lexical resources, and the restricted capacity for effective cross-lingual knowledge transfer. As a result, models that perform well in high-resource languages such as English and French tend to exhibit reduced effectiveness in African contexts, further exacerbating the digital divide and hindering equitable access to downstream NLP technologies. Existing research on multilingual sentiment analysis has predominantly focused on machine translation, the development of cross-lingual embeddings, and the creation of monolingual lexicons for individual languages. While these approaches have yielded promising results, they are not without their limitations. For instance, machine-translated lexicons often introduce errors and cultural discrepancies, particularly in morphologically complex African languages. Additionally, most word extraction techniques reliant on large datasets require domain-specific resources, which are typically scarce for low-resource languages. Furthermore, while models like AfroXLMR and AfriBERTa have shown progress, their performance remains constrained by the insufficient availability of high-quality sentiment lexicons, which are essential for accurate classification in multilingual settings. To address these challenges, we introduce TriLex, a novel three-stage framework designed to expand sentiment lexicons for African languages with limited resources. TriLex incorporates: 1) corpus-based analysis to identify candidate terms indicative of sentiment, 2) cross-lingual semantic alignment to ensure lexical equivalence across English, French, and target African languages, and 3) Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) to refine, validate, and expand the lexicon through large language models (LLMs) that are conditioned on multilingual retrieval signals.
