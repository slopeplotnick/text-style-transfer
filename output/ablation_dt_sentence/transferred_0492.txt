Because concepts are not explicitly defined in data features, it is common to use probing techniques to detect neurons, or configurations of neurons, that best represent target concepts. These probes are often linear, but they may also be non-linear in cases where the concept is not linearly separable. Moreover, probes can be trained using either supervised or unsupervised methods. The supervised approach requires labeled data corresponding to the human-specified concept, while the unsupervised approach identifies concepts pivotal to the model but not necessarily interpretable by humans. This study extends the modeling of structural equations beyond mere observation-based analyses. It considers various levels of modeling, ranging from FSCMs, which are made to be easily interpretable with domain-informed assumptions, to PSCMs, which include all FSCMs consistent with empirical data. This highlights the importance of selecting an appropriate modeling approach and demonstrates how partial information can be valuable when complete FSCMs are not available. Galhotra et al. (2021) present an XAI methodology that employs FSCMs to calculate the probabilities of sufficiency and necessity. They focus on tabular data and incorporate causal modeling into data features, assuming these represent high-level concepts, while allowing the integration of additional concepts not initially part of the model input. Although this approach only applies when features are significant concepts, it eliminates the need for a causal generator. Furthermore, it assumes a preference regarding the explanation of model outputs, explaining which subsets of input values are sufficient to alter a model's prediction to a preferable state or to maintain a preferable prediction when the outcome is ideally preserved unchanged.
