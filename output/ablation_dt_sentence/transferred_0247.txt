Conversely, the daughter language model has been developed specifically as part of the MOD-OMA framework. Similar to Delilah, the daughter agent functions as both a parser and a generator; however, unlike the adult language model, its grammatical representations are initially underspecified and become progressively more defined through language acquisition procedures. A notable similarity between the adult and child systems is that both employ graph structures to represent grammatical knowledge, and their respective parsers and generators execute grammars by unifying lexical structures. This enables the generation of well-formed utterances as well as the provision of grammaticality judgments and parses in accordance with the currently specified grammar. Crucially, the parser and generator of the child agent are capable of operating on an incomplete grammar, reflecting the human-like process of acquiring language. As with the daughter language model, which interacts with the mother agent before completing acquisition, the child agent must also engage with the mother model to develop its linguistic competence. The daughter agent comprises three interconnected components: (1) an automaton functioning as both parser and generator; (2) a grammar, the structure of which is incrementally specified through acquisition; and (3) a language acquisition device that mediates the learning process. Importantly, the parser and generator remain unchanged throughout learning. Language acquisition results in explicit grammatical knowledge, represented either by newly acquired lexical entries or by grammatical properties assigned to lexical entries that constrain their combinatory potential. These data structures offer comprehensive representations of words and constructions, including grammatical attributes such as phonological form, semantic content, and other acquired grammatical properties. These properties are encoded as feature-value pairs, for example, [CONTENT OR FUNCTION WORD: content word] and [CONTENT OR FUNCTION WORD: function word], the acquisition of which is a central aim of the present study. Since the learning process is unsupervised, the daughter language model has no access to the labeling conventions or grammatical descriptions used by the mother model. Accordingly, the acquired grammatical properties and semantic content are denoted by unique alpha-numeric labels, such as property type 'A' and property value 'b', as illustrated in Figures 2 and 3. These representations also include additional grammatical features that fall beyond the scope of this investigation. For instance, the terminal feature differentiates terminal lexical items from abstract syntactic structures, while the semantic content index allows for the encoding of referential dependencies, such as reflexive anaphora. Furthermore, technical identifiers—such as the session ID and lexical entry number—are included to uniquely identify each rule. As the simulation of language acquisition proceeds, the grammar evolves to encompass increasingly complex structures, wherein HEAD and ARGUMENT positions may contain data structures analogous to lexical entries.
