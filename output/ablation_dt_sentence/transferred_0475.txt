Despite the increasing prevalence of this approach, research concerning the interactions between solvers and verifiers remains sparse. Prior work has predominantly focused on the self-verification and subsequent self-improvement of individual models (Song et al., 2025). However, these processes are not invariably guaranteed to be effective: models may be biased toward their intrinsic cognitive patterns, which their training processes can exacerbate, and the benefits from verification can vary significantly across different tasks. Furthermore, a sole emphasis on self-verification offers limited insight into the dynamics that emerge when the solver and verifier are distinct entities. With the availability of open-source models in various iterations and configurations, we can systematically investigate these verification processes across different model frameworks. Thus, we expand our study to encompass both intra-family and inter-family verification. We pose the following critical inquiry: Under what circumstances does verification yield significant benefits, and how do factors such as model family, model size, additional post-training, solver-verifier congruence, or task typology modulate the efficacy of verification in enhancing the solver's performance? To determine this, we evaluate verifiers across a diverse array of tasks, including those necessitating precise logical reasoning or symbolic computation, such as 3-SAT, Sudoku, and matrix multiplication. In addition, we assess mathematical reasoning tasks like AIME and GSM8K, as well as commonsense and factual reasoning tasks, such as CSQA and GPQA, and broad domain knowledge in STEM and social sciences. This evaluation is conducted using 37 models drawn from seven distinct model families. Our study indicates that self-verification does not invariably yield beneficial outcomes: models frequently favor solutions that align with their intrinsic reasoning processes, and supplementary training can intensify this bias. Moreover, certain tasks inherently derive less advantage from verification processes than others. Therefore, we propose practical advice, grounded in empirical findings, on the effective utilization of verifiers.
