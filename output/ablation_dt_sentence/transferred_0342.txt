Our findings indicate that the fine-tuned model Menta demonstrates stronger performance in detecting depression and stress than in classifying suicide risk. In general, both fine-tuned and non-fine-tuned language models—including larger variants—tend to achieve superior results on depression and stress detection tasks compared to suicide risk classification. For instance, a systematic review by Bauer et al. (2024) notes that language models often misinterpret subtle expressions of suicidality, largely due to class imbalance and their reliance on contextual or temporal cues, which are typically absent in isolated social media posts. Consistently, Lamichhane (2023) reported F1-scores of 0.86 for depression detection but only 0.37 for suicidality detection using social media data. These outcomes suggest that depression and stress are more readily identifiable due to their salient linguistic markers and broader data availability, whereas suicidality tends to be rarer, context-dependent, and linguistically nuanced, making it more challenging for models to detect reliably. Therefore, language models may be better suited for general screening tasks—such as detecting depression or stress—than for nuanced suicide risk classification, unless supported by multimodal inputs or temporal data. **Limitations and future directions.** While our study demonstrates the efficacy of models such as Qwen-3 (4B) in predicting mental health conditions, several limitations affect the interpretability and generalizability of our findings. Although we evaluated diverse tasks, all inputs were limited to textual social media posts. This focus excludes key multimodal features such as writing style evolution, user profile data, and patterns of online interaction, all of which may offer valuable context for mental health assessment. Incorporating such signals could improve robustness and ecological validity in future research. Another limitation is the narrow scope of mental health conditions examined—restricted to depression, stress, and suicidal ideation. These categories, while commonly used in affective computing, represent only a subset of the broader psychological spectrum. Real-world mental health conditions—such as anxiety, bipolar disorder, and emotional dysregulation—remain underrepresented in our current evaluation. Moreover, our analyses relied exclusively on publicly available, anonymized social media data, rather than clinical sources. While this approach ensures privacy and reproducibility, it constrains our ability to draw conclusions about clinical safety, ethical standards, and long-term reliability in healthcare settings.
