This work seeks to identify mechanisms by which heterogeneous systems can be effectively aligned and coordinated to ensure robust and coherent interoperability. To this end, we are constructing controlled test environments where artificial agents can enforce, interpret, and adaptively revise shared normative frameworks. The primary aim is to assess whether these methods contribute to mitigating the issues discussed previously. In doing so, our objective is to establish a research trajectory in AI safety that integrates theoretical foundations with empirical investigations, ultimately advancing the development of standardized frameworks. This work serves as a conceptual framework, with subsequent sections providing the empirical evidence and experimental validation necessary to evaluate these propositions. Notably, this paper argues that a safety framework centered on individual language models—encompassing prompt engineering, output filtering, and agent-specific alignment—fails to adequately address the complex, emergent behaviors arising from LLM-to-LLM interaction. As artificial intelligences increasingly collaborate, it becomes clear that mere adherence to localized protocols is insufficient to prevent significant failures. Ensuring the safety of an individual AI agent differs significantly from safeguarding the integrity of the entire system. To address this, we introduce the concept of the Emergent Systemic Risk Horizon (ESRH) as a framework for understanding when this discrepancy becomes evident. We demonstrate that risks can escalate from minor deviations to widespread system-level issues as the interplay between interaction dynamics, complexity, and divergent objectives intensifies. Importantly, these risks arise not only from the malfunction of individual AI systems but also from the intricate interactions within networks, where collective dynamics contribute to emergent vulnerabilities. By enabling agents to adapt and collaboratively revise normative frameworks, the system aims to maintain stability in collective behavior without relying solely on centralized oversight. This work represents an initial step in exploring these challenges. Both the ESRH framework and the Institutional AI proposal require rigorous empirical validation, formal theoretical development, and iterative refinement. Our subsequent efforts will focus on conducting controlled experiments to quantify the defined indicators, exploring coordination models, and developing practical tools to implement these concepts in real-world AI systems. The transition from single-agent to multi-agent AI is already underway, but without the introduction of innovative approaches and effective management strategies, safety concerns will remain hidden until they reach an unmanageable scale. This paper aims to make these challenges explicit and outline a systematic approach for addressing them.
