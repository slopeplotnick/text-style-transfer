In recent times, brain-computer interfaces (BCIs) have garnered significant attention as both invasive<sup>1</sup> and non-invasive<sup>2</sup> tools facilitating direct communication between the brain and external devices or digital systems. These interfaces have proven especially valuable in supporting individuals with severe motor disabilities, including those caused by conditions such as locked-in syndrome, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), spinal cord injury, and stroke<sup>4–8</sup>. BCIs enable these individuals to communicate and, in some cases, regain motor control, offering hope where traditional therapies fall short. Consequently, there is a growing imperative to enhance non-invasive BCIs by analyzing a broader array of features derived from electroencephalography (EEG) data. Feature extraction methods for EEG signals are highly diverse, ranging from basic approaches such as time-domain and frequency-domain analysis to more advanced multi-dimensional techniques. These methods are essential in the transformation of raw EEG signals into meaningful representations—such as time-domain statistics, frequency-domain features (e.g., power spectral density), and time-frequency methods (e.g., wavelet transforms)—which are crucial for accurate signal classification and system performance. Moreover, as EEG signals often exhibit significant variability across individuals, it is vital to consider features that demonstrate robustness across subjects. This variability, along with changes in data over time and discrepancies between datasets, underscores the necessity of employing transfer learning and domain generalization strategies to ensure the reliability of the features. A key challenge in EEG analysis is identifying the optimal combinations of feature extraction and classification methods, particularly given the substantial inter-subject variability. To address this challenge, researchers must adopt evaluation methodologies that minimize optimistic bias, such as participant-independent validation strategies (e.g., leave-one-subject-out (LOSO) cross-validation) and performance metrics that are robust to class imbalances, such as balanced accuracy and AUROC. Additionally, it is essential to assess statistical significance through nonparametric or permutation testing and to rigorously prevent data leakage by ensuring that preprocessing steps, like filtering or normalization, are applied exclusively to the training data, leaving the test data untouched to preserve its integrity.
