However, developments are unfolding rapidly. Over the past decade, the global mining industry has found itself at the heart of the energy transition, with the demand for critical minerals likely rising along with the associated geopolitical, social, and environmental challenges. This dynamic has led to the creation of new types of companies, often backed by venture capital, that seek to disrupt the mining sector as they have in healthcare, renewable energy, and climate technology. Furthermore, a new wave of digital technology is emerging, one that could reshape current economic, educational, social, and industrial systems through the use of large language models. Whether these changes will have the promised impact remains to be seen, yet the fear of missing out is widespread. Nonetheless, the mining industry should have no fear, only opportunities in employing quantitative methodologies such as artificial intelligence. This paper explores where these opportunities exist and how AI may contribute. My group, Stanford Mineral-X, has been pivotal in a major copper discovery in the Zambian Copperbelt, where AI significantly aided in drilling planning and subsurface model construction. Several AI-focused mineral exploration startups have arisen from this program. This document does not aim to list all possible AI applications in exploration nor provide a comprehensive literature review, as such works are already available. Instead, I will examine AI through the lens of enhancing decision-making based on a novel scientific method. In Part I of this paper, I will outline the key decision-making questions in the exploration process and illustrate how developing a scientific methodology can enhance efficiency. Indeed, a universally accepted scientific method is currently absent in mineral exploration. What exists is a series of tasks carried out by domain experts, each following their own scientific approaches recognized as paradigms in their respective fields. I believe that a comprehensive scientific methodology for mineral exploration is largely lacking, and I intend to argue that this is a critical issue in making exploration more efficient, ultimately reducing the costs of discovery.
