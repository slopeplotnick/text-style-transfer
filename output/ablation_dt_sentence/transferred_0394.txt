Despite its significance, the field still lacks a comprehensive, reproducible, and scalable methodology for quantifying and analyzing the power and energy consumption associated with LLM inference, particularly across varying model sizes, hardware configurations, software stacks, and deployment contexts, ranging from single-node to multi-node environments. Although existing efforts attempt to address this issue, they do not offer a complete solution. For example, the MLPerf Inference benchmark (Reddi et al.) provides a standardized framework for evaluating model inference performance across diverse hardware architectures and deployment configurations. However, the 2020 standard, while valuable, establishes a performance benchmark for a range of machine learning tasks without systematically assessing total power consumption or normalizing energy usage to LLM-specific service units, such as Joules per generated token or per prompt. The MLPerf Power benchmark, as outlined by Tschand et al., offers a critical framework for evaluating power consumption and efficiency. Nonetheless, the 2025 study primarily (i) investigates configurations involving a single computing unit, (ii) examines smaller models compared to the latest large-scale LLMs, such as Llama 3 405B, which have received limited attention in the literature, and (iii) relies on costly, high-precision instruments that pose challenges for replication across various settings. Moreover, current benchmarks frequently overlook real-world adjustable settings, such as batch size, parallel processing, context length, and quantization levels, which are crucial for assessing practical energy and performance trade-offs in system configurations. These configuration settings significantly impact both instantaneous power consumption and the average energy expenditure per token. To address these gaps, we propose TokenPowerBench, a novel and user-friendly benchmarking framework designed to measure power and energy consumption associated with LLM inference. TokenPowerBench features a system that interfaces with vendor-specific tools, such as power sensors for GPU, CPU, and memory, enabling energy consumption monitoring at the node level. Additionally, the system supports optional measurements at the rack or facility level, when available. A unified metrics pipeline aligns each power reading with the two primary inference stages (prefill and decode), enabling precise identification of energy consumption at each phase. We conduct a series of experiments to systematically investigate the relationship between energy consumption and key factors such as batch size, maximum context length, task division, and quantization.
