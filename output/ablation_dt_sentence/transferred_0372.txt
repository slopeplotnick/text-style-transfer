When planning a sequence of measurements, such as determining optimal drilling locations, AI frameworks like Monte Carlo tree search can be utilized to develop the most efficient drilling plan (Mern and Caers, 2023). However, the priorities guiding decision-making will evolve over time. A logical progression can be outlined as follows: initially, one assesses the validity of underlying assumptions; next, the extent or quantity of relevant variables is evaluated; and finally, attention shifts to the qualitative characteristics of these variables. Once all alternative scenarios have been eliminated, the focus should shift to evaluating the quantity of the mineral resource, followed by an assessment of its compositional quality. Alternatively, both factors can be integrated within a quality-versus-quantity framework to facilitate further analysis. Despite the potential of scientific methods to make exploration more efficient and cost-effective, several non-scientific barriers persist within the field. These challenges include ingrained organizational practices, structural issues within the exploration industry, and the lack of integrated software solutions. Companies are structured in such a way that specialists handle tasks based on their training and expertise. Often, the focus is on specific details, without a clear understanding of their relevance to the final decision. Successful implementation of AI requires a shift in large organizations, from a focus on task execution to addressing decision-making challenges. This requires more than just a reorganization; it also demands a process of education. While geologists do not need to become data experts, it is essential for them to gain a deeper understanding of one another's terminologies and frameworks, a topic I will discuss further in the education section. Practitioners still work with a single model through software that perpetuates biased and deterministic interpretations. Over time, these models acquire a sense of authority and truth. As individuals move on, the rationale behind the original interpretation is lost, and the generated geometry is regarded as data rather than a model. In organizational contexts, uncertainty is often viewed as a failure. The assumption is that if an individual spent more time on a problem and exerted more effort, their knowledge would increase, and uncertainty would be reduced.
