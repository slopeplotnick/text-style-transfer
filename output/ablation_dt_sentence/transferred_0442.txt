Large Language Models (LLMs) frequently produce content that, while articulate and coherent, is often factually inaccurate, a phenomenon known as hallucination. These inaccuracies erode confidence in LLMs and hinder their adoption in practical applications. To tackle this issue, two primary approaches have emerged: Hallucination Detection (HD), which examines the internal workings of the language model, and Fact Verification (FV), which assesses the factual accuracy of its output. Despite their shared objective, these approaches have evolved separately, employing divergent methodologies, datasets, and evaluation procedures. This separation has hindered collective progress. In response, we present UniFact, a unified evaluation framework that allows for direct, instance-level comparison between HD and FV by generating model outputs and factuality labels dynamically. Our extensive evaluation of various models and methods has led to three main findings: (1) No single approach is universally superior; (2) HD and FV identify complementary aspects of factual errors; and (3) hybrid approaches that integrate both methods consistently offer superior performance. Additionally, we provide an in-depth analysis of the reasons for their divergence and offer empirical evidence for the necessity of their integration. Our findings suggest a pressing need for a new, integrated research agenda to unify Hallucination Detection and Fact Verification within LLMs. The advent of LLMs has profoundly transformed the digital landscape, enabling advanced information retrieval and the automation of previously unattainable tasks. However, their deployment in critical areas is significantly hampered by the generation of fluent yet factually incorrect content. In fields such as healthcare, legal services, and journalism, where accurate information is vital, these errors can erode trust, disseminate misinformation, and lead to harmful outcomes. Therefore, developing effective mechanisms to identify and address factual inaccuracies is not just a technical challenge but a fundamental requirement for building reliable and intelligent systems.
