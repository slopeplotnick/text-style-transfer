The present study investigates whether a computational simulation of language acquisition can independently identify grammatical structures and lexical categories through unsupervised interaction with Delilah, a language model providing representative samples of adult linguistic input. Once the program, referred to as the daughter model, acquires this grammar, it can use it to communicate with another program, the mother model. Specifically, the daughter agent is capable of generating new sentences based on the acquired grammar, which it then shares with the mother agent during their interaction. If permitted by the experimental configuration, the mother model can provide evaluative feedback on the adequacy of the induced grammar, allowing the daughter model to refine and improve its grammatical representations. Additionally, the daughter model can leverage its acquired knowledge to interpret novel sentences from the mother model, including classifying the types of words used by the mother. This aligns with a well-established finding in corpus linguistics: words fulfilling a grammatical function tend to occur more frequently, while content words, which convey specific meanings, appear less often. Some content words may even appear only once or a few times in a given text, a pattern that reflects Zipf's law. This law posits that the frequency of word usage in a language is inversely proportional to its rank in the list of most frequent terms, with the most common words occurring significantly more often than less frequent ones. This phenomenon has been extensively studied by researchers such as Mandelbrot and Zipf. Moreover, a connection has been made between word distribution in languages and the grammatical categories analyzed by linguists. This paper explores whether a similar relationship can be identified in language generated by a computational system.
