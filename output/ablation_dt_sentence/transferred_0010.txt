Citation quality is evaluated using three key metrics: Recall, Precision, and their harmonic mean, F1. Recall measures how many ground-truth references, sourced from the human-written survey, are correctly included in the generated survey, reflecting the breadth of literature coverage. Precision, on the other hand, assesses whether the references cited in the generated survey align with those in the human reference set, thereby indicating citation accuracy and preventing the inclusion of irrelevant or fabricated sources. In this way, we can directly compare the generated reference list with the human-authored list, providing a clear and interpretable view of how well the automatic system's literature grounding matches human research practices. This approach allows for a systematic comparison of the depth and breadth of literature coverage between automatic and human-written surveys. LLM-as-a-Judge has become a widely adopted framework for evaluating generated text, owing to its scalability, consistency, and low annotation cost. In its typical form, the model is provided with predefined rubrics and score ranges to rate system outputs. However, this rubric-only approach can be overly lenient, particularly for complex tasks such as long-form surveys. To address this, we employ two distinct versions of LLM-as-a-Judge: one with a human-written survey for overall content evaluation, and another that utilizes principles for outline validation. The first configuration, which uses a human-written survey, enhances overall survey quality assessment by providing a high-quality reference for the model. This reference helps the model make relative judgments, rather than relying solely on abstract rubric criteria. The inclusion of a concrete expert example reduces the tendency of the model to overrate vague or superficial content, ensuring stronger contextual anchoring. Moreover, comparing the generated survey to a detailed human counterpart increases the model's sensitivity to higher-level qualities, such as argumentative rigor and evidential support—areas where rubric-only evaluations may fall short. Furthermore, this setup mitigates the score saturation often seen when different systems receive uniformly high marks despite significant quality differences, leading to clearer differentiation and more reliable insights into the strengths and weaknesses of automated survey generation.
