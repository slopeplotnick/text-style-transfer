Referring Expression Comprehension (REC) involves the task of localizing a target entity specified by a natural language description. This area of research is crucial in vision-language tasks, particularly when integrating visual perception with linguistic comprehension. Recent advancements have moved beyond simple surface-level word matching, focusing instead on a deeper understanding of meaning through the inclusion of additional contextual knowledge. Generalized Referring Expression Comprehension (GREC) [8, 7] expands upon this by interpreting descriptions that may refer to no objects or multiple objects, as in the expression "Pick up those three boxes." This shift relaxes the assumptions of referential certainty, better reflecting the complexities of real-world communication, where the referent may be ambiguous, occluded, or absent. However, such research often depends on extensive collections of real-world images paired with human-generated descriptions, a process that is both resource-intensive and costly. Online platforms have been instrumental in reducing the cost and complexity associated with empirical evaluation, providing more efficient access to large datasets. Despite this, the challenge intensifies when models must simultaneously interpret visual scenes, maintain discourse states, and engage in multi-turn interactions. Under these conditions, existing approaches begin to show significant performance degradation. A recent study introduced a novel GREC benchmark based on dialogues within the Minecraft game environment, revealing the difficulties faced by current AI models in effectively handling these tasks. Furthermore, the study highlighted the high cost of generating written versions of these interactions, which limits the availability of training data. To address this issue, this paper proposes a novel approach for generating additional data through the construction of concise descriptions and detailed dialogues, aimed at improving AI comprehension capabilities. While previous research on AI-driven data generation is relevant, applying it to GREC presents unique challenges, particularly in accurately modeling spatial relationships and linking these observations to visual representations.
