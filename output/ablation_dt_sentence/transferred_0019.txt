Our methodology employs a hierarchical model that enables the integration of multiple statistical distributions, each capturing distinct components of the underlying statistical process. This approach reflects our assumption of statistical independence among factors. For instance, different instances of the same hardware platform, such as physical CPUs with identical specifications, exhibit varying levels of energy consumption depending on the instructions executed. However, these variations are independent of one another. Our methodology defines a Worst-Case Energy Consumption (WCEC) model to estimate the total energy usage of a device, as illustrated in Figure 1. This model consists of two components: (1) the measurement component (Section 3.1), which quantifies the energy consumed by the benchmark harness executed on the target hardware, and (2) the modelling component (Section 3.2), which constructs a Bayesian model of the energy consumption characteristics of the programming language. Several statically typed programming languages are designed to run within the JVM ecosystem. To model these languages with a unified approach, we measure the energy associated with bytecode patterns — the transformation of source code into bytecode by the compiler, as shown in Figure 2. Each microbenchmark consists of a single permutation of a source code statement, selected from all possible data types and data sizes relevant to a given operation, as depicted in Figure 3. The measurement cycle for each microbenchmark captures the current, voltage, and execution time of the operation. Each cycle begins by configuring the multimeter for measurement, followed by invoking the garbage collector to eliminate memory-related noise (as detailed in Section 5.1). To account for statistical variability, each microbenchmark cycle is executed 10 times. Furthermore, a split plot design is employed, wherein the execution order of the microbenchmark cycles is randomized to mitigate potential ordering bias.
