To ensure the safe handling of harmful prompts and responses, particularly in offline and on-device settings, it is crucial that devices such as smartphones, laptops, smart assistants, drones, and in-vehicle systems are equipped with the ability to process such interactions independently, without relying on cloud-based infrastructure. This not only reduces latency but also enhances privacy preservation and ensures reliable operation under resource-constrained or deployment-limited conditions. Recent efforts have sought to develop safety models effective across multiple languages; however, these models often struggle to perform adequately, especially for underrepresented languages or those with insufficient support in existing datasets. A common approach to addressing this challenge is to train a model in English or another high-resource language and then evaluate its performance on other languages, leveraging shared linguistic features for cross-lingual transfer. This strategy typically yields better results for languages that are typologically similar or well-represented in the training data, but its effectiveness diminishes for low-resource or typologically distant languages that share fewer linguistic features and are underrepresented in the datasets. To address these limitations, we introduce CREST, a model designed to support 100 languages, consisting of just 0.5 billion parameters and trained on 13 widely supported languages. We employ a multilingual transformer model to cluster languages based on their semantic and structural similarity within a shared representational space. By training the model on a well-supported language, we enhance its performance across other languages within the same cluster, thereby improving generalization for less common languages in the same linguistic family. This approach proves especially valuable in safety-critical applications, where annotated data is scarce or entirely unavailable across many languages. Despite its compact size, CREST outperforms existing models, demonstrating robust performance across both well-resourced and less commonly supported languages. The model achieves cross-lingual generalization by leveraging shared vocabulary structures and script similarities across languages. To the best of our knowledge, no prior work has successfully demonstrated a safety model that performs effectively across such a diverse range of languages.
