This paper discussed how distinct linguistic categories can be acquired by a computational multi-agent language acquisition framework involving an adult language model, Delilah, and a daughter agent. The daughter language model has been developed for the MODOMA framework and employs explicit graph representations of the acquired grammatical knowledge to generate and parse utterances, which become more refined as language acquisition progresses. Conversely, Delilah consists of a parser and generator, developed independently by Cremers and Hijzelendoorn (1995/2024a), to implement a grammar model of Dutch, and employs this model to both produce and analyze utterances. Thus, the MODOMA framework requires a revised methodological approach to research on Delilah: in addition to improving Delilah’s capacity to generate utterances and provide parses, systematic investigation of its output is essential to enable the daughter to identify the structural patterns necessary for acquisition. To this end, analyses and techniques from disciplines such as linguistics, psycholinguistics, and corpus linguistics could be employed to gain deeper insight into the machine-generated language output, in much the same way as they are applied to human-generated language data. Considering the increasing relevance of large language models, evaluating whether machine-generated language exhibits patterns similar to those produced by humans has become an important area of inquiry. This paper presents the results of experiments conducted to explore this approach. Interestingly, the utterances generated by Delilah during interactions with the daughter agent consist of independent exemplars, while corpora typically form a coherent sequence of connected sentences. Nonetheless, similar grammatical patterns can be observed in both types of linguistic data. This may suggest that such patterns are more reflective of the individual utterances within the collections than of the corpora as wholes. Crucially, the study presented in this paper reveals that the divergent frequency distributions of content and functional categories—well established in the analysis of human language data—are likewise attested in Delilah’s output. As Delilah is not configured to reproduce these statistical patterns in its utterances, the observed distribution must be attributed to the underlying grammar model employed.
