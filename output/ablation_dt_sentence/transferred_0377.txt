We propose a method to estimate the potential improvement in a solver's performance when a verifier is employed to aid in selecting the correct answers. To assess the accuracy of this hypothesis, we conduct experiments with various solver-verifier pairs selected from a pool of 12 models, ensuring inclusion of the three smallest models from each of the four distinct groups. For each problem, the solver generates solutions iteratively until the verifier labels one as correct, with a maximum of nine attempts. If a correct answer is not found, the previous attempt is retained. The results obtained, alongside the anticipated improvements, are presented in Figure 4. Although marginal gains are not easily observable under the nine-attempt cap, which limits a comprehensive evaluation, the overall outcomes align with our expectations. Notably, self-reviewing contributes less significantly to the quality of work compared to having it reviewed by an independent party, especially one from a distinct group. The central claim here is that our approach to estimating performance improvements is both effective and reliable, requiring minimal additional methodological overhead. Moreover, solvers tend to favor responses similar to their prior outputs, reflecting a form of self-similarity bias commonly observed in human judgment. Our findings reveal that a comparable phenomenon occurs in the interaction between solvers and verifiers. As previously demonstrated, models with strong reasoning capabilities do not benefit significantly from self-verification. However, their performance improves when assessed by alternative models, revealing a similar bias. The key implication is that increased alignment between the solver and verifier leads the verifier to preferentially accept erroneous solutions, thereby reducing the effective performance gains attributed to verification. Therefore, utilizing a verifier with a distinct solution distribution mitigates this issue.
