The Area Under the Curve (AUC) is employed to evaluate a model’s ranking capability independent of predefined decision thresholds, offering a robust metric particularly well-suited for scenarios marked by class imbalance. In this section, we introduce a unified evaluation framework that integrates Hallucination Detection (HD) and Fact Verification (FV)—two closely related yet historically siloed research directions. We begin by exploring why these paradigms have evolved in isolation and examine the structural incompatibilities in existing benchmarks that have hindered their joint evaluation. We then outline the core design principles of our framework, followed by a detailed explanation of its operational mechanisms. To contextualize our framework, it is essential to first examine the foundational reasons behind the separation of HD and FV. This divide stems from a core misalignment between the text-centric orientation of traditional FV datasets and the model-centric requirements of HD techniques. FV benchmarks such as FEVER are composed of static claim–label pairs, which are agnostic to the claim’s provenance or generation context. This design makes them inherently incompatible with HD methodologies—such as those that leverage internal model states or inter-sample consistency—as such generative signals are entirely absent from FV datasets. Consequently, unified evaluation becomes infeasible under current protocols. One seemingly promising solution is the use of static model outputs generated by large language models, such as those compiled in HELM. While valuable, this approach faces two critical limitations in the fast-moving AI landscape: - **Model Obsolescence**: The rapid pace of LLM development renders static datasets quickly outdated. Outputs from today’s state-of-the-art models (e.g., LLaMA-3) risk becoming obsolete as newer architectures emerge. - **Inability to Evaluate New Models**: Static benchmarks inherently lack the flexibility to evaluate HD methods on unseen or out-of-distribution models. Since HD performance often hinges on specific model behaviors, any framework that cannot accommodate emerging models significantly constrains methodological progress and fails to capture true generalizability.
