To ensure that each (chunk + summary) pair remains within the 512-token limit, we split each article into smaller segments. The summary is then evaluated against each chunk, and the final SummaC score is computed by averaging entailment scores across all chunks, yielding a value in the range [0,1]. To explore the impact of input position on summary generation, we employed heatmap visualizations and conducted a one-way ANOVA. Figure 3 illustrates the mean values of the three evaluation metrics across the six input orders and three stance categories. For each condition, the mean is derived by averaging the metric scores across all article triplets with the same input order and stance. The variation in shading and metric values provides empirical evidence that input positioning significantly influences the similarity patterns in LLM-generated summaries. Specifically, summaries exhibit higher ROUGE-L and BERTScore values when the document is placed first in the sequence, with BERTScore values exceeding those for both Position 2 and Position 3. This suggests that earlier documents contribute more strongly to the model's generated summaries. In addressing RQ1, our findings reveal a consistent pattern across ROUGE-L and BERTScore: the generated summaries align more closely with the first article in the input sequence. It is important to note that the absolute metric values observed in our study differ from those commonly reported in previous summarization research. Unlike studies that evaluate model outputs against human-written reference summaries [G et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2023b], our evaluation directly compares the generated summary to the full original articles. Due to the significant length disparity—summaries averaging 220 words compared to source articles ranging from 300 to 1600 words—the token-overlap and embedding-based similarity scores are naturally diminished. As a result, we focus on relative scores across different input conditions rather than the absolute metric values. Additionally, a methodological constraint arises from the limited availability of metrics specifically designed to assess the similarity between a summary and its source documents. Most existing evaluation frameworks are reference-based, providing few options for measuring source alignment. SummaC, which evaluates sentence-level entailment, yields low scores and shows minimal sensitivity to positional variations. Since abstract summaries omit finer details from source content, entailment-based frameworks penalize unsupported or implicitly inferred statements. Consequently, although the model may prioritize information from the first article, it is unlikely to reproduce its exact phrasing. Therefore, SummaC is unable to reward subtle content biases unless explicit supporting sentences are retained.
