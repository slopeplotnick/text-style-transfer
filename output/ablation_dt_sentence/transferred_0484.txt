We assessed multiple methods, including RL-Trained, Mixture-of-Inputs, CoLaR, and LiteReason, and generated chapters using Qwen 2.5 7B-Instruct based on each method's plans. We then conducted pairwise evaluations with participants to analyze the comparative performance of these outputs, detailed further in Appendix E. Initially, we performed evaluations on the Flawed Fictions and NCP tasks, with results shown in Table 4. Additionally, LiteReason was benchmarked against two baseline models, both derived from the Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct: a prompted default model and an RL-trained version, neither incorporating latent reasoning. Interestingly, LiteReason without reasoning prompts (RP) extended the reasoning process length and performed worse than the RL-Trained baseline. Table 7 further compares models with and without implicit-thought prompts. Moreover, we explored the frequency of latent thoughts used throughout training to ascertain RL's impact on their occurrence. We observed a marked increase in latent-thought usage at the beginning of RL training, which tapered off by its conclusion. Future research could investigate enhancing latent reasoning by increasing implicit-token markers or altering the prompt. LiteReason's performance improvements were modest—0.8% on Flawed Fictions and 58% on NCP—likely due to its similarity in approach to the RL-Trained baseline while exploring novel reasoning paths. Furthermore, training-free methods like Mixture-of-Inputs (MoI) and Soft Thinking yielded performance levels close to the untrained model. On the NCP task, Soft Thinking slightly outperformed the baseline but with increased token count, while MoI performed worse with fewer tokens. On Flawed Fictions, performance deviations from the baseline were within the confidence intervals. Thus, more research is needed to consistently enhance the efficiency and performance of training-free approaches across varying tasks and reasoning styles.
