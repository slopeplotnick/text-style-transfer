Our results demonstrate that prompt-engineered LLMs, when combined with Isolation Forest scores, can outperform traditional JETs and ML baselines by offering both strong anomaly detection and natural-language rationales. While challenges remain around false positives, cost, and real-world deployment, this study highlights the promise of AI-augmented auditing where accuracy and interpretability are jointly optimized. Importantly, we provide the first empirical evidence that LLMs can be effectively applied to tax-related ledger data, a high-stakes domain where transparency and trust are critical. Our findings suggest that integrating LLMs with classical anomaly signals offers a practical path forward, balancing precision, recall, and explainability. Future work will extend these insights to larger, more diverse datasets and explore robustness to prompt variation and model updates. **Data scope and labels.** Our evaluation is limited to a single anonymized tax ledger and a synthetic benchmark dataset. However, real-world ledgers vary widely across industries, systems, and jurisdictions, and obtaining ground-truth labels remains a significant challenge. Pseudo-labels derived from JETs may reflect embedded rule-based biases, while synthetic anomalies are simulator-defined. Therefore, establishing external validity will require evaluation on larger, heterogeneous datasets with authentic audit labels. **Model stability and explainability.** LLM performance is highly sensitive to input phrasing and model architecture: even minor prompt changes can produce divergent outcomes, and we observe variation across model families. Although our AuditCopilot design reduces variance, it remains vulnerable to paraphrasing, adversarial inputs, and shifts in model behavior over time. Similarly, while auditors may find natural-language explanations helpful, these may not always faithfully reflect the model’s true decision boundaries. **Deployment and governance.** While preliminary results are promising, applying LLMs in financial audits introduces several risks, including hallucinations, biases, prompt sensitivity, and changes in model weights over time. These concerns underscore the need for robust human oversight, clear access controls, and governance protocols. We emphasize that our approach is intended as a decision support tool—not a replacement for human judgment—and should be deployed with appropriate safeguards in real-world settings.
