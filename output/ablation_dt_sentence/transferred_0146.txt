As discussed in the introduction, systems that evolve without external guidance tend to plateau rapidly and become unstable, often resulting in a collapse of diversity and the emergence of reward manipulation strategies. Our empirical findings clearly reflect these dynamics. In the left panel, R-Zero demonstrates a marked decline in question diversity early in training, falling from 35 to below 20 within the first 50 steps. Although the 2-gram diversity score rises beyond 50 steps, this increase coincides with a sharp escalation in question length, as shown in the middle panel, indicating that the diversity gain is primarily a length-induced artifact rather than true semantic expansion. This occurs because longer questions inherently produce more unique n-gram combinations, thereby inflating the diversity metric without corresponding conceptual variety. A similar trend appears in the question length trajectory: R-Zero steadily elongates its outputs, ultimately generating excessively long questions. This behavior suggests the exploitation of superficial heuristics—most notably verbosity—to inflate perceived diversity or difficulty without introducing meaningful variation. In contrast, R-Few preserves consistent diversity throughout training while maintaining stable question length, suggesting that minimal human input can effectively mitigate diversity collapse. Turning to the right panel, we observe that both methods yield increasingly difficult questions over time; however, this similarity conceals crucial qualitative differences. To assess difficulty, we relabel generated questions at various training stages using Gemini-2.5-Pro and evaluate them by measuring how often Qwen3-8B-Base answers them incorrectly—that is, the inverse of its accuracy. While both curves trend upward, indicating rising difficulty, the underlying causes diverge significantly. R-Zero achieves this increase largely by inflating question length, rather than by requiring deeper reasoning, reflecting yet another form of reward hacking that boosts apparent complexity without genuine cognitive advancement. By contrast, R-Few maintains consistent question length, enabling the model to evolve toward truly more challenging questions without compromising structural integrity or diversity.
