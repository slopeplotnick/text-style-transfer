Except for Study 1, all simulated datasets successfully replicated the factor structure of their corresponding scale (H1). Similarly, M-CFA shows that simulated participants and real-world ones respond to the scales in comparable ways (H2). These results align with previous research on group-level simulated data (Argyle et al., 2023; Hewitt et al., 2024). Notably, Studies 3 and 4 achieve higher levels of measurement invariance between datasets compared to Studies 1 and 2, suggesting that scales developed from simulated participants may generalize better to human subjects than the reverse. One interpretation of this finding is that, when responding, LLMs tend to process scale items with a more standardized approach, exhibiting less susceptibility to random variations and biases stemming from study conditions and population characteristics. As a result, factor structures derived from simulated samples may exhibit greater semantic generalizability to external samples than those derived from real-world data. Further research is needed to investigate the factors influencing the different ways LLMs and human participants interpret and respond to survey items. While CFA consistently produces favorable results, simulated samples fail to replicate pairwise correlations (H3), distributional properties (H4), and variance (H5) observed in real-world data. Taken together, these results indicate that although both datasets share the same factor structure, their score distributions differ significantly across all measured dimensions. Therefore, while group-level datasets and their underlying structures are robust enough to support pilot testing, individual-level synthetic data do not exhibit similar adequacy for this purpose. These limitations are consistent with the findings of Petrov and colleagues (2024). It is also worth noting that, with the exception of Study 1, Studies 2, 3, and 4 present similar results. This divergence can be attributed to several factors: first, the factorial structure of Study 1 was determined using a different extraction method (minres) compared to other scales, which may have led to less distinct response patterns (Sass & Schmitt, 2010). Second, this scale employs a 5-point Likert scale for responses, whereas other scales use 6-point (Study 2) or 7-point formats.
