We reassess the robustness of pretrained VLA models and demonstrate that performance degradation under visual perturbations primarily results from shifts in spatial representations. To address this, we introduce a unified one-shot robustness adaptation framework that combines Feature Token Modulation (FTM) and Feature Linear Adaptation (FLA), allowing for efficient recalibration of visual representations with minimal parameter updates. Our approach achieves state-of-the-art generalization to unseen camera viewpoints and visual perturbations on the Libero benchmark, revealing previously untapped robustness potential in existing VLA models. VLA models aim to integrate visual perception, language grounding, and action generation into a single policy framework. Extending pretrained vision-language models to the robotic domain, recent VLAs such as RT-2, Pi0, PaLM-E, Octo, and OpenVLA exhibit strong in-distribution generalization across diverse manipulation tasks. These models leverage large-scale embodiment datasets to integrate varied visual inputs, language instructions, and proprioceptive states, alongside noisy actions, to generate a shared action policy that enables robots to perform versatile behaviors. However, despite these advancements, recent studies show that even state-of-the-art VLAs, after pre-training and fine-tuning on extensive datasets, remain surprisingly brittle under distribution shifts. Performance degrades significantly when exposed to unseen camera viewpoints, novel lighting conditions, or scene noise—limitations that persist even in models trained on millions of demonstrations. Thus, addressing this brittleness is critical for deploying VLA systems in real-world environments, where visual complexities far exceed those found in controlled Libero settings. Enhancing robustness to visual perturbations has long been a challenge in improving VLA performance. Previous work has explored data-centric strategies to increase viewpoint invariance, such as domain randomization, multi-view demonstrations, or large-scale visual perturbation datasets. While these approaches are effective, they rely heavily on extensive data collection or manual annotation, limiting their scalability.
