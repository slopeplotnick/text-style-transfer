Thinking large language models (LLMs) are increasingly being employed as automated judges for evaluating the outputs of other generative systems, a paradigm known as "Thinking-LLM-as-a-Judge" (Saha et al., 2025). This approach provides a scalable and cost-effective alternative to traditional human evaluation, which is often time-consuming and resource-intensive. To mitigate the inherent stochasticity and noise of single-instance judgments, a prevalent strategy is to leverage significant inference-time compute by generating multiple independent predictions and evaluations for each item under consideration. However, the reliability of the final decision critically depends on how these diverse outputs are aggregated. Existing methods—such as majority voting, confidence-based heuristics, or LLM-generated aggregators—are often brittle and statistically suboptimal, especially in the presence of ties. For instance, simple vote aggregation fails to distinguish between a narrowly split 5–4 outcome and a unanimous 9–0 decision, thereby discarding crucial information about the strength of evidence encoded in the vote distribution. This lack of sensitivity to evidential robustness undermines the reliability and interpretability of model-based judgments. In this work, we contend that aggregation is not a mere post-processing step but a fundamental component in effectively harnessing inference-time compute. To that end, we propose a principled Distribution-Calibrated Aggregation framework that transcends naïve vote-counting by operating directly on the complete counts of positive, negative, and tie votes, thus preserving the full informational content of the evaluation samples. Our method adopts a Bradley-Terry-Davidson model (Davidson, 1970) to jointly parameterize both the relative strength of preference and the global tendency toward ties. Parameters are estimated via maximum likelihood on a small validation set, and at inference, we employ the Mean Absolute Error (MAE) Bayes action to ensure alignment with the target evaluation metric. This probabilistic calibration avoids loss-metric mismatch and yields more robust and accurate judgments.
