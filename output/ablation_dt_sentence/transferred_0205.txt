To systematically address this question, we decompose the task into two interrelated components, as detailed in Section 2. First, we construct semantic mappings between the informational requirements expressed in the query and the corresponding product attributes, thereby establishing interpretable paths that bridge query intent with item features. Subsequently, we follow these paths to perform concrete discrimination at the attribute-product level. To support this process, the model must possess three core capabilities. (1) *Knowledge and Reasoning Ability.* The model must first utilize domain-specific knowledge to interpret technical terminology and infer implicit meanings within the query. Building upon this, reasoning mechanisms enable the model to uncover the user's true intent and identify the derived item attributes. As illustrated in Fig. 1(a), the model must recognize that the phrase "morning C, evening A" denotes a regimen involving Vitamin C and retinol, and deduce that "for mom" signals the suitability of this routine for middle-aged women. (2) *Multi-modal Matching Ability.* Since product attributes span across textual and visual modalities, the model must align the inferred query intent with multi-modal attribute representations. As shown in Fig. 1(b), when the query specifies "yellow," yet this feature is absent in the textual description, the model must instead rely on visual cues from the product image to identify the color. (3) *Rule-Aware Ability.* Beyond attribute matching, the model is required to comply with pre-established decision rules to ensure consistent and principled inference. As illustrated in Fig. 1(c), for a query like "LV bags" that returns a second-hand item, relevance cannot be resolved purely through feature alignment and thus must adhere to rule-based criteria to determine appropriateness.
