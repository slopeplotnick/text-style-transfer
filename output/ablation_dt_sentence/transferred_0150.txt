Overall, the strongest models—GPT-5 Mini, Claude 3.7 Sonnet, and GPT-4o—achieve exceptionally low error rates, comparable to or exceeding human performance (left panel, Fig. 8). With the exception of Gemini 2.5 Flash Lite, the left panel of Figure 8 shows a general trend: more accurate models tend to exhibit stronger evidence of Bayesian behaviour. However, this relationship is empirical rather than necessary. For instance, a model that perfectly predicts all stimuli may resemble a simple linear model and fail to exhibit any probabilistic characteristics. Under steering ablation, when a sequence of prior observations is introduced into the context prompt, LLM behaviour varies substantially. As shown in the top right panel of Figure 6, evidence for sequential behaviour diminishes markedly. In particular, when a deliberately biased numerical range is provided, as illustrated in the left panel of Figure 6, error rates increase (triangle icons typically correspond to higher NRMSE than circles). This pattern aligns with the observation that LLMs heavily rely on the in-context information they receive. From the middle panel of Figure 8, it is clear that strong NRMSE performance does not necessarily imply efficient cue combination. GPT-5 Mini, despite its strong NRMSE results, displays poor cue combination efficiency. This shortcoming is especially evident in the maze distance estimation task, where GPT-5 Mini performs nearly perfectly in the text modality but significantly worse in the image modality. This suggests that an optimal Bayesian combination would require the model to further downweight image input—something GPT-5 Mini fails to do to the necessary extent (see Appendix A.12 for more detail). By contrast, in the line length ratio task (bottom right panel, Fig. 6), many LLMs succeed in downweighting the image modality in noisy conditions, indicating a Bayes-consistent adaptation.
