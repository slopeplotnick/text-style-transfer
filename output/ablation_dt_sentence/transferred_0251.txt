When conceptualizing a story, individuals may start with a specific event, shaping the traits and development of the entities associated with that event. Alternatively, one might begin with an entity and construct a series of events around it. E2, a doctoral researcher in literary studies, argues that narratives can be deconstructed into hierarchical units, including story outlines, sequences, propositions, and parts of speech. By progressively combining these components in reverse order, a coherent narrative can ultimately be constructed. The parts of speech—such as an individual, an action, or an attribute—can form a proposition. Several propositions can then be organized into a sequence, and multiple sequences can form a complete narrative. However, merely assembling these components does not yield a compelling story. The elements that bind these components together are relationships, which play a crucial role in ensuring the coherence and functional integration of the overall narrative structure. Interviews with two experts revealed that a story consists of four primary constructs: entities, events, relationships, and structural outlines. During our discussions with the experts, we explored these components in greater detail, noting that their interpretations may vary depending on the context. Events are central to a narrative; however, their specific details may not always be explicitly conveyed through the narrative structure. While events may exhibit a degree of relative autonomy, it is the patterns of interaction between events that create narrative tension and contribute to the construction of meaning. Moreover, the presence and identity of entities, along with the nature of their relationships, significantly influence the events in a story. In addition, information about entities and their relationships may be systematically analyzed as distinct components.
