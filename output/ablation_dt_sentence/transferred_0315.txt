Legal and ethical concerns surrounding generative models have been intensifying, particularly regarding the use of copyrighted content in their training. At the heart of these concerns is the issue of authorship and moral rights, especially the right to be recognized as the author of one’s work. This right, often described as "the most moral of rights" by Ginsburg, is central to the ongoing debates. Numerous lawsuits argue that training on copyrighted material without consent constitutes a violation of property rights. For example, the New York Times has filed a lawsuit against OpenAI for the unauthorized use of its journalistic archives, while Getty Images has taken legal action against Stability AI for generating content derived from its proprietary database. Beyond these lawsuits, artists and performers have voiced significant concerns. Notably, Elton John and Dua Lipa, along with more than 200 other artists, signed an open letter urging technology companies to protect human creativity from AI systems capable of replicating their voices and styles without consent. In response to these concerns, policy bodies and international organizations have started to propose new governance frameworks. The European Union’s AI Act outlines rules for transparency in AI systems, while the United Kingdom’s National AI Strategy and UNESCO’s ethical guidelines advocate for responsible AI, emphasizing privacy and intellectual property protections. Furthermore, Denmark has recently proposed an amendment to its copyright law, which would grant individuals legal rights over their voice, face, and bodily likeness. This marks the first such initiative of its kind in Europe and aims to address legal challenges related to AI’s ability to replicate personal characteristics. As the Danish culture minister emphasized, this initiative ensures the right to one’s own body, voice, and facial features, underscoring the need for identity protection in the context of generative AI. Despite these efforts, most generative models remain opaque, offering limited insight into how training data influence the generated output. Even open-source models, despite being accessible, are of such a scale that exact replication is unfeasible. Existing methods, such as influence functions or retraining-based attribution, require access to the internal mechanisms of the model.
