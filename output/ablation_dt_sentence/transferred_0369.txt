Empirical observations reveal that the ordering of input articles significantly influences the content of the generated summaries. Specifically, large language models (LLMs) exhibit a consistent bias toward the first article, affecting both the framing of the information and the degree of semantic similarity between the generated summaries and the original articles. Previous research has documented similar position bias in LLMs when processing long contextual inputs, and our results extend this finding to multi-document settings, showing that the first article receives disproportionate attention during information ranking. This primacy effect is consistently captured by BERTScore, which reveals a marked preference for the initial article across all evaluations. The phenomenon arises because LLMs tend to prioritize lexical and argumentative content from the first article, leading to its overrepresentation in the generated output. As a result, token-level contextualized embeddings yield higher similarity scores for the first article. In contrast, surface-level overlap metrics such as ROUGE-L, which focus on word-level similarity, are less sensitive to these biases, especially in abstractive summaries. Additionally, SummaC, which evaluates factual consistency, does not capture the shifts in framing that characterize the primacy effect. Consequently, these methods exhibit reduced sensitivity to first-article bias compared to BERTScore. Across all analytical perspectives, Position 1 consistently exhibits a stronger effect than Position 2 and Position 3, with no significant distinction between the latter two. This suggests that the bias arises from the model’s prioritization of the first article, rather than a gradual decrease in importance across the sequence. Such bias can have tangible consequences, particularly in real-world applications like the AI Overviews feature in Google Search, powered by Gemini models. This feature may inadvertently prioritize content from earlier-ranked articles, potentially neglecting later content. As a result, summaries may reflect skewed perspectives, influencing user comprehension, particularly among those who rely heavily on AI-generated summaries.
