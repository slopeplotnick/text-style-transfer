Overall, these findings indicate that reasoning-based safety alignment not only enhances the efficacy of omnimodal guardrails across different modality settings but also facilitates deeper understanding and generalization in complex cross-modal safety scenarios where label-only supervision may fall short. To assess whether safety knowledge learned from seen modalities can effectively generalize to unseen ones, we conducted cross-modal training and evaluation. Specifically, for each experimental setup, OMNIGUARD was trained using data from three modalities for training and seen modality evaluation, intentionally leaving one modality out for evaluating unseen modalities. The average F1 scores and accuracy across all four seen and unseen modality evaluations are presented in Table 5. From these experiments, we draw two main conclusions. First, strong cross-modal transferability is evident across all four modalities, as the performance on unseen modalities closely mirrors that on seen modalities (with average F1 scores of 79.4 versus 81.8). This suggests that OMNIGUARD successfully learns modality-invariant safety representations, effectively aligning harmful semantic patterns across text, image, video, and audio inputs, while acquiring a generalizable safety reasoning capacity across these modalities. However, one notable exception emerges in the audio modality, where the seen modality F1 score (64.6%) slightly underperforms compared to the unseen modality F1 score (65.1%). This phenomenon can be attributed to the degradation of the OMNIGUARD variant's performance in the WildGuard-TTS benchmark when trained without textual data. WildGuardMix-TTS represents the auditory version of WildGuardMix, constructed by a text-to-speech model. Moreover, training without textual data leads to challenges in the audio setting, revealing that content which is semantically equivalent but transmitted through different modalities (such as harmful text versus its spoken version) can mutually influence each other during safety alignment. Thus, knowledge encapsulated in one form can indeed be transferred to another semantically invariant form.
