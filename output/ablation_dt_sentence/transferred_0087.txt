A closer examination of the results for each sample reveals that, in some cases, selecting a different resolution yields more accurate outcomes compared to using a fixed resolution of 112, as shown in Figure 3. The image crops selected at different resolutions, together with the visualization of their semantic similarity maps, provide an intuitive understanding of this phenomenon. Since the complete object is divided into multiple segments, some parts exhibit higher semantic similarity, as measured by VisRAG, while others show lower similarity. After filtering, only the segments with higher similarity are retained. However, this compromises the integrity of the target object, which interferes with the MLLM’s judgment. To mitigate this issue, we propose a novel framework called Multi-resolution Retrieval Detection (MRD). The core design of MRD lies in its use of multiple resolutions across different scales to better localize regions containing the target objects. This enables the search process to more effectively identify image crops corresponding to the objects of interest, removing irrelevant distractions and enhancing MLLMs’ perceptual understanding of high-resolution images. Building on the findings in subsection 3.2, we argue that computing semantic similarity at multiple resolutions is more effective for objects of varying sizes and spatial positions. Motivated by this observation, we introduce a simple yet effective method called Multi-resolution Semantic Fusion, which computes semantic similarity maps at different local resolutions and applies a fusion strategy to refine and improve similarity accuracy. To directly locate the target objects, we incorporate an open-vocabulary object detection model that scans the entire high-resolution image using a sliding window approach, generating confidence scores for regions likely to contain the target. By integrating these detection scores with the multi-resolution semantic similarity maps, our method not only enhances the localization of relevant regions but also captures fine-grained differences among image crops, thereby aiding subsequent search processes in identifying key areas with greater precision.
