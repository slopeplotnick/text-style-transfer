Enhanced taxonomic frameworks acknowledge that, while robots with specialized capabilities are deployed to perform complex tasks, construction environments present distinct MRTA challenges. These challenges differentiate construction sites from more controlled settings, where traditional approaches have proven effective. Construction sites are characterized by unstructured layouts, dynamic material flows, unpredictable weather conditions, and intricate interdependencies between various trades, all of which contribute to their inherent complexity. Moreover, these environments require allocation frameworks that can manage spatial constraints, temporal dependencies, and safety-critical operations. Furthermore, construction applications demand continuous human oversight, which cannot be predetermined or easily encoded in mathematical formulations. As a result, these construction-specific challenges necessitate adaptive coordination methodologies capable of handling dynamic conditions and unstructured environments. Traditional optimization approaches have sought to address these challenges through a variety of algorithmic strategies. Classical optimization methods serve as the foundation of established multi-robot coordination strategies, providing mathematical rigor and performance assurances through various computational techniques. Kuhn (1955) laid the theoretical groundwork for optimal assignment problems with the development of the Hungarian method. This approach demonstrated that bipartite matching between tasks and resources could be efficiently solved using linear programming, offering polynomial time complexity guarantees. However, the Hungarian method was limited to simple one-to-one assignments and was insufficient for addressing heterogeneous robot capabilities or task dependencies. In construction environments, robots possess specialized capabilities, such as heavy lifting and precision assembly, and workflows require coordinated task sequences with explicit dependencies. These limitations prompted the development of more advanced optimization methodologies. Atay and Bayazit (2006) introduced mixed-integer linear programming (MILP) formulations for multi-robot task allocation, incorporating robot capability constraints, task precedence relationships, and communication limitations. Military and aerospace domains further advanced MILP-based coordination through sophisticated formulations that explicitly account for heterogeneous capabilities and stringent temporal constraints. MILP offers substantial advantages in modeling complex multi-robot scenarios with explicit constraint satisfaction. However, MILP approaches rely on centralized optimization architectures, which encounter scalability issues when coordinating large robot teams. Construction projects typically involve numerous robots distributed across expansive, disordered sites with limited communication infrastructure, making centralized coordination impractical. These scalability limitations led to the adoption of distributed coordination methods.
