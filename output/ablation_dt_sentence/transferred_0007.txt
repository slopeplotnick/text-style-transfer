While model innovation continues to advance, many practical deployments hinge on choices that are often underexplored in academic literature: how we define virality, what signals are feasible to collect (e.g., textual vs. numeric/metadata), and which evaluation protocols best reflect real-world constraints such as accessibility, time limitations, and class imbalance. This paper adopts an evaluation-first stance on these critical design decisions. **Contribution.** We prioritize evaluation and demonstrate, across two recent datasets, that choices in label schema and input modalities (text-only vs. text+signals) can rival or even exceed the impact of architectural modifications. We release reproducible baselines that make these trade-offs explicit. While our emphasis lies on evaluation design rather than model novelty, we show that lightweight architectures can match or outperform recent multimodal and graph-based approaches on standard benchmarks such as FAKENewsNET (see Section 5). Specifically, we examine two distinct tasks: fake-news detection and early virality prediction. Which signals and definitions are most crucial for maintaining stable performance under realistic conditions? To answer this, we compare textual representations (e.g., sentence-level transformer embeddings) with lightweight numeric signals, including user-level statistics, early engagement metrics, and temporal features. These tasks are evaluated on datasets with different structural configurations: one based on self-contained news articles, the other comprising temporally ordered sequences of social media posts. We consider two supervised classification tasks across two benchmark datasets, resulting in four experimental conditions: (i) fake-news detection and (ii) virality prediction on EvONS (Krstovski et al., 2022) and FAKENewsNET (Shu et al., 2020). **T1: Fake-news detection.** Given an item—whether a news article or a tweet series—the goal is to assign a binary veracity label (true or false). This task is applied to both EvONS articles and FakeNewsNet tweet series, with results reported using standard classification metrics. **FAKENewsNET.** We adopt the tweet-series configuration provided by the public repository. The data are anonymized via identifiers and require API hydration. The Politifact split was obtained through prior academic sharing; however, the GossipCop dataset was unavailable due to current access constraints. Each instance consists of a series of tweets referring to the same news article, capturing its dissemination trajectory.
