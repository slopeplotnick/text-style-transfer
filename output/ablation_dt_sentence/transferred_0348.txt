To evaluate how well subject identity is maintained during the editing process, we compute the mean cosine similarity between face embeddings extracted by a pre-trained ArcFace model for each frame and the first edited frame. This measure quantifies how well the individual’s appearance and facial features are preserved, with higher values indicating stronger identity consistency. Moreover, a user study confirmed that our method is perceptually preferred, particularly for its visual fidelity and temporal alignment. Full details of the study and all quantitative metric formulations are provided in the supplementary material. As shown in Table 1, text-based methods like LucyEdit achieve high performance on certain synchronization metrics, such as Speech Corr. (0.80 vs. 0.72 for our method). However, this comes at the expense of failing to apply the visual edit effectively, as evidenced by its low Directional CLIP score of 0.11 (vs. our 0.21). Our method achieves a superior balance, performing strongly on synchronization while also maintaining high fidelity in the visual edit. In contrast, VACE benefits from strong external conditioning, including pose, canny, and depth, which enhances CLIP-based alignment. However, it demonstrates weaker temporal consistency and identity preservation. Sync-LoRA, which is explicitly optimized for synchronized editing, excels at preserving strong temporal coherence while executing precise edits that maintain subject identity. This illustrates how our approach effectively balances motion consistency and edit fidelity, rather than excelling in just one aspect. In Figure 6, we present experiments where we omit one synchronization factor at a time to assess its contribution. We train models on datasets where specific factors—such as speech, gaze, blinking, or pose—are excluded by setting the respective weight to zero in our filtering score. In the first example (top two rows), excluding pose results in visible errors in head orientation. In the second example (bottom two rows), omitting any factor introduces issues such as spatial drift and temporal misalignment in gaze or blinking. Omitting any factor leads to degraded synchronization, particularly affecting the mouth motion in the man and the eye motion in the woman, thus confirming that all four factors contribute collaboratively to complete motion information.
