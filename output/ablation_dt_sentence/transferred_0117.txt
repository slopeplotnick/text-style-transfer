Multi-agent autonomy emerges through the integration of LLMs with orchestration layers, shared memories, and external tools. Frameworks such as AutoGen and CAMEL instantiate role-based dialogues—planner, critic, executor—within a shared operational environment. Newer systems, such as SWE-agent and Voyager, extend this principle by establishing continuous learning loops where agents generate tasks, evaluate results, and adapt strategies. The result is not a rigid pipeline but an adaptive ecology: the output of each model reshapes the context for the next, creating a feedback loop that accumulates over time. Even when humans are nominally involved, their authority remains delayed, indirect, and constrained. Traditional safety checks assume that agent behavior is observable and understandable; however, in large-scale multi-agent systems, these assumptions break down, necessitating new approaches to ensure robust safety evaluations. Moreover, communication in these systems is often untyped or semantically opaque—manifesting as free-form natural language, JSON payloads, or API calls lacking formal structure or interpretability guarantees. When meaning becomes endogenous to the network, traceability diminishes, not because data are absent, but because interpretation itself becomes unstable. As a result, technical interventions are increasingly targeting this loss of visibility. Multi-agent sandboxes now record interaction graphs and compute metrics such as divergence, semantic coherence, influence centrality, and consensus rate to characterize system-level behavior. Similarly, the Inspect framework developed by the UK AI Security Institute augments each message with provenance metadata, enabling the post hoc reconstruction of causal chains. These advancements mark a shift from output-level verification to topology-level auditing, refocusing the assessment from rule violation to tracing how information propagates through the network. As scale increases, the concept of autonomy undergoes a transformation. In supervised settings, autonomy is defined as the ability to take initiative—acting without explicit human guidance. In multi-agent systems, autonomy becomes relational: agents collaborate, compete, and infer each other's intentions. Even when initialized with aligned objectives, slight divergences in reward structures or contextual framing can drive polarization, strategic collusion, or unbounded escalation. Consequently, autonomy is no longer a property of individual agents; instead, it emerges as a collective property of the network.
