Experiment 2 demonstrated that a single system prompt was sufficient to enhance performance across all evaluated languages. This improvement points to a systematic change in the model’s behavior. To further understand how the model's output has changed, we analyze the intermediate reasoning processes and examine how alterations in specific reasoning behaviors correlate with performance across different languages. Specifically, we investigate whether prompt optimization influences the model’s language preference. We began by analyzing the linguistic elements employed in the reasoning chains generated by the model. As shown in Figure 7, even when using the same set of random prompts, the model exhibits varying language preferences depending on the language of the input query. These variations are linked to the availability of training data for each respective language. For instance, due to the abundance of Chinese-language data in model families like Qwen, the model responds to Chinese tasks in Chinese almost as frequently as it does in English. Following prompt optimization, these language preferences become even more pronounced: the model now predominantly generates responses in the language of the input query, rather than defaulting to English as observed with the system prompt. However, for languages with limited training data, such as Hindi, the proportion of English responses remains largely unchanged after optimization. Moreover, our findings suggest that languages other than the task language and English do not significantly contribute to model performance, as their influence diminishes once the prompts are optimized. Further details regarding language identification are provided in Appendix A.9, with the corresponding data presented in Table 6 and Table 7. In Figure 8, we explore the distribution of reasoning behavior types across different languages. While the overall patterns are broadly consistent, certain languages exhibit distinct preferences. For example, English tends to favor Reframing, Chinese exhibits a preference for Logical Reasoning, and Spanish leans towards calculation. In contrast, languages with limited training data, such as Hindi, show a higher proportion of the Other category, indicating insufficient exposure to structured reasoning examples during training. Although these trends are consistent, they seem to reflect language-specific patterns in model pretraining, rather than being directly influenced by prompt design.
