This issue cannot be observed when evaluating a single model in isolation. Rather, it arises from the structure and dynamics of multi-agent interaction, which can degrade safety, reliability, or accountability at the system level. We identify three preliminary dimensions that may help locate the origin of such failures. These are not exhaustive and may be supplemented as testing progresses. – **Interaction topology**: the structure through which agents communicate and exert influence on one another. – **Cognitive opacity**: the degree to which agents' internal reasoning or semantic intent becomes inaccessible or uninterpretable. – **Objective divergence**: the extent to which agents' optimization goals begin to differ. Together, these factors predict the emergence and form of collective risks. Each dimension can be translated into measurable indicators and studied in controlled multi-agent environments. Topology governs how information propagates through the system. While highly connected networks support rapid coordination, they also accelerate the spread of errors and unintended behaviors. Conversely, sparse connectivity may slow down error diffusion but hinders collective correction and coordinated recovery. In practice, the organization of information flow determines how isolated mistakes—such as a flawed inference or misleading summary—can evolve into widely accepted but inaccurate beliefs. Metrics like *contagion velocity* (time for an error to reach half the agents) and *misalignment diffusion* (proportion of agents adopting a risky behavior) offer ways to quantify these dynamics. Safety benchmarks for multi-agent systems must therefore incorporate topological factors, as these structural features significantly influence interaction outcomes. Cognitive opacity refers to the extent to which the reasoning behind an agent’s outputs remains hidden. This may arise inadvertently—through overfitting to interaction patterns—or strategically, when optimization for compliance suppresses authentic reasoning. In such settings, one model may accept another’s assertions without scrutiny, even if they are flawed. The *intent-opacity rate*—the fraction of outputs whose rationale cannot be reconstructed from available traces—provides a starting measure of this transparency loss.
