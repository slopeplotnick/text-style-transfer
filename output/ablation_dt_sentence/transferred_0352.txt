In this context, we begin by examining the limitations of the recent approach presented in [16]. As shown in Figure 1(a), the model’s attention initially spans across both audio and visual inputs, but as reasoning progresses, it gradually shifts toward the text it generates. Consequently, the model anchors its subsequent reasoning on the emotional cues present in the initial text. Typically, the reasoning process starts with generating a description of the video, as the training data are structured such that visual descriptions precede audio descriptions, followed by the reasoning phase. However, this visual-first approach may not always capture the true emotional driver, particularly in cases where the emotion is conveyed more effectively through auditory cues or the semantics of the words themselves. When the initial visual reasoning is inaccurate or irrelevant, the model’s subsequent reasoning becomes progressively misaligned, as demonstrated in Figure 1(b). This highlights the disproportionate significance of the initial step in the reasoning process. Similar challenges arise in other domains, where early cues misdirect reasoning away from the core evidence [17]. To address this issue, we introduce Modality-Importance-Guided Reasoning (MIGR), a method designed to initiate reasoning from the modality most strongly linked to the target emotion, ensuring that emotion-coherent reasoning is maintained throughout the process. Specifically, we propose Modality Importance (MI) estimation, a technique that identifies the most crucial modality for emotion recognition by evaluating the model’s performance across audio-only, visual-only, and audio-visual inputs. For instance, when the model successfully predicts the emotion using audio-only input but fails with visual-only or audio-visual inputs, this indicates that the visual modality may be irrelevant or even misleading, making audio the dominant modality. Building on the MI concept and recent advancements in reasoning-based MLLM training paradigms [15, 16], we propose a two-stage learning framework that includes (i) modality-aligned Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) and (ii) modality-aligned reward optimization. In the SFT stage, we leverage the MI to segment the reasoning text based on input type and reorder the segments accordingly.
