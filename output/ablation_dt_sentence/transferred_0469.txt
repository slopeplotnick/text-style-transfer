Probabilistic programming languages. Probabilistic programming languages (PPL) are languages equipped with first-class constructs for managing probabilistic elements, such as distributions, samplers, and probabilistic estimation and assertions. There are numerous popular examples of these languages, spanning imperative [Bingham et al. 2018; McCallum et al. 2009; Sampson et al. 2014] and functional paradigms [Dash et al. 2023; Goodman et al. 2008; Scontras et al. 2021]. However, a key limitation of current languages is their inadequate adaptation to text-intensive graphs, which are crucial data structures for specifying language model systems (LMS), leading to the design of LUMOS. These languages tend to be generative, based on user-specified probability distributions. For instance, [Fremont et al. 2019] employs PPL to generate random images or scenes in contexts like autonomous vehicle navigation using user-defined scene graphs [Chang et al. 2023]. In contrast, to design specifications applicable to the real world, we ground these with a fixed underlying graph from which subgraphs are sampled to define scenarios. DSLs for LLMs are query languages devoid of probabilistic constructs that provide interfaces for crafting, optimizing, and securing prompts to achieve a desirable response from a target LMS [Beurer-Kellner et al. 2023; Khattab et al. 2023; Okuda and Amarasinghe 2023; Sharma et al. 2024]. They complement LUMOS, whose programs assess the desirability of LMS behaviors over distributions of prompts, as specified programmatically within LUMOS. LMS alignment. To reveal LMS vulnerabilities, prior works have benchmarked their performance [Liang et al. 2023; Mazeika et al. 2024; Xie et al. 2025] or conducted adversarial attacks [Perez et al. 2022; Purpura et al. 2025; Vega et al. 2023; Xu and Parhi 2025]. Recently, probabilistic certifiers have been developed for assessing specific LLM properties [Chaudhary et al. 2024, 2025; Wang et al. 2025a]. These certifications offer formal guarantees for evaluation, thus making it a more reliable method of evaluating LMS properties [Singh and Chawla 2025]. In our research, we generalize LMS certification using LUMOS to specify and certify arbitrary desirable attributes.
