However, these methods exhibit significant limitations in two core dimensions. First, regarding data types, most studies still primarily rely on numerical time series, with the utilization of textual information limited to shallow feature extraction. This restricts the integration of multi-source data, such as market sentiment and policy interpretation. Second, in terms of model mechanisms, while neural networks enhance feature learning capabilities, they fall short in comprehending the underlying semantic logic of the scenarios they analyze. As a result, they are unable to establish causal relationships between event dynamics and price movements, which limits the adaptability of the models in complex market environments. The advent of large language models (LLMs) has catalyzed a paradigm shift, moving from historical pattern matching to the implementation of memory mechanisms. For example, FinMem[22] constructed a specialized financial memory bank that stores condensed representations of news and event embeddings. Similarly, MagicNet[12] introduced a hierarchical memory architecture that structures market data according to time granularity. However, these approaches still face challenges related to memory structure design. Memory units are primarily composed of isolated text fragments or embedding vectors, and retrieval mechanisms rely on semantic similarity, which complicates support for complex scenario analogies. Consequently, while methods have evolved from historical pattern analysis to memory-based approaches, there remains a need for deeper exploration of effective information mining and understanding, providing a clear direction for this paper's research. The event-driven paradigm seeks to uncover the fundamental causal mechanisms behind stock price movements. Prior to the emergence of LLMs, this field evolved from event studies based on cumulative abnormal returns to the automated identification of event signals within text, initially using models like Bag-of-Words[24], topic models[1], and later, deep learning representations such as BERT[8]. Despite this progression, a fundamental limitation persists across all these methods: they remain entrenched in a "numerically-driven" paradigm. Whether event labels or sentiment scores are extracted, they are ultimately incorporated as features into temporal models such as LSTM or Transformer. As a result, these models capture statistical correlations instead of event logic, losing critical semantic information during the feature extraction process and lacking the ability to perform deep reasoning.
