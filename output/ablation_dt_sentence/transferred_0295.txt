To ensure alignment with the user-specified edit, the resulting video must preserve the motion dynamics of the original sequence, maintaining consistency in frame-to-frame movement patterns throughout the clip. Specifically, every motion, including blinks and head turns, must occur simultaneously with the corresponding actions in the original video. This requirement is especially critical in conversational videos, where even minor discrepancies can disrupt perceptual coherence and compromise the realism of the synthesized output. Typically, the modifications sought are subtle, such as the addition of a mustache or the alteration of a facial expression. The objective is, therefore, to modify only what is necessary while preserving all other elements in their original form. Achieving perfect synchronization while maintaining the integrity of all temporal aspects remains a significant challenge for most video editing tools. To address this, we propose Sync-LoRA, a novel approach designed to generate edited videos that preserve precise synchronization with the original content. The modification is confined to the initial frame of the source video, with a text prompt determining the alterations to be implemented. We train our model using pairs of videos, where one represents the original version and the other the edited counterpart. These paired frames are synchronized such that corresponding frames demonstrate identical motion trajectories, with differences solely attributable to the edits applied. To construct these pairs, we initially generate them and then verify their synchronization. Our model integrates both visual and textual information to generate diverse modification proposals, which are then transformed into corresponding images that are synthesized into video sequences. However, these videos often exhibit imperfect alignment, which necessitates verification of synchronization through human motion data, such as facial expressions and body movements. We assess alignment across speech, gaze, blink, and pose channels, retaining only the most synchronized instances for model training. By utilizing these carefully aligned examples, the model is trained to preserve the original video's motion dynamics while implementing only the desired modifications, thereby ensuring consistent frame-level synchronization with the source video.
