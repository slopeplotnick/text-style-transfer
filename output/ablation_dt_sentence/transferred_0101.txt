Understanding high-resolution images presents a persistent challenge for large language models (LLMs). A recent study addresses this issue by partitioning the image into smaller segments and computing the semantic similarity between each segment and a query using a specialized model. The most relevant segments are then prioritized to identify the target object while filtering out irrelevant components. However, this segment-based processing can fragment complete objects across multiple regions, thereby undermining the alignment between the segments and the query. In our experiments, we observed that objects of varying sizes are better interpreted at different levels of detail. Based on this observation, we propose a training-free framework for interpreting detailed images without additional model fine-tuning. To mitigate the issue of object fragmentation and semantic inconsistency, we introduce a multi-resolution semantic fusion method that integrates similarity maps across multiple granularities, thereby enhancing information retrieval while preserving object integrity. Furthermore, to enable direct localization of target objects across the entire image, we adopt a model that identifies object regions through a sliding-window mechanism. Empirical evaluations on detailed image understanding benchmarks validate the effectiveness of our approach. Large language models (LLMs) have shown strong capabilities in integrating and interpreting both visual and textual modalities, thereby supporting tasks that require multimodal understanding and reasoning. By leveraging visual cues, these models can connect low-level pixel data to high-level semantic representations. However, most existing models process images at a fixed resolution—a design choice that simplifies architecture but introduces notable limitations. In particular, resizing high-resolution images to a uniform low resolution often results in shape distortion and blurring, which degrade fine-grained visual details. Recent research demonstrates that current methods remain inadequate for tasks involving intricate visual comprehension, as reflected by their subpar performance on dedicated high-resolution benchmarks.
