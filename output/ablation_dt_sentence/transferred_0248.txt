1. **Adaptive collective regulation**: Agents detect behavioral drift or anomalous coordination patterns and recommend normative adjustments to restore systemic stability or enhance behavioral diversity. 2. **Peer monitoring and reputational weighting**: Agents evaluate each other’s compliance with prevailing norms and modulate trust or responsiveness accordingly, thereby constructing a decentralized reputational infrastructure. 3. **Functional differentiation**: Agent roles are partitioned to approximate legislative (rule generation), judicial (rule interpretation), and executive (task execution) functions, preventing the concentration of authority in a single entity. These mechanisms remain intentionally abstract, delineating necessary governance functions without prescribing specific implementation methods. Subsequent research will explore how these structures can be realized within current orchestration systems and assessed through controlled experimentation. The aim of Institutional AI is not to achieve autonomy in a philosophical sense, but rather to maintain system stability amid ongoing change. Within this framework, safety is reconceptualized as a continuous internal process of monitoring and norm upkeep, as opposed to a static audit applied post-deployment. Agents contribute to formulating the very constraints that govern their behavior, enabling partial self-correction in instances where external oversight is delayed or insufficient. These designs are not intended to replace human supervision, but to augment existing governance baselines. By distributing evaluative responsibilities throughout the system, this architecture sustains traceability, encourages epistemic diversity, and mitigates systemic drift, as outlined by the Emergent Systemic Risk Horizon (ESRH) framework. The overarching goal is to strengthen the collaborative capacity of model networks—enhancing their interpretability and ensuring sustained operational coherence without continuous external input. The Institutional AI model should thus be regarded as a direct extension of the ESRH framework developed in this paper: while the ESRH taxonomy explains the genesis of emergent collective risks, the institutional perspective addresses how such risks might be managed. This contribution provides only an initial conceptual foundation.
