The researchers employed a different dataset, AI2-THOR, rather than the TEACH dataset used in the benchmarking phase. This transition was methodologically necessary, enabling direct replication for feasibility assessment and providing a common ground for fair algorithmic comparison. Moreover, it facilitated an indirect comparison of performance with tasks of a similar nature. However, the benchmarking of LTAA against traditional MRTA approaches utilized the same dataset. The authors are also testing SMART-LLM on the TEACH dataset to establish a more balanced benchmark system. Additionally, the study was conducted on a relatively small scale. While this allowed for a systematic baseline comparison and a thorough analysis of capabilities, real-world construction entails a far broader range of tasks with complex spatial and temporal constraints, which were not fully captured in the evaluation dataset. To address this, the study used abstract task descriptors, such as elemental/compound and heavy/dexterous, instead of focusing on specific tasks, in order to enhance generalization and representation. This abstraction was also necessary due to the lack of a large-scale, real-world construction operations database. The authors assert that the existence of such a database would enable evaluation of the LTAA in additional scenarios, including those involving dynamic replanning and resource conflicts, thereby enhancing confidence in the robustness of the framework. Moreover, the variability in LLM outputs raises concerns about the safety and reliability of construction operations. Although multi-stage validation mitigated execution failures, the controlled stochasticity allowed for nuanced reasoning but also introduced the potential for allocation variations. Given that construction environments demand high precision, additional verification mechanisms beyond the current validation processes may be required. Finally, when comparing the computational resources required, SMART-LLM's token and time costs could not be measured per stage, as all three stages shared a single concatenated prompt. To enable a comparison with LTAA, which evaluates only allocation, the stage-level costs of SMART-LLM were approximated by dividing its total token usage and runtime by three. While this approximation is reasonable, it may not fully capture the true allocation-specific overhead, and future work involving stage-level instrumentation would provide a more accurate analysis.
