Models perform differently across topics. As shown in Table 2, performance tends to be slightly lower in the domains of Religion and Sport, typically ranging from 7.8 to 8.2, while performance in Game and Lifestyle is notably stronger, often exceeding 8.4. This pattern suggests that models are more cautious and reserved when dealing with culturally or ethically sensitive subjects such as religion and sports. In contrast, topics like gaming and lifestyle are more neutral and better represented in training data, allowing for smoother and more natural mediation. Moreover, steering and judgment performances are strongly correlated. As shown in Fig. 3, there is a clear positive relationship between average steering and judgment scores across all models. Those excelling in judgment also tend to perform well in steering, indicating that both tasks rely on shared underlying reasoning and alignment abilities. This consistency suggests that improvements in core mediation skills, such as contextual understanding and balanced decision-making, can generalize across multiple evaluation tasks. Future work could explore how to further unify these capabilities, enhancing both interpretability and control in large language models. The results in Table 3 reveal that mediation has varying effectiveness across different linguistic aspects. The reduction in toxicity and exclamation usage suggests that the mediation process successfully fosters more neutral and composed responses, thereby improving conversational tone and creating a more balanced, less emotionally charged interaction. However, the impact on argumentativeness remains minimal, indicating that mediation has a weaker influence on managing discursive conflict. Interestingly, trends differ between the user simulation setting and the main experiment, highlighting that models behave differently when generating both sides of an interaction versus responding to real user input. This underscores the importance of evaluating mediation not only as a process (how mediation is applied) but also in terms of its outcomes (the actual communicative effects in realistic contexts).
