Predictions can be modeled using machine learning methods, such as neural networks or generative algorithms. Offline and online sequential planning under uncertainty can be addressed using Monte Carlo Tree Search algorithms. Having identified these two major components of AI—prediction and decision-making—I will review how they are currently applied in mineral exploration. Making predictions requires datasets. However, the most significant challenge in mineral exploration lies not in selecting a machine learning method, but in the creation and curation of datasets. These datasets are often complex, being multivariate, three-dimensional, compositional, or extremal. Rarely do such challenges arise in other domains. I will now consider two crucial yet frequently overlooked aspects of data curation. First, data are not always represented on a common scale. Second, not all datasets should be pooled into a single unified set. Furthermore, models trained in one area may not transfer effectively to another. A related issue is the upscaling of ground truth data, such as core samples, to match the resolution of geophysical data. Additionally, the deterministic interpolation of geophysical and geochemical datasets invariably reduces variance, and merging data from geologically distinct zones often lacks scientific justification. It is also common practice to apply machine learning models developed in one domain to another without adequate validation. Collectively, these missteps contribute to an increased rate of false positives and unfairly damage the reputation of AI in the industry. The root cause, however, lies not in AI itself, but in the way data are curated by practitioners. A well-known error in mining geostatistics, as established by Journel and Huijbregts (1978), involves variance differences across scales. For example, interpolated geophysical data will always exhibit reduced variance compared to original measurements. Likewise, label data from drilling—collected at centimeter resolution—is often upscaled or relocated to facilitate comparison with geophysical datasets. These practices artificially inflate the correlation between labels and predictors, leading to spurious relationships. Consequently, current mineral potential or prospectivity maps tend to suffer from a high incidence of false positives.
