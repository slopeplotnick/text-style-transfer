Advanced reinforcement learning techniques incorporated domain randomization and reward shaping to address the gap between simulated environments and real-world deployment. Tobin et al. (2017) demonstrated that domain randomization enables effective sim-to-real transfer for robotic tasks, while Grzes and Kudenko (2010) developed reward shaping methods to improve learning efficiency in multi-agent systems. These techniques enhanced robustness to environmental variability, particularly in construction robotics. However, they require substantial computational resources and careful parameter tuning, which becomes impractical in dynamic construction settings where conditions change frequently. Furthermore, the policies learned through these methods remain difficult to interpret and validate. These limitations in interpretability prompted the adoption of game-theoretic approaches, which offer rigorous mathematical frameworks for modeling interactions among autonomous agents. In 2023, Martin et al. applied game theory to multi-robot task allocation, using Shapley values to quantify individual robot contributions. Their method delivered strong empirical performance while supporting detailed analysis of equilibrium solutions and system stability. Nevertheless, game-theoretic models also struggle with interpretability and rest on the assumption of fully rational agents—an assumption often invalid under the uncertainty and complexity of construction environments. Additionally, they require precise utility function definitions, which are difficult to formulate for complex construction tasks. Despite these advances, AI-driven and learning-based approaches continue to face significant challenges in construction coordination scenarios that demand explainable decision-making. The opaque nature of deep learning and game-theoretic methods obscures the reasoning behind coordination decisions, making it difficult for human supervisors to assess them in terms of safety and project oversight. These challenges have motivated the investigation of Large Language Models (LLMs) as a promising solution for coordination, due to their ability to generate natural language explanations and support transparent decision-making. LLMs represent a transformative approach to multi-robot coordination through their advanced capabilities in natural language understanding, contextual reasoning, and adaptive decision-making. Unlike traditional optimization-based methods that require exhaustive problem specification, LLM-based approaches support intuitive human–robot interaction and improve the transparency and interpretability of coordination through natural language reasoning.
