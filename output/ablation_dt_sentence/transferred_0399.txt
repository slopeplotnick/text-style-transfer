The task is framed as unsupervised fraud detection in journal entry (JE) data, where labels for fraudulent activity are often scarce and difficult to obtain, making supervised learning impractical at scale. Therefore, we focus on detecting anomalous entries by modeling irregular patterns across both structured and unstructured fields, reflecting real-world auditing conditions. To evaluate our approach, we utilize two datasets. First, we use a synthetic JE dataset introduced by Gronewald et al. (2024), designed to simulate the day-to-day bookkeeping practices of a medium-sized enterprise. Each entry in this dataset includes features such as a unique posting ID, timestamps for both posting and transaction, credit/debit (CD) flags, tax rate, and account ID. Additionally, we benchmark our method on a real-world anonymized JE dataset from an industry partner. Due to anonymization, this dataset lacks the typical posting IDs that link balanced debit-credit cycles in standard double-entry bookkeeping systems, which forces us to treat it as a transaction-level fraud detection problem. To address the absence of detailed transactional context, we enhance the prompts for large language models (LLMs) with bookkeeping heuristics, guidelines, and summary statistics. This approach proves essential for outperforming traditional machine learning baselines. Given the sensitivity of accounting data, privacy considerations led us to favor open-weight LLMs, such as LLaMa AI (2025) and Gemma Team (2024), over proprietary models like GPT-4 (OpenAI, 2024). Our method demonstrates superior performance compared to the baseline methods on both the synthetic dataset from Gronewald et al. (2024) and the real-world anonymized dataset. For the baseline, we employ the Isolation Forest method, as proposed by Liu et al. (2008), which is particularly effective for tabular data and unsupervised anomaly detection, aligning with the goals of this project.
