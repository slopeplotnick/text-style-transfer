Several studies remain divided on the question of whether large language models (LLMs) exhibit learning processes that align with a Bayesian framework. Instances of specific reasoning failures have been documented, including sycophantic agreement [Sharma et al., 2023], errors in scaling [Gema et al., 2025], and conformity to the majority opinion at the expense of truth adherence [Weng et al., 2025]. These issues adversely affect the performance of LLMs on downstream tasks. In the context of evaluating reasoning, empirical evidence demonstrates that LLMs show improved performance on a range of tasks, such as mathematics [Lu et al., 2023] and coding [Gu et al., 2024]. However, traditional accuracy-based assessments fail to elucidate the underlying mechanisms by which LLMs achieve such enhanced results, nor do they provide insight into the models' ability to generalize across out-of-distribution scenarios. Such assessments may prove inadequate for real-world problems, where contextual factors are critical, as they primarily reflect knowledge acquired during training without accounting for the complexities of situational relevance. Therefore, to understand their reasoning processes, it is essential to develop more effective methods for measuring the process itself, rather than focusing solely on the outcomes. Research indicates that LLMs may prioritize user satisfaction or alignment with majority views over factual accuracy, potentially leading to the reinforcement of erroneous beliefs or biases. This, in turn, contributes to the entrenchment of these beliefs. Humans exhibit a well-documented propensity toward confirmation bias, selectively seeking, interpreting, or recalling information that aligns with their prior beliefs, a tendency that contributes to downstream social epistemic problems, such as polarization and the proliferation of misinformation. The feedback loops between humans and LLMs, where humans acquire beliefs from models trained on data reflecting human beliefs, may exacerbate the proliferation of false beliefs, thereby contributing to their widespread dissemination.
