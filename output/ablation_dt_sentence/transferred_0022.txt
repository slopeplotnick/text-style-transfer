We examine three interrelated research areas: methods incorporating external feedback, strategies for reducing reliance on human annotations, and the theoretical implications of representation choices on output quality. The standard approach for aligning large language models with human preferences is Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF). RLHF trains a reward model using human preference data, fine-tuning the policy to optimize this learned signal while adhering to a KL divergence constraint relative to a reference model. This approach forms the basis for models like ChatGPT and LLaMA, yet it faces well-known challenges, including the high cost and noise associated with preference data, as well as the susceptibility of reward models to manipulation and distribution shifts. These limitations have led to the development of alternative methods, such as Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), KTO, Bayesian RLHF, and MaxMin-RLHF, which aim to optimize the policy directly through pairwise comparisons. These approaches eliminate the need for a dedicated value network but still rely on carefully curated preference datasets. In addition, several models provide scalar scores and qualitative feedback to assess the quality of reasoning and evaluation, maintaining external supervision beyond the model's core computations. A complementary approach reduces reliance on human labels by leveraging automatic supervision or signals derived from the model itself. Verifiable-reward methods, such as DeepSeek-R1 (DeepSeek-AI, 2025) and TÜLU 3 (Lambert et al., 2024), replace human input with programmatic feedback.
