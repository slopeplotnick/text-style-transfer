Agentic systems are modern software architectures composed of orchestrated modules, which expose interfaces and are integrated into software pipelines. Unlike traditional programs, these systems operate in a stochastic and adaptive manner, with their execution—known as trajectories—tailored to the problem at hand. Evaluation of such systems typically centers on the outcome, measuring their success or failure at the final stage. This narrow perspective, however, overlooks crucial aspects of their operation, such as how agents reason, plan, make decisions, and evolve their strategies over time. To address this gap, we introduce GRAPHECTORY, a framework designed to encode the temporal and semantic relationships within these systems. GRAPHECTORY enables the development of process-centric metrics and analyses that assess agentic workflows independent of their final success. Using GRAPHECTORY, we analyze 4000 trajectories from two key agentic programming workflows—SWE-agent and OpenHands—paired with four core Large Language Models (LLMs), aiming to resolve SWE-bench Verified issues. Our automated analyses reveal that: (1) agents utilizing richer prompts or more powerful LLMs tend to exhibit greater GRAPHECTORY complexity, reflecting deeper cognitive exploration, broader contextual acquisition, and more rigorous validation before submitting patches; (2) the problem-solving strategies of agents are influenced by both task difficulty and the underlying LLM—with successful trajectories often following structured localization-patching-validation sequences, while unsuccessful ones display disordered, repetitive, or regressive patterns; (3) even in successful cases, agentic systems frequently follow inefficient trajectories, resulting in unnecessarily prolonged solution paths. Powered by large language models (LLMs), agentic systems have emerged as a promising approach for autonomously tackling complex, multi-step tasks across diverse domains, including software engineering, web navigation, virtual assistance, scientific discovery, and robotic navigation. Like traditional software systems, agentic systems consist of modular components such as LLMs, tools, APIs, and memory, and feature inputs, internal states, execution logic, and outputs. Rather than following a fixed procedure, these systems generate trajectories—ordered sequences of cognitive, operational, and perceptual steps—that together implement the execution logic required to complete the task. The evaluation of agentic systems has traditionally focused on outcomes, determining the effectiveness of their trajectories based on their ability to solve the problem at hand.
