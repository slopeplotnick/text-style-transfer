The MODOMA framework consists of two primary components: the mother agent and the daughter agent. Delilah (Cremers and Hijzelendoorn 1995/2024a), the mother agent, serves as a tool for generating and analyzing the Dutch language. Notably, Delilah’s grammatical knowledge does not rely on a corpus; instead, it operates based on predefined graph structures composed of attribute-value matrices. These graphs represent words and grammatical constructions, detailing properties such as phonological form, concepts, logical meaning representation (cf. Cremers and Reckman 2008), grammatical number, person, and syntactic categories (e.g., determiner, noun, or verb). For instance, a template for the article "de" ('the.MASC/FEM') is specified in Delilah, as shown in Figure 1, illustrating its lexical and grammatical knowledge. These templates are grounded in comprehensive linguistic analyses of the Dutch lexicon and grammar, ensuring a strong link between Delilah's grammar and linguistic insights into Dutch. As such, Delilah offers a distinct perspective compared to the data-driven approaches of large language models. While Delilah’s lexicon includes a finite set of words and constructions, these templates can be combined to form more complex utterances, such as noun phrases and sentences. For example, the template for "de" can generate noun phrases like "de vrouw" ('the woman'). This combination occurs through the process of unification, a mathematical procedure that checks for any conflicting features across templates. When no conflicts are found, the templates are unified into a new template that integrates all relevant information. As demonstrated in Figure 1, the syntactic structure of "de" is constrained to combine only with noun phrases, as indicated by the specification ARG|SYNSEM|CAT:n. Given the detail in Delilah's representations, a complete account of all features involved exceeds the scope of this paper; however, Cremers et al. (2014, 259-265) provide an in-depth explanation of unification within Delilah. In terms of unification for producing and parsing utterances, Delilah shares similarities with head-driven phrase structure grammar (HPSG, Pollard and Sag 1986, 1994), where there is no formal distinction between grammar and lexicon. Furthermore, Delilah employs a combinatory list grammar, which is closely related to combinatory categorial grammar (CCG, Cremers 2002, pp. 378-386; Cremers et al. 2014, pp. 115-137). These mechanisms have been discussed extensively in the literature, particularly by Cremers et al. (2014) and Reckman (2009, pp. 25-75). For the MODOMA system, Delilah is used in its original form, without modification.
