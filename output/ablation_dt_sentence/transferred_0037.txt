Self-Consistency (SC) (Wang et al., 2023b) aggregates multiple outputs using majority voting. In contrast, Soft Self-Consistency (Soft-SC) (Wang et al., 2024) selects the minimum, mean, or product of confidence scores within each category. Generative Self-Aggregation (GSA) (Li et al., 2025) prompts the LLM to generate a new response by synthesizing information from multiple samples. Additionally, Universal Self-Consistency (USC) (Chen et al., 2023) leverages the LLM to select the most consistent response among a set of candidate answers. We consider the following Thinking LLMs: gemini-2.5-flash (Comanici et al., 2025), qwen3 next-80b (Qwen Team, 2025), and gpt-oss-120b (OpenAI, 2025). For evaluation, we examine two machine translation tasks (Song et al., 2025) and six tasks from the Reward Bench 2 benchmark (Malik et al., 2025), which evaluates reward models across six key dimensions: Factuality, Precise Instruction Following (IF), Mathematical Reasoning, Safety, Focus, and Tie-Breaking. To construct the final benchmark, we generate preference pairs by pairing all possible responses from each task's source dataset, which includes both accepted and rejected responses. These pairs are categorized into 'non-tie' pairs (one accepted and one rejected response) and 'tie' pairs (two accepted or two rejected responses). From this comprehensive set, we sample 1000 examples for each of the six tasks. A detailed breakdown of the vote distributions for each task is provided in Appendix B. The results for the different Thinking LLMs, including gemini-2.5-flash, gpt-oss-120b, and qwen3 next-80b (Tables 6 and 7), exhibit a consistent qualitative pattern, suggesting that the effectiveness of our approach is robust across different LLM families. The structure of the problem is influenced by factors such as the frequency of ambiguous cases, the balance between directional and tied votes (its inherent tie propensity), and how these characteristics interact with the MAE-aligned Davidson model. Consequently, some tasks yield smooth, well-behaved calibration landscapes that transfer well, while others produce sharper landscapes where the fitted parameters fail to generalize effectively. Designing principled tests to predict when one task can be transferred to another—and to quantify robustness under significant distribution shifts or out-of-distribution targets—remains a promising avenue for future work.
