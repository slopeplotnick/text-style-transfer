In order for comparative analysis across various studies to be statistically meaningful, independently gathered samples must exhibit dimensional congruence. To ensure this, we conducted a Multigroup-CFA using our two samples as independent groups—one from real-world data and the other from simulations—while introducing increasing levels of constraint. This analysis involved examining configural invariance (H2.1), metric invariance (H2.2), scalar invariance (H2.3), and residual invariance (H2.4). If configural invariance is confirmed, it indicates that the pattern of factor loadings is consistent across both groups. Similarly, confirmation of metric invariance allows for the comparison of variances between latent variables. Moreover, if scalar invariance is established, comparisons of latent scale means become feasible. Finally, residual invariance permits further comparisons of observable mean values and analyses of manifest scale scores (Putnick & Bornstein, 2016; Vandenberg & Lance, 2000). Achieving high degrees of measurement invariance between real-world and simulated samples suggests that data from simulations can effectively replicate the properties of real-world datasets, leading to reliable results from in silico studies. We hypothesized that responses from the real-world dataset would substantially agree with those from the simulated dataset (H3), and that the distributions of scores across both datasets would not differ significantly (H4). To assess whether the distribution characteristics of the simulated dataset aligned with those of the real-world dataset, we first employed a Mann-Whitney U test to examine differences in mean values between the two datasets. Subsequently, a Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was conducted to evaluate the distributional congruence of the datasets.
