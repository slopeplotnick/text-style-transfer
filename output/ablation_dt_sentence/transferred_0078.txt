Faulty code not being executed. Our approach requires that faulty statements are executed by at least one failing test case; statements that are never executed cannot be diagnosed. As a result, insufficient input coverage may lead to false negatives, where issues go undetected. To mitigate this, we rely on multiple failing observations and lightweight static analysis. Bounded model checking (BMC) is employed with bounded encodings of programs. Loop unrolling and depth bounds can exclude feasible behaviours, which may prevent otherwise valid diagnoses from being detected under a given bound. Furthermore, when bounds truncate iterations, diagnoses that hold for the bounded model may not generalise to the full program. We select bounds conservatively and report them alongside the results, but the incompleteness due to bounding remains an inherent limitation. Pointers, arrays, and undefined behaviour. As discussed in Section 5.1, CFAULTS uses static analysers such as CPPCHECK [36] and CLANG-TIDY [37] to detect issues like uninitialised variables and potential runtime errors (e.g., division by zero). If any such issues are identified, CFAULTS halts immediately and reports them to the user. Additionally, CFAULTS incorporates CBMC’s memory-safety checks (e.g., --bounds-check, --pointer-check) to identify invalid memory operations, including out-of-bounds accesses and unsafe pointer arithmetic. However, CFAULTS adheres to CBMC’s specification and encoding of ANSI C: behaviours that are considered well-defined by CBMC’s memory model are treated as valid in our encoding, while violations that are not flagged by CBMC may remain undetected. Further work will focus on clarifying and, where possible, tightening the alignment between CBMC’s configuration and the intended semantics of C for pointers and arrays. External validity. Our evaluation is based on benchmark and student programs that compile successfully and include explicit specifications (such as assertions or expected outputs). Extending this approach to larger, system-scale codebases, programs with extensive I/O, concurrency, or complex libraries, or to specifications expressed in richer formalisms may require additional engineering and could potentially alter the results observed in this study.
