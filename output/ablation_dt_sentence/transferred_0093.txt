A growing body of research has focused on understanding the mechanisms underlying diffusion models, particularly with respect to the balance between memorization (i.e., the tendency to reproduce training samples) and generalization (i.e., the ability to synthesize novel samples). Previous studies have explored this phenomenon by analyzing model behaviors and identifying the factors that influence the tendency to memorize training data versus generate novel outputs. Most existing works, however, primarily examine model behavior when sampling from scratch, and validate their findings on small-scale datasets such as FFHQ and CIFAR-10. While such insights are valuable in data-scarce contexts, memorization becomes increasingly unlikely as datasets scale to sizes comparable to ImageNet or larger. Song et al. [35] recently observed this divergence between memorization and generalization in diffusion models trained on large-scale datasets. Specifically, they found that when sampling from earlier timesteps, the model tends to generate novel images, whereas later timesteps tend to reproduce previously encountered samples. This raises an important question: what governs the trade-off between memorization and generalization in large-scale diffusion models? To address this, we examine the training objectives underlying these models. Notably, while flow matching (FM) and its gradient-equivalent, conditional flow matching (CFM), are generally considered intractable due to the unavailability of ground-truth velocity fields, we show that under a Gaussian prior and a finite training set, the marginal velocity field of rectified flow admits a closed-form expression. This enables us to determine the oracle velocity field at any point in the sample space, which provides insight into the model's effective training objective. Through this lens, we observe that the training procedure consists of two distinct stages. In the early stage, closer to the prior, the oracle velocity integrates information from multiple data points, guiding the model toward a mixture of relevant data modes. In the later stage, the velocity field is dominated by a single data point, marking the transition to a more refined state. Moreover, we identify the scale of the dataset and the sample size as crucial factors in determining the timing of this transition.
