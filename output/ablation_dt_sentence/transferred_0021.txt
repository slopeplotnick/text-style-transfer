We establish baseline performance on the Swivuriso dataset by fine-tuning three speech recognition architectures: Whisper-large-v3-Turbo, Wav2Vec-BERT 2.0, and Massively Multilingual Speech (MMS). **Table 6** summarises the Word Error Rate (WER) achieved by each model at early (1,000 steps) and final (10,000 steps) training checkpoints. Continued fine-tuning yielded consistent improvements across all models, with Whisper-large-v3-Turbo achieving the most substantial reduction at 0.22, followed by Wav2Vec-BERT at 0.27 and MMS at 0.15. To assess the influence of training data volume, we further examined the performance of Whisper-v3-Turbo trained for a single epoch using subsets of 50, 150, and 250 hours of data. These experiments demonstrate that the Swivuriso dataset contributes meaningfully to enhancing ASR performance across diverse architectures and languages. Fine-tuning consistently reduced error rates, with results indicating that the dataset offers stable learning signals even at early training stages. Moreover, increasing the volume of training data led to measurable gains for most languages. These findings position Swivuriso as a valuable asset for advancing South African ASR research and for continued progress in multilingual speech modelling. Substantial efforts have been made by researchers in developing high-quality ASR datasets, where precision and the elimination of anomalies are critical requirements [34, 18, 35, 36, 37, 38]. However, in the case of low-resource languages—particularly African ones—progress has been comparatively limited. A noteworthy contribution in this space is the study by Abdulmumin et al. [39], which proposed enhancements to the FLORES dataset to improve its effectiveness for four African low-resource languages: Hausa, Northern Sotho (Sepedi), Xitsonga, and isiZulu. In their work, native speakers meticulously reviewed and corrected the dev and devtest splits, addressing inconsistencies and inaccuracies that could negatively impact downstream NLP tasks such as machine translation. Their modifications significantly improved the dataset’s linguistic accuracy and overall quality, as demonstrated through both qualitative evaluation and statistical analysis. This work underscores the essential role of native speakers in ensuring the linguistic and cultural appropriateness of low-resource language datasets. In line with these recommendations, we actively collaborated with native speakers to refine the quality and linguistic accuracy of our dataset. Another study that informed our methodology, particularly in sentiment analysis, is the work presented in [40]. The study highlighted the disparity in the availability of sentiment analysis benchmark datasets between high-resource and low-resource languages. Focusing on Hindi, the authors addressed challenges specific to socially salient domains and introduced a balanced dataset annotated with three sentiment categories: positive, negative, and neutral. This study provided valuable insight into the complexities of sentiment analysis in low-resource settings and further guided our approach.
