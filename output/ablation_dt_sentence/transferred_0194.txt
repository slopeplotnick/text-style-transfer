This approach can be applied across various fields: geologists typically represent conceptual models through diagrams in their papers and reports, mathematicians translate these diagrams into constraints for prior probability models, and geophysicists use these priors to perform Bayesian inversion. Another example involves the incorporation of alternative datasets, as the initial hypothesis may later prove to be incorrect. This issue represents a broader scientific challenge: how can we validate the accuracy of our assumptions? It was the Austrian philosopher Karl Popper who dedicated his career to analyzing such philosophical questions. Popper argued that science should avoid using induction, which involves deriving theories from observations. Instead, theories are seen as conjectures created by the human intellect, typically to address the limitations of previous models. Once proposed, these theories must be rigorously tested through empirical observations. If a theory fails to align with the observations, it must be discarded. Theories that withstand rigorous testing are considered the best models available at that time. In the context of uncertainty quantification (UQ), models are viewed as probabilistic representations of reality. One has as many hypotheses as there are models, each constrained by existing knowledge. However, empirical data should not be used to confirm a model. Rather, it should serve to falsify the model when discrepancies arise between the model’s predictions and the observed data. Popper provides a framework to address, though not fully resolve, the subjectivity inherent in Bayesian reasoning, which is a form of inductive inference, as opposed to the deductive logic of falsification. If the prior distribution fails to predict the current data (within an acceptable margin of error), the Bayesian prior is considered falsified. This means that, regardless of how many models are sampled from the prior, each will conflict with the observed data. While Popper’s concept of falsification may reduce the subjectivity of the prior after testing, he does not offer a mechanism for revising the prior, leaving this process still subject to human judgment.
