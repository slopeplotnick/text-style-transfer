To address this significant issue, the research community has proposed two primary approaches: Fact Verification (FV) and Hallucination Detection (HD). Fact Verification is a well-established research area focused on assessing the veracity of claims by leveraging evidence retrieved from external sources, such as web documents and structured databases. A key characteristic of FV is its origin-agnostic nature, as it evaluates the factual accuracy of a statement regardless of whether it is human-authored or machine-generated, based solely on the available evidence. In contrast, Hallucination Detection emerged more recently, gaining prominence with the rise of large language models (LLMs). HD primarily focuses on identifying content generated by these models, and unlike FV, which evaluates text in isolation, HD incorporates an additional layer of analysis by considering the underlying generative process of the text. Although HD methods, like FV, can incorporate external knowledge, they predominantly emphasize signals originating from within the model itself. These signals include token-level uncertainty, activation dynamics, and the consistency of model outputs across different generations. Therefore, HD is inherently model-centric, enabling it to identify fabricated information even in the absence of external corroborative evidence. Despite both approaches sharing the ultimate goal of identifying factual errors, they have evolved largely in isolation, as evidenced by their reliance on distinct benchmarks, evaluation metrics, and publication venues. This divergence stems from the inherent incompatibility between their respective evaluation paradigms. Fact Verification, being fundamentally text-centric, uses benchmarks such as FEVER, where claims are treated as discrete textual units for evaluation.
