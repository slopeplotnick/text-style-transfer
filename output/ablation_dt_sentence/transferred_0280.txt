Another line of investigation explores robustness by enforcing consistency in the interpretation of shapes and geometric structures. Recent studies show that viewpoint shifts can induce significant distortions in the embedding space of pretrained ViT encoders, thereby disrupting coordination between perception and downstream decision-making modules. Motivated by these findings, we examine whether correcting such mismatches—without modifying the overall VLA architecture or incorporating additional training data—is sufficient to improve robustness to viewpoint variation. Parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) has emerged as a compelling alternative to full-model adaptation, enabling models to adapt to new tasks or domains while keeping the number of trainable parameters minimal. Representative methods include adapters, LoRA, Prefix-Tuning, and BitFit, which insert lightweight trainable modules while keeping the majority of the pretrained model frozen. These approaches are widely adopted in large language models and multimodal systems for their efficiency and stability. In embodied AI, PEFT has primarily been applied to language or policy components, such as lightweight updates to decision transformers or instruction encoders. However, in the context of viewpoint variation, most methods seldom address the problem by directly finetuning or adapting the visual module. Approaches that introduce targeted, lightweight modifications to the visual pathway within VLA models remain particularly rare. In contrast to methods that adapt the entire VLA stack, our work investigates whether parameter-efficient modification of the visual pathway—specifically through lightweight adaptation of vision tokens or the vision encoder—is sufficient to recover robustness. Prior approaches typically mitigate visual perturbations by retraining large portions of the system or the entire visuomotor policy. However, we argue that such full-stack adaptation is unnecessary: the primary effect of viewpoint shifts is to induce systematic distortions in the processing of visual inputs, thereby destabilizing downstream computations.
