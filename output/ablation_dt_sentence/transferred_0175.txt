We examined the efficiency and effectiveness of various systems in editing PowerPoint files. Among these, only MiniMax Agent and PPTPilot consistently complete the editing task within two minutes. In contrast, the remaining systems exhibit slower performance or frequent execution failures. Given the importance of speed, we restricted our comparison to single-pass outputs, with results for loop-enabled edits presented separately in Table 4. Our findings reveal that effective PowerPoint editing within PPTPilot relies on three core components: (i) a planning process that comprehends the structural and semantic aspects of slides, (ii) a system capable of transitioning between API utilization and direct XML manipulations, and (iii) an iterative process that rigorously evaluates and refines edits, enabling the handling of lengthy and intricate tasks. These results also highlight that many advanced systems continue to struggle with complex editing tasks, mixed media, and modifications spanning multiple slides. However, PPTPilot outperforms these systems in these areas. Moreover, we conducted a systematic evaluation of the core components of PPTPilot to assess their individual contributions to overall performance. The evaluation metrics mirror those presented in Section 3, including task success rates, file integrity, adherence to instructions, slide quality, and computational resources. For this evaluation, we utilized the complete PPTPilot pipeline, which includes generating a JSON snapshot, determining the appropriate application of XML modifications or Python-based library functions, ensuring adherence to output schemas, and implementing automated XML validation and correction. We systematically evaluated different versions of PPTPilot by selectively disabling or modifying specific components while holding all other factors constant. For instance, when all modifications were performed directly in XML, the method proved effective for small, specific alterations, such as resizing individual shapes. However, this approach was slower and less efficient for larger-scale adjustments, such as standardizing text size or implementing a consistent theme across the deck. In contrast, an alternative version employing the Python library proved effective for large-scale modifications, such as batch renaming or translation. However, it exhibited limitations when addressing detailed structural adjustments requiring precise control over individual elements.
