As we move toward more conversational agents powered by language models, addressing the diversity of spoken information is not just a matter of fairness but also crucial for achieving the robustness and versatility required in real-world applications. While voice-interface-based language models have made significant strides, their limitations in handling the wide range of linguistic variations—such as accents, dialects, and sociolects—continue to present both challenges and opportunities for further improvement. Historically, speech processing models have struggled with accent and dialect diversity. Despite the impressive performance of language models, they still reflect the biases inherent in their training datasets, often prioritizing standard accents or high-resource languages. As a result, these models tend to perform suboptimally for speakers with less-represented accents. To overcome this, it is essential to design models that can generalize across diverse phonetic and prosodic patterns. In this regard, emerging approaches like in-context learning-based adaptation and retrieval-augmented clustering hold promise for making models more resilient to linguistic variation, enabling them to adapt to previously unseen accents with minimal data. However, the current standard evaluation metrics—such as word error rate (WER)—are insufficient for capturing the true performance disparities across various linguistic groups. Therefore, it is crucial to expand evaluation frameworks to include fairness-driven metrics that assess model performance across different demographics, accents, and languages. Moreover, error analysis should go beyond simple quantitative measures and provide qualitative insights into which groups are most affected by model biases. Such a nuanced approach to evaluation will guide the development of more equitable models. Additionally, the variation in speech patterns within a single language—driven by factors such as age, gender, socioeconomic background, and profession—adds another layer of complexity. Traditional models often overfit to more homogeneous data distributions, predominantly reflecting the sociolects of dominant groups within training datasets. Addressing this challenge requires more than just diversifying the data; it calls for the development of advanced mechanisms such as style transfer and speaker adaptation that allow models to interpret a wide range of linguistic and paralinguistic cues. In particular, paying attention to prosody, intonation, and emotional expression in speech is vital for understanding socially or emotionally charged speech.
