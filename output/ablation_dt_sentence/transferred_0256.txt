In cases like "pearl white" or "ivory white," the core need is a specific shade of white, rather than the "pearl" or "ivory" components themselves. Similarly, when referring to "phone gas station," the underlying need is a power bank, with "gas station" serving as a metaphor. In such scenarios, a higher-level semantic understanding is essential, as relying solely on surface-level semantic parsing proves insufficient. Moreover, domain-specific knowledge is critical in addressing needs that involve niche attributes or characteristics that cannot be inferred from surface forms alone. This is particularly evident in vertical e-commerce, where jargon, brand taxonomies, model coding rules, and category-specific descriptions (such as "mouthfeel" for tea or wine) play a significant role. Additionally, users often express their needs through misspellings, aliases, or slang, which requires an integration of both general and domain-specific knowledge. To handle these challenges effectively, the model must possess robust capabilities in spelling correction, variant normalization, and alias alignment. Furthermore, intent recognition, which distinguishes between queries asking for items and those seeking item-related information (such as reviews or usage guides), depends heavily on reasoning. This process involves identifying the true target of the need—whether a specific item or a derived attribute—by analyzing contextual clues and distinguishing between primary and secondary entities. For example, in a query like "charger/headphones for iPhone 17," the core need pertains to either a charger or headphones, with "iPhone 17" serving as a compatibility constraint. At times, users may also express abstract needs that do not correspond to specific items but instead relate to broader categories or domains. For instance, "phones with the best battery life" requires the system to understand and measure "battery life" in the context of the phone domain, constructing appropriate ranking or filtering rules to support comparison across potential candidates. Finally, ambiguity often arises when users omit crucial contextual information, a common occurrence in real conversations. To mitigate this, the system must leverage dialogue history, user profiles, and default settings to infer missing constraints and preferences, ensuring a more accurate interpretation of the query.
