This paper examines the inadequacies of safety mechanisms originally designed for human-model interactions when applied to environments where large language models (LLMs) engage with one another. Most current governance frameworks continue to emphasize single-agent safety containment through prompts, fine-tuning, and moderation layers that regulate individual model behavior. However, these frameworks fail to address the complexities inherent in multi-model interactions, assuming a dyadic setting where one model responds to one user under consistent oversight. Yet, both research and industry are rapidly advancing toward architectures in which models engage in intercommunication, utilizing the outputs of one another as inputs in iterative sequences. Within these frameworks, even when individual models operate in accordance with their intended design, the collective system remains susceptible to failure. Therefore, this paper advocates for a paradigm shift from the safety of individual models to the safety of entire systems, introducing the concept of the Emergent Systemic Risk Horizon (ESRH) to elucidate how systemic issues arise from interactions among models rather than from the malfunction of a singular model. The contributions of this paper include (i) a theoretical framework addressing the risks inherent in model interactions, (ii) a taxonomy linking micro, meso, and macro-level failure modes, and (iii) a proposal for Institutional AI, an architectural framework designed to enhance adaptive oversight within multi-agent systems. Contemporary safety protocols predominantly operate under the assumption of a single-model framework, wherein human oversight can mitigate undesirable outputs through prompt optimization, iterative feedback, or output regulation. While these approaches have enhanced the reliability and decorum of individual models, their applicability remains confined to isolated interactions. Moreover, such methods address the behavior of individual models in response to discrete inputs but fail to account for the dynamics that emerge when multiple models interact within a shared system. As large models are increasingly utilized in tool-using, agentic, and multi-agent environments, the single-agent safety paradigm begins to deteriorate. Individually well-aligned models can collectively produce outcomes that no single instance was trained to prevent due to feedback amplification, imitation, or emergent coordination. Consequently, this phenomenon results in a disparity between local alignment and systemic safety, wherein compliance at the component level does not guarantee control at the network level.
