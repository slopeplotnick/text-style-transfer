Predictions can be modeled using machine learning methods, such as neural networks or generative AI algorithms. Offline and online sequential planning under uncertainty can be effectively addressed using Monte Carlo Tree search algorithms. Artificial intelligence fundamentally consists of two major components: prediction and decision-making. In the context of mineral exploration, making predictions requires datasets, and the most significant challenge lies in the creation and curation of these datasets rather than the choice of machine learning methodology. These datasets often exhibit considerable complexity, being multivariate, three-dimensional, compositional, and extremal, which presents challenges rarely encountered in other domains. Two critical and often overlooked aspects of data curation include the inconsistency of data scales and the inadvisability of pooling all data into a single dataset. Furthermore, methodologies that work in one region may not necessarily apply to another. For example, ground truth data, such as core samples, is often upscaled to match geophysical datasets, a process that must be carefully managed to avoid inaccuracies. Similarly, deterministic interpolation of geophysical and geochemical datasets is sometimes conducted without sufficient geological justification, undermining the reliability of interpretations. Another common error involves applying machine learning models developed for one area to another without validating their suitability, which often leads to methodological flaws. These oversights contribute to an increase in false positives and tarnish the reputation of AI systems in the industry. However, the root issue lies not in AI itself but in the methodologies employed for data preprocessing and analysis. A well-documented problem in the mining industry is the failure to account for the varying statistical properties of data collected at different scales, particularly discrepancies in variance. For instance, interpolated geophysical datasets inherently exhibit reduced variance compared to original measurements due to the interpolation process. Additionally, drilling data, typically collected at finer spatial resolutions, is often upscaled or spatially adjusted to facilitate comparison with geophysical datasets. Such manipulations introduce spurious correlations between dependent variables (labels) and independent variables (predictors), which arise solely from methodological handling of the data. Consequently, mineral potential or prospecting maps frequently display a high incidence of false positives.
