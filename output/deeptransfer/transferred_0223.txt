Another important issue in psychometric scale development is the "Jingle-Jangle Fallacy" (Gonzalez et al., 2021), which occurs when two measures with the same label assess different constructs (jingle fallacy) or when two measures with different labels assess the same construct (jangle fallacy). In the latter case, the nuanced nature of psychological phenomena, combined with unclear construct definitions and terminology, often leads to seemingly distinct scales exhibiting significant overlap, as evidenced by high correlation coefficients. Addressing these fallacies primarily involves conceptual engineering (Chalmers, 2025), which necessitates a meticulous evaluation of construct validity, criterion validity, and discriminant validity (Campbell & Fiske, 1959; Cronbach & Meehl, 1955). This iterative process, which includes pilot testing, frequently demands substantial resources and time, thereby posing challenges for researchers. Consequently, the lack of systematic efforts to mitigate this issue has contributed to the proliferation of redundant measurement instruments, further fragmenting quantitative psychological research (Anvari et al., 2025; Elson et al., 2023). Meanwhile, the emergence of Generative Artificial Intelligence (GAI) technologies, such as Open AI's "Chat GPT," offers promising avenues for advancing computational methodologies in areas like social psychology, where traditional modeling approaches have often been deemed ineffective. Specifically, Large Language Models (LLMs) demonstrate considerable potential in assisting researchers with psychometric scale development (Beghetto et al., 2025; Hussain et al., 2024). For instance, LLMs can rapidly generate pools of draft scale items for subsequent empirical validation (Götz et al., 2024) through processes such as "Automatic Item Generation" (AIG) (P. Lee et al., 2023, 2025).
