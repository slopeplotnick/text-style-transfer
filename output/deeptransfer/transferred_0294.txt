We propose a method to extract semantic triples from images using advanced multimodal language models, aligning these with domain-specific ontologies to construct interpretable knowledge graphs. Furthermore, we introduce a graph-based influence analysis pipeline that evaluates the relationship between generated images and their training counterparts through graph representations, thereby enabling human-understandable explanations of similarity. This approach proves particularly useful for tasks such as copyright attribution, enhancing interpretability, and ensuring transparency in data provenance—demonstrating its potential to improve traceability in generative models. To evaluate our method, we conducted experiments on both locally trained models and large-scale models. For the latter, we focused on the increasingly popular Ghibli-style image generations to assess the method’s ability to detect stylistic influences, even in black-box scenarios. The structure of the paper is as follows: Section 2 reviews related work on interpretability in generative models and the construction of knowledge graphs using multimodal language models. Section 3 details our proposed methodology. Section 4 describes our experimental setups and presents results across both localized and large-scale generative models. Finally, Section 5 synthesizes key findings and outlines potential directions for future research. As generative models grow more sophisticated, the demand for interpretability has expanded across technical, ethical, and policy domains. General techniques such as t-SNE, LIME, and attention visualization provide valuable insights into model behavior; however, they fail to directly address the attribution of training data—an increasingly pressing concern in contexts such as authorship and copyright. To address this, various attribution techniques have been proposed. Early methods, including influence functions, retraining-based attribution, and Shapley value approximations, have demonstrated effectiveness in well-defined domains like classification tasks. However, their applicability to large-scale generative models is limited due to their dependence on gradient computation, retraining access, and significant computational resources. Consequently, these methods are rarely employed in modern deep learning pipelines, particularly for generative models characterized by their inherent opacity.
