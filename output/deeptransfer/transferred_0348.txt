Together, these metrics collectively evaluate both the directional consistency of the applied edits and their semantic alignment throughout the sequence. To assess identity preservation, we compute the mean cosine similarity between face embeddings extracted by a pre-trained ArcFace model for each frame and the edited first frame. This metric reflects how faithfully the appearance and facial structure of the individual are maintained during temporal propagation, with higher values indicating stronger identity consistency. Moreover, a user study confirmed a perceptual preference for our method, citing its superior visual quality and temporal alignment. Full details of the study and all quantitative metric formulations are provided in the supplementary materials. As shown in Table 1, text-based methods like LucyEdit achieve high scores on certain synchronization metrics (e.g., Speech Corr. 0.80 vs. our 0.72); however, this comes at the cost of failing the visual edit itself, as indicated by its low Directional CLIP score of 0.11 compared to our 0.21. Our approach achieves the best overall balance, excelling in synchronization while faithfully applying the visual edits. Conversely, VACE benefits from strong external conditioning (pose, canny, depth), resulting in higher CLIP-based alignment, yet it exhibits weaker temporal correspondence and identity consistency. Sync-LoRA, by explicitly training for synchronized editing, ensures robust temporal alignment while executing precise edits that preserve subject identity. This trade-off underscores the strength of our in-context formulation, which adeptly balances motion coherence and edit fidelity rather than excelling in a single dimension. In Figure 6, we present a leave-one-out experiment to test the contribution of each synchronization channel. This is achieved by training models on datasets curated without one component (speech, gaze, blink, or pose), accomplished by setting its respective weight to zero in our filtering score. Specifically, in the first example (top two rows), excluding the pose component leads to visible errors in head orientation, while in the second example (bottom two rows), removing any channel introduces spatial drift and temporal misalignment in gaze or blinking. Omitting any channel degrades synchronization, notably in the articulation of the male subject's mouth and the ocular movements of the female subject, confirming that all four cues provide complementary motion information.
