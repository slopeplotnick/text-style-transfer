The phenomenon whereby adversarial training enhances the transferability of attacks is not attributable to specific architectural designs of models. Instead, it arises from the modifications in information processing induced by adversarial training, an effect consistently observed across diverse model architectures. This underscores its critical implications for comprehensive security considerations. To better understand the mechanisms underlying this phenomenon, researchers have utilized the integrated gradients method to analyze the interpretability of model decision-making processes. For example, a comparative analysis of two DenseNet121 models—one adversarially trained (AT) and the other trained under standard conditions (ST)—revealed notable differences in attribution patterns. The AT model demonstrated a pronounced focus on semantically significant regions of the image, such as the head, body, and legs of an ostrich, while the ST model predominantly emphasized localized features, including feather textures and background elements. This distinction highlights the AT model's tendency to prioritize object-level features that encapsulate high-level visual concepts, thereby facilitating broader generalization across diverse images. Conversely, the ST model's reliance on fine-grained, texture-specific details limits its capacity for feature transferability across architectures. Moreover, adversarial examples generated by AT models exhibit enhanced transferability across diverse architectures due to their targeting of features commonly shared among various models. For instance, DenseNet121 and DenseNet201 exhibit analogous image processing methodologies, whereas MobileNetV3 demonstrates heightened vulnerability due to its incorporation of design principles from EfficientNet.
