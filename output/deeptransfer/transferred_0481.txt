To better interpret the trends suggested by the accuracy and false positive rate (FPR) results, we examine the verifier gain in the final row. Verifier gain quantifies the expected benefit of using the verifier during rejection sampling, which involves repeatedly sampling until the verifier accepts a solution—a common solver-verification interaction setting (Song et al., 2025). This visualization offers a clearer view of verification quality: self-verification yields the smallest gains, and more accurate solvers do not exhibit greater self-improvement. Moreover, gains increase slightly in intra-family verification, while cross-family verification provides the greatest potential benefits. We examine verifier performance across different model families and sizes. In Figure 3, we repeat the experiments from Figure 2 but plot each verifier metric as a function of model size within each model family. We observe that verification accuracy and false negative rate (FNR) consistently improve as models become larger, whereas FPR behaves more inconsistently, often increasing with model size, as seen in intra-family verification for DeepSeek. For state-of-the-art post-trained models such as Qwen3 and DeepSeek, we find that verifier gains are largest in the cross-family setting, smaller in the intra-family setting, and minimal during self-verification. At first glance, this appears to contradict Song et al. (2025), who report that self-verification GV-Gaps increase with more pretraining FLOPs. However, their analysis focuses on older model families, and Figure 2 likewise shows larger verifier gains for older models such as Qwen2.5 and Llama3. We hypothesize that stronger post-trained models like DeepSeek and Qwen3 show negligible gains in self-verification and limited gains in intra-family verification for two reasons: (a) they may already engage in spontaneous self-verification when used as solvers, reducing the benefit of an additional forced verification round, and (b) their distributions are significantly sharpened by post-training, which limits the improvement obtained from rejection sampling.
