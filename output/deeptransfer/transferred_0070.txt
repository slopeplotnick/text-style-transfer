We reproduce official baselines, such as majority, random, and SVM, as our initial benchmarks. Furthermore, we generate results for Logistic Regression (LR), Random Forest (RF), and Decision Tree (DT), considering them as additional baseline methods. Moreover, we fine-tune several transformer-based models, including DistilBERT, BanglaBERT, mBERT, and XLM-RoBERTa, and provide a detailed evaluation of their performance. Additionally, we offer an in-depth discussion of modeling challenges and dataset limitations. Social media has become an integral part of daily life, enabling rapid communication, seamless information sharing, and the exchange of opinions across geographical boundaries. However, this freedom of expression has also facilitated the proliferation of hate speech and offensive language on these platforms. Unfortunately, limited attention has been given to identifying hate speech in Bangla social media, and there is a significant lack of high-quality datasets for this purpose. The linguistic patterns on social media often differ substantially from those in traditional print media, making the automated detection of Bangla hate speech particularly challenging. Traditionally, hate speech detection has been framed as a supervised classification problem (Mossie and Wang, 2020; Alkomah and Ma, 2022). Early studies primarily relied on lexicon-based and rule-based approaches, but these methods often failed to capture implicit or context-dependent expressions of hate (Cavicchio, 2024; Gitari et al., 2015; Asghar et al., 2017). More recent research has employed machine learning classifiers, such as logistic regression and support vector machines (SVMs), to detect online vulnerabilities and harmful content. Traditional supervised learning techniques have been widely applied in this domain.
