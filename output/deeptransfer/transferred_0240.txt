Despite these evaluation limitations, the replication study confirmed three critical findings: (1) LLM-driven coordination is feasible for multi-robot task allocation, (2) structured validation mechanisms significantly enhance reasoning reliability, and (3) hierarchical retry systems effectively address inconsistencies in LLM outputs. With the feasibility of LLM-MRTA established, the authors developed the LTAA framework to enhance its performance. The LTAA framework was evaluated using the TEACH dataset, the standard benchmark for Q-learning, DQN, and DP comparisons in MRTA research. This dataset's transition from AI2-THOR household tasks to construction robotics scenarios enables direct performance comparison with established algorithmic baselines under identical evaluation conditions. The LTAA framework specializes in the allocation component of the Self Corrective-Agent Architecture, focusing exclusively on robot-task assignments and accepting pre-decomposed tasks, as traditional algorithms do. This alignment ensures that both LLM-based and algorithm-based approaches are evaluated on equivalent problem formulations. Building on the self-corrective principles mentioned earlier, the LTAA framework implements a nine-node workflow. Unlike the Self Corrective-Agent Architecture, which handled full-pipeline coordination, the LTAA framework concentrates solely on the allocation decision process, producing robot-task assignments comparable to traditional algorithmic outputs. Moreover, the LTAA framework introduces three core technical innovations that enable competitive performance with traditional optimization algorithms while maintaining reasoning transparency: (1) phase-adaptive allocation strategies that dynamically adjust decision priorities throughout the project's lifecycle, (2) multi-stage validation with hierarchical retry and self-correction mechanisms to ensure the accuracy of LLM reasoning, and (3) context-aware reasoning integration that structures allocation decisions based on quantitative capability priors. These contributions are implemented through a comprehensive nine-node LangGraph workflow that systematically processes tasks through structured reasoning stages.
