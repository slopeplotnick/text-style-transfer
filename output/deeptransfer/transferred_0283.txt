To ensure data quality, the survey included an attention check similar to the one used by Shank et al. (2023) ("Who was the composer of the music you just heard?") before the music rating task. Following this, participants were asked to self-report the honesty and care with which they answered on a 5-point scale and to specify their listening setup (headphones vs. speakers). Participants were informed that their responses would not affect feedback or compensation. An overview of the study design is presented in Figure 1. All participants were asked to rate their familiarity with the music and their level of enjoyment on a scale from 1 (not at all) to 5 (very). If they selected a value of 4 or higher for familiarity, they were presented with an additional yes-or-no question about whether the music evoked memories of a specific person or experience. Participants in the "AI or human" condition were also required to indicate whether they believed the music excerpt was generated by an AI or a human, along with their confidence level on a 6-point scale, ranging from 1 (definitely AI) to 6 (definitely human). Emotional responses to the music excerpts were assessed using the Geneva Emotion Music scale (GEMS; Zentner et al., 2008) for all participants. The GEMS includes nine music-specific emotion dimensions, summarized into three overarching factors: (1) Sublimity (Wonder, Tenderness, and Nostalgia), (2) Vitality (Joyful Activation and Power), and (3) Unease (Sadness and Tension). We employed the abbreviated GEMS-9 version, representing each dimension with a single label and associated exploratory terms. Emotion ratings were provided on a scale from 0 to 100, with initial values set at 0. If no selection was made, the rating remained at 0. Additionally, an open-ended text box allowed participants to add up to three supplementary emotion terms not included in the GEMS, along with corresponding intensity ratings.
