Challenges like climate change and the transition to renewable energy far exceed the expertise of any single scientific discipline, yet there is still no dedicated degree in Mineral Exploration. Meanwhile, the production of information has become nearly instantaneous, with artificial intelligence continuously advancing its capabilities. What once required trips to the library to manually extract data from journals has been replaced by algorithms that can search and synthesize thousands of documents in real time. However, interest in fields such as economic geology and mining engineering continues to decline in Western nations. This makes it more urgent than ever to preserve the century-old knowledge of ore deposit formation, much of which is buried in outdated geological reports scattered across the globe. High-quality databases, like those in Canada and Australia, are critically needed worldwide. In Africa, for instance, only one country has established such a database (gsd.gov.zm). Making this information openly accessible through online platforms is essential to attract greater investment. In this essay, I argue for a collaborative effort between the scientific and industrial communities to develop a new methodology for mineral exploration. As exploration increasingly moves "under cover," the traditional approach of starting with surface outcrops and relying on deterministic geophysical inversion has become ineffective, as evidenced by declining discovery rates. Instead, a decision-focused framework that explicitly incorporates risk assessment through uncertainty quantification is required to reduce false-positive drilling outcomes. This approach must also address the fundamental gaps in our understanding of orebody formation by systematically quantifying epistemic uncertainty. Furthermore, the development of advanced geostatistical methods to appropriately manage data at their respective scales is urgently needed, as the current lack of such methodologies contributes to the persistence of false positives.
