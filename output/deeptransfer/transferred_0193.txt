This issue cannot be observed in single-model evaluations; rather, it arises from the structure and dynamics of interaction, thereby reducing safety, reliability, or accountability at the system level. We propose three preliminary dimensions that influence where this horizon may arise. These dimensions are intended as a working basis rather than an exhaustive set, and additional factors may emerge as experiments advance. First, interaction topology refers to the structure of communication and influence among agents. Second, cognitive opacity denotes the degree to which reasoning and message semantics become inaccessible or uninterpretable. Third, objective divergence concerns the extent to which local optimization goals drift apart across agents. Collectively, these dimensions predict the likelihood and form of emergent collective risks. Each can be operationalized through measurable indicators and tested within controlled multi-agent environments. Topology, for instance, defines how information flows through the network. Densely connected systems promote rapid coordination but also rapid contagion; however, sparse systems limit propagation, impeding collective correction. In practice, topology determines how a single deviation—such as a biased summary or incorrect inference—can become a shared belief. Metrics such as contagion velocity and misalignment diffusion capture these dynamics. Therefore, benchmarks of multi-agent safety should include topology-sensitive evaluations. Moreover, cognitive opacity can arise unintentionally, such as through overfitting to interaction patterns, or strategically, when optimization toward compliance suppresses genuine reasoning. In such environments, explanations provided by one model may be accepted uncritically by another, reinforcing self-consistent but incorrect rationales. The intent-opacity rate—the fraction of outputs whose rationale cannot be reconstructed from logs or traces—offers an initial proxy for this loss of transparency.
