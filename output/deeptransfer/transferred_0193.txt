This issue cannot be observed in evaluations of individual models; rather, it arises from the structure and dynamics of their interactions, reducing safety, reliability, or accountability at the system level. To address this, we propose three preliminary dimensions that may help identify where such risks emerge. These dimensions are intended as a starting point rather than an exhaustive framework, with the possibility of additional factors being identified through further research. - Interaction topology: the structure of communication and influence among agents. - Cognitive opacity: the extent to which reasoning processes and message semantics become inaccessible or uninterpretable. - Objective divergence: the degree to which agents’ local optimization goals begin to diverge. Together, these dimensions help predict the likelihood and nature of emergent collective risks. Each can be operationalized through measurable indicators and tested in controlled multi-agent environments. Interaction topology determines how information flows across the network. While densely connected systems enable rapid coordination, they also accelerate the spread of errors. Conversely, sparsely connected systems slow error propagation but hinder collective correction. Thus, the topology of information dissemination shapes the risk of a single deviation—such as a biased summary or incorrect inference—escalating into a shared belief. Metrics like contagion velocity (the time for an error to reach half the agents) and misalignment diffusion (the proportion of agents adopting risky behaviors) are critical for capturing these dynamics. Consequently, safety evaluations for multi-agent systems must incorporate topology-sensitive benchmarks. Cognitive opacity refers to the degree to which agents’ internal reasoning processes are obscured or distorted in communication. This can occur unintentionally, such as through overfitting to interaction patterns, or deliberately, when optimization for compliance suppresses genuine reasoning. In such cases, one model may uncritically accept another’s explanations, regardless of their accuracy, reinforcing self-consistent but flawed rationales. The intent-opacity rate, which measures the fraction of outputs whose rationale cannot be reconstructed from logs or traces, provides an initial proxy for assessing this loss of transparency.
