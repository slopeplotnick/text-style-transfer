Except for Study 1, all simulated datasets successfully replicated the factor structure of their corresponding scale (H1). Moreover, M-CFA demonstrates that both simulated participants and real-world ones respond to the scales in comparable ways (H2). These findings align with prior research on group-level simulated data (Argyle et al., 2023; Hewitt et al., 2024). Notably, Studies 3 and 4 exhibit high levels of measurement invariance between datasets compared to Studies 1 and 2, which may suggest that scales developed from simulated subjects are more generalizable to human populations than the reverse. One plausible explanation is that, when responding, LLMs tend to interpret scale items using a more standardized "common semantic denominator," less influenced by randomness and biases stemming from study conditions and population characteristics. Consequently, factor structures derived from simulated samples may exhibit greater semantic generalizability to external samples than those extracted from real-world data. Future research should investigate the factors shaping differences in how LLMs and human subjects interpret and respond to questionnaire items. However, while CFA consistently yields positive outcomes, simulated samples fail to replicate the pairwise correlations (H3), distributions (H4), and variances (H5) observed in real-world samples. Collectively, these findings suggest that although both datasets share the same factorial structure, their score distributions differ significantly across all measured dimensions. Therefore, while group-level data and their dimensional structures are sufficiently reliable for use in pilot testing, individual-level simulated data are not suitable for this purpose. These limitations are consistent with the observations of Petrov and colleagues (2024). Importantly, while Studies 2, 3, and 4 yielded similar results, Study 1 diverged significantly. This discrepancy may be attributable to several factors: first, the dimensional structure of this scale was determined using a different rotation method (minres) than those used for other scales, which may not have elicited divergent responses (Sass & Schmitt, 2010). Second, this scale employs a 5-point Likert scale for responses, whereas others utilize either a 6-point (Study 2) or a 7-point scale.
