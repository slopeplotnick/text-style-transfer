Experts in numerical modeling translate high-level conceptual hypotheses into quantitative frameworks, implementing details through model parametrization. At the highest level, discrete probabilities are assigned to these overarching hypotheses, while individual variables are represented using probability distributions to capture their uncertainties. This prior distribution model must be tested against empirical data, which involves forward modeling conducted by domain experts familiar with these data sets. A falsification test, a statistical test requiring metrics and p-values, is essential to identify any discrepancies between predictions and actual data. Domain experts specializing in Monte Carlo simulation will then refine their conceptual frameworks and specific model parameters through Bayesian inversion techniques, given the data. Decision-making processes are informed by the quantification of uncertainty associated with predictive outcomes, necessitating collaborative communication among all involved experts. Rather than providing formal definitions of AI, I will use an illustrative example for clarity. Consider a self-driving car tasked with navigating from one location to another. The goal is to arrive safely and lawfully, within a reasonable time. To achieve this, the car must execute various maneuvers, such as steering, accelerating, and braking. Sensors assess the current state of the road, and predictions are made regarding future actions of the car and other road users or objects. The primary components can be delineated as follows: Rewards, representing the overarching objectives of safety, legality, and efficiency; Actions, encompassing maneuvers such as steering and braking; and State, pertaining to the prevailing conditions on the road, including the presence and movement of other vehicles, pedestrians, and their respective velocities.
