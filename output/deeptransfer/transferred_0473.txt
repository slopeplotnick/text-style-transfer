Using scikit-learn, we trained Majority, Random, n-gram SVM, Logistic Regression, Random Forest, and Decision Tree classifiers as baseline models. Feature extraction was performed using TF-IDF and count-vector representations. While the baseline models were trained on a CPU, all experiments involving transformer models were conducted in a GPU-enabled environment (Google Colab T4). The primary evaluation metric for our analysis was the micro-averaged F1-score, complemented by accuracy, precision, and recall to ensure a fair comparison. In our study, we evaluated a diverse set of models, including m-BERT cased, XLM-RoBERTa base, BanglaBERT, DistilBERT cased, and the baseline models, to classify hate speech in the BLP2025 dataset. Table 2 presents the classification report for subtask 1A. BanglaBERT achieved the highest accuracy of 0.71, outperforming all other models in our evaluation. Moreover, BanglaBERT demonstrated superior precision and recall, achieving scores of 0.70 and 0.71, respectively. The official scoring metric for subtask 1A was the micro-F1 score, for which BanglaBERT achieved a score of 0.71, further solidifying its dominance. In comparison, the BERT multilingual base (m-BERT) and XLM-RoBERTa base achieved micro-F1 scores of 0.66 and 0.67, respectively, both surpassing the baseline models. However, DistilBERT fell short with a micro-F1 score of 0.57, which was lower than the performance of the baseline SVM and Logistic Regression models. Among the baseline models, SVM demonstrated the best performance with an F1 score of 0.61, while the random baseline exhibited the lowest performance with an F1 score of 0.16. Notably, the SVM and Logistic Regression models achieved the highest F1 scores of 0.63 and 0.62, respectively, among the baseline methods.
