The traditional approach begins with performing a deterministic inversion, which is then used to make a deterministic interpretation, as shown in Figure 2. Following this, an expert geologist interprets the deterministic inversion by identifying geometrical shapes, such as intrusions relevant to exploration. These interpretations are carried out using software that focuses on a single deterministic model. In this methodology, geophysical inversion precedes geological interpretation. While it could be argued that regularization terms incorporate geological knowledge, this claim is both vague and limited in scope. Consequently, the approach leads to a redundant application of the same geological knowledge: first through the regularization terms and then again during the interpretative process. This redundancy results in a self-perpetuating cycle, fraught with circular reasoning. In contrast, the Bayesian framework addresses these issues simultaneously, treating them as a unified problem. Within this framework, plausible solutions—such as geometries—must be articulated before performing any inversion. Bayesian inversion offers relatively straightforward solutions that are easy to implement and widely applicable. To illustrate this further, let us revisit Figure 2 and consider the process of constructing a prior probability model. The starting point is to ask: what is the minimum amount of information we have about the subsurface before acquiring any data? First, it is well-established that petrophysical properties fall within characteristic ranges, and with knowledge of the lithologies involved, these ranges can be further refined. Second, spatial variation of these properties is neither random nor uniform across large areas. Instead, the processes responsible for deposit formation induce distinct, case-specific variations in these properties. Conceptually, the minimum required information includes constraints on histograms and the presence of spatial correlation. Mathematically, this spatial correlation can be expressed through a spatial covariance model, commonly referred to as a variogram in geostatistics. However, the specific parameters of the variogram—such as the mean, ranges, and anisotropy—remain uncertain and can vary significantly, though not randomly.
