Sentiment analysis has become a central component of modern Natural Language Processing (NLP), enabling applications in digital governance, public health surveillance, financial inclusion, and user-generated content moderation. However, despite significant advancements in deep learning and multilingual language modeling, South African low-resource languages remain critically underrepresented in sentiment analysis research. This underrepresentation is evident in the scarcity of annotated datasets, the inadequacy of lexical resources, and the limited cross-lingual transferability of existing models. Consequently, sentiment analysis models trained on high-resource languages such as English and French often exhibit diminished performance in African contexts, thereby exacerbating the digital divide and restricting equitable access to NLP technologies. While existing research in multilingual sentiment analysis has largely focused on machine translation pipelines, cross-lingual embeddings, and monolingual lexicon construction, these approaches are not without their shortcomings. For instance, machine-translated lexicons frequently introduce errors and cultural misalignments, particularly in morphologically rich African languages. Additionally, corpus-based lexicon extraction methods require large, domain-specific datasets, which are typically unavailable for low-resource languages. Furthermore, although state-of-the-art African language models such as AfroXLMR and AfriBERTa have shown improved performance, their effectiveness remains constrained by the lack of high-quality sentiment lexicons necessary for guiding classification in multilingual settings. To address these challenges, this paper introduces TriLex, a novel three-stage retrieval-augmented framework designed to support scalable sentiment lexicon expansion for African low-resource languages. The TriLex framework comprises three key stages: corpus-based lexical extraction to identify candidate sentiment-bearing terms, cross-lingual semantic mapping to align these terms across English, French, and target African languages, and Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) to refine, validate, and expand the lexicon using large language models conditioned on multilingual retrieval signals. Moreover, we develop and publicly release an extensive multilingual sentiment lexicon encompassing multiple South African languages, thereby fostering reproducible research and advancing linguistic resource development. Finally, we conduct the first comprehensive evaluation of AfroXLMR and AfriBERTa using a lexicon-expanded resource for isiXhosa and isiZulu, demonstrating the potential of TriLex to bridge the gap in sentiment analysis for low-resource African languages.
