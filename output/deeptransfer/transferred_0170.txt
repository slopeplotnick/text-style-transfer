However, these methods exhibit significant limitations in two core dimensions. Regarding data types, most studies predominantly rely on numerical time series data, with the utilization of textual information limited to superficial feature extraction. This constraint hampers the comprehensive integration of multi-source information, such as market sentiment and policy interpretation. In terms of model mechanisms, while neural networks enhance pattern recognition capabilities, they lack a deep understanding of the semantic logic underlying scenarios. As a result, they fail to establish causal relationships between event logic and price movements, thereby restricting their adaptability in complex market environments. The emergence of large language models (LLMs) has introduced a paradigm shift from historical pattern recognition to advanced memory mechanisms. For instance, FinMem[22] developed a specialized financial memory bank that achieves preliminary memory functions by storing news summaries and event embeddings, while MagicNet[12] proposed a hierarchical memory architecture that organizes market information into layers based on temporal granularity. Nevertheless, these approaches face notable challenges in memory structure design. Specifically, memory units often consist of isolated textual fragments or embedding vectors, lacking a coherent modeling of continuous event development trajectories. Additionally, retrieval mechanisms predominantly rely on semantic similarity, which limits their ability to address complex scenarios. Overall, while these methodologies represent progress in transitioning from historical patterns to memory mechanisms, substantial advancements are still required to effectively mine and understand critical information. This paper focuses on the domain of event-driven stock prediction, which seeks to uncover the fundamental causal mechanisms driving stock price fluctuations. Before the advent of LLMs, this field evolved from studying the impact of events on stock returns to the automatic identification of event signals in text using methods such as Bag-of-Words[24], topic models[1], and advanced deep learning frameworks like BERT[8]. However, a persistent limitation across these approaches is their reliance on a "numerically-driven" paradigm[6, 16]. Regardless of how event labels or sentiment scores are extracted, they are ultimately transformed into features for temporal models such as LSTM or Transformer. Consequently, these models primarily learn statistical correlations rather than the intrinsic logic of events, leading to the loss of critical semantic details and hindering their capacity for deep reasoning and comprehensive analysis.
