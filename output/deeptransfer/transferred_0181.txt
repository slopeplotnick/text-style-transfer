The feasibility of LLMs for addressing complex optimization problems is demonstrated through their application in resource allocation tasks using dynamic prompting. To validate their computational efficiency, LLM performance is systematically compared against algorithmic solutions such as DQN and Q-learning. This framework achieves its objectives through a structured progression from task decomposition to balanced allocation optimization. Long-horizon construction tasks are initially decomposed into manageable subtasks using object-centric and skill-centric strategies derived from the SMART-LLM framework (Kannan et al. 2024). Object-centric decomposition categorizes tasks based on physical objects and their associated manipulations, while skill-centric decomposition organizes subtasks according to specific robot capabilities, such as precision handling, force application, or delicate maneuvering. These subtasks are then assigned to heterogeneous robotic units through a capability-aware matching mechanism that aligns robot proficiencies with task demands. To optimize task completion, the LTAA framework employs LangGraph orchestration, which probabilistically models the likelihood of success for each robot-subtask pairing based on feature-capability alignment. However, initial testing revealed workload imbalances, as success-probability maximization disproportionately burdened high-capability robots while underutilizing specialized units. Consequently, phase-adaptive allocation strategies were introduced to dynamically adjust decision priorities throughout the project. Early phases prioritized success rates, while later phases emphasized workload equity, ensuring both mission success and fair resource utilization. The methodology development, as illustrated in Fig. 1, followed three stages. Initially, the SMART-LLM framework was implemented to assess LLM feasibility for MRTA, uncovering challenges such as validation inconsistencies and output reliability issues. Subsequently, the Self Corrective-Agent Architecture addressed these issues through multi-stage validation and hierarchical retry mechanisms. Finally, the LTAA framework integrated these validation principles with phase-adaptive allocation strategies, enabling systematic benchmarking against traditional optimization methods. The authors first describe the implementation of the SMART-LLM framework and the critical challenges encountered, including validation inconsistencies, coordination inefficiencies, and reliability concerns. These limitations prompted the development of the phase-adaptive and self-corrective Self Corrective-Agent LangGraph Architecture, which incorporates multi-stage validation protocols and retry mechanisms to mitigate these deficiencies. These innovations culminated in the LTAA framework, which was systematically benchmarked against traditional optimization methods. Using the TEACH dataset as a standard benchmark, the LTAA framework was directly compared with Q-learning, DQN, and DP under consistent task conditions. Evaluation criteria included workload balance distribution, feature-specific performance across task types, and reasoning quality, providing a comprehensive assessment of both allocation efficacy and overall system performance.
