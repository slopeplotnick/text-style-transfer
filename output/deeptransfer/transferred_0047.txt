Achieving optimal performance of these models across diverse scenarios remains challenging. Despite leveraging extensive and varied training datasets, these models frequently fail to generalize effectively to novel camera angles, lighting variations, or other unforeseen scene alterations. Consequently, this limitation significantly reduces their utility in real-world environments, which are characterized by constant change and unpredictability. To address these challenges, researchers typically employ two primary strategies: one emphasizes the utilization of more diverse datasets, while the other concentrates on enhancing the model's spatial representation capabilities. The data-centric approach entails leveraging extensive datasets, such as Libero-Plus, which encompass a wide array of perspectives to acclimate models to varied visual scenarios. However, the acquisition of such data in real-world settings is both costly and labor-intensive, posing significant difficulties for sustained implementation. Conversely, representation-centric methodologies endeavor to enhance models' comprehension of geometry, particularly the spatial configuration of objects in three-dimensional space, through the utilization of multi-view supervision or specialized architectures tailored for 3D awareness. These approaches facilitate the models' capacity to manage variations in viewpoint; however, they continue to exhibit vulnerabilities to extraneous visual factors such as background clutter and illumination changes, indicating a persistent limitation in achieving comprehensive visual adaptability. Predominantly, it is presumed that augmenting data or refining 3D-centric designs is requisite for bolstering model robustness. Nevertheless, there is a paucity of research investigating whether the issue fundamentally stems from the manner in which spatial information is represented. To elucidate this issue, we employ a methodology that conceptually bifurcates the model into two distinct components: the first component is responsible for Spatial Modeling, executed by the visual encoder. This component determines spatial relationships such as object positions, orientations, contact relations, and occlusion structures, thereby generating the spatial representations essential for subsequent processing stages. The second component pertains to Physical Modeling, which synthesizes spatial representations with task directives and historical action data to facilitate decision-making and formulate executable action sequences. A change in camera angle predominantly impacts the spatial configuration of the scene, while leaving the underlying task semantics and action dynamics largely unaffected. Therefore, performance degradation can primarily be attributed to misalignment within the spatial modeling component, despite the physical modeling component remaining functional. In essence, while the model retains its capacity for reasoning and planning, inaccuracies in the spatial representations disrupt its ability to effectively translate this information into actionable outputs.
