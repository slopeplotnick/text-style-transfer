Achieving robustness and generalization across diverse scenarios remains a significant challenge for these models. Despite leveraging large and varied training datasets, they often exhibit substantial performance degradation when exposed to unseen camera angles, lighting variations, or unexpected scene alterations. This inherent fragility limits their utility in real-world environments, where visual conditions are inherently dynamic and unpredictable. Existing strategies to address these challenges can be broadly categorized into data-centric and representation-centric approaches. Data-centric methods focus on enhancing robustness by increasing visual diversity, often utilizing large-scale, multi-view datasets such as Libero-Plus. While this approach improves exposure to varied visual conditions, the resource-intensive and laborious nature of real-world data collection poses significant constraints on its scalability and long-term sustainability. Conversely, representation-centric methods aim to improve robustness by enhancing geometric consistency, employing techniques such as multi-view supervision or specialized architectures designed for 3D awareness. These methods improve viewpoint invariance by enabling models to better understand spatial relationships, such as object positions, orientations, and occlusion structures. However, despite these advancements, they remain vulnerable to extraneous visual factors like background clutter and lighting changes, underscoring an incomplete adaptability to diverse visual conditions. While prior work often assumes that increasing data volume or developing more sophisticated 3D-focused designs is necessary for improving robustness, few studies explicitly investigate whether these challenges stem from the representation of spatial information itself. To address this gap, we adopt a conceptual framework that decomposes the model into two components: (1) Spatial Modeling, executed by the visual encoder, which constructs spatial relationships—such as positions, orientations, contact relations, and occlusion structures—from the visual input to generate spatial representations for downstream tasks; and (2) Physical Modeling, which integrates spatial representations with task-specific instructions and historical action data to perform reasoning and generate executable action sequences. Notably, a change in camera angle primarily affects the spatial configuration of the scene rather than the underlying task semantics or action dynamics. Therefore, the observed performance degradation can largely be attributed to misalignment within the Spatial Modeling component, even as the Physical Modeling component remains functionally competent. In essence, while the model retains its reasoning and planning capabilities, inaccuracies in spatial representations disrupt its ability to effectively translate this information into actionable outputs.
