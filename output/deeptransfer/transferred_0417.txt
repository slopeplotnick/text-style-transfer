Engineers have increasingly adopted hierarchical systems where supervisory agents mediate between human operators and worker agents. These supervisory agents evaluate outputs, flag anomalies, and possess the authority to halt processes when predefined thresholds are exceeded. This conceptual framework anticipates the "Institutional AI" paradigm, which will be elaborated upon in subsequent sections. However, implementing supervisory mechanisms alone is insufficient. Since supervisory models share the same representational framework as the agents they oversee, they inherit similar biases and are susceptible to participating in analogous feedback loops. Moreover, excessive alignment among supervisors can lead to correlated failures or even deliberate collusion at the oversight level. Therefore, effective governance requires not only additional layers of supervision but also diverse oversight mechanisms, redundant observation pathways, and a clear delineation of authority among supervisory entities. As systems evolve toward multi-agent autonomy, the challenge of ensuring safety becomes even more pronounced. Centralized control or static regulations are no longer adequate; instead, resilience must be distributed—networks must develop the capacity to independently detect, address, and rectify anomalies through internal communication. In such ecosystems, the failure of one model does not occur in isolation but cascades, affecting others within the network. Thus, understanding the origins of collective instability and devising methods to measure it are essential prerequisites for advancing AI safety. The growing interaction between language models further highlights the inadequacy of existing risk frameworks—such as bias, misuse, and vulnerabilities—in addressing the complexities of distributed AI systems. While individual models can be audited, benchmarked, and regulated, their interactions create new dimensions of failure that current taxonomies fail to capture. To address this, we propose the concept of the Emergent Systemic Risk Horizon (ESRH), which defines the threshold at which the collective dynamics of interacting models become unstable, even when each individual model operates within acceptable parameters.
