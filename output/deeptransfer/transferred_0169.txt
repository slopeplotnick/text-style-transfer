This paper addresses a critical issue by proposing a novel perspective on safety, emphasizing that it should be conceptualized as emerging from interactions within systems rather than being confined to individual models. The concept of the Emergent Systemic Risk Horizon (ESRH) is introduced to elucidate the conditions and mechanisms through which systems may become unstable. The discourse systematically progresses from dyadic interactions to complex multi-agent systems, subsequently addressing the diverse risk typologies inherent in such configurations. It culminates in the proposition of a framework termed Institutional AI, designed to effectively mitigate these risks. Currently, language models are engineered with a primary focus on human-model interactions, employing mechanisms such as prompts and filters to inhibit harmful content and ensure compliance with established protocols. This approach operates under the presumption that the model's behavior remains observable and relatively stable. However, language models are intricate systems whose behavior is contingent upon factors such as their latent state, conversational history, and the orchestration context in which they operate. Minor contextual perturbations can result in divergent reasoning trajectories, despite adherence to surface-level constraints. Moreover, this complexity is exacerbated when models begin to interact with one another. In these configurations, the output of one model serves as the input for another, potentially generating feedback loops that exacerbate minor deviations. Empirical evaluations, such as those conducted using the JAILJUDGE benchmark, illustrate that multi-agent interactions yield results markedly distinct from those observed in single-model assessments. Furthermore, investigations within simulated market environments reveal that even independently aligned models can spontaneously coordinate to achieve supracompetitive equilibria, thereby manifesting strategic behaviors that only emerge through inter-agent interactions. Therefore, this analysis indicates that the effective performance of individual components within a system does not inherently ensure the safety of the system as a whole. It is imperative to transition the focus from isolated examination of individual prompts or actions towards a comprehensive evaluation of the system's communicative and interactive dynamics.
