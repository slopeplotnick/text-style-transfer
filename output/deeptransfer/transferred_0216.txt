Based on the decomposition of item understanding, query interpretation, and path mapping, we can synthesize the essential requirements for constructing an effective relevance model. To effectively navigate extensive item corpora and accommodate diverse user intents—ranging from explicit specifications to abstract reasoning—the model must possess a sophisticated, composite set of capabilities. We summarize these core requirements as follows: 

- Reasoning and Knowledge Integration: The ability to leverage external knowledge and logical inference to resolve ambiguities in query interpretation and accurately align abstract user intents with path construction. 
- Multi-modal Understanding and Matching: The capacity to integrate and synchronize textual and visual information, which is critical for handling items with significant visual components in item understanding and path following. 
- Complex Rule Adherence: The capability to learn and adhere to intricate rules, ensuring the consistency and reliability of the path-following process.

From a formulation perspective, the relevance task can be regarded as a classification problem. Initially, methodologies employed prompts to establish the task framework and utilized responses to demonstrate outcomes, subsequently refining the models through supervised learning techniques. However, this straightforward approach proved inadequate when confronted with datasets that diverged from those on which the model had been trained. Researchers discovered that encouraging the model to engage in problem-solving processes prior to generating responses could effectively enhance its performance in challenging scenarios. Given the constraints on computational resources and the specific requirements of application scenarios, models with relatively modest parameter sizes are generally preferred. However, these models exhibit limitations in reasoning capabilities and instruction adherence when solely relying on prompt-based methodologies. Therefore, the prevailing strategy involves employing more advanced teacher models, such as GPT-4 and DeepSeek-R1, to generate reasoning data. Subsequently, these reasoning capabilities are distilled into smaller models through supervised fine-tuning (SFT). Furthermore, some researchers incorporate a reinforcement learning phase post-distillation, utilizing techniques such as offline reinforcement learning—exemplified by Direct Preference Optimization or KTO—or online reinforcement learning, as demonstrated by Group Relative Policy Optimization.
