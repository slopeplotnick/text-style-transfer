While LLMs generally excel across a broad range of tasks due to their larger capacity and advanced instruction-following capabilities, SLMs demonstrate competitive and, in some cases, superior performance on specific tasks, particularly those related to depression and stress prediction. However, for other tasks, despite notable advancements, SLMs still underperform compared to LLMs (Xu et al., 2024). This suggests that SLMs can be highly effective when their linguistic processing capabilities align closely with the demands of the task. Nevertheless, a critical limitation persists: SLMs struggle to manage long input sequences due to token-length constraints and reduced ability to maintain coherence over extended contextual dependencies (Kumar, 2025). Consequently, their applicability diminishes in scenarios requiring nuanced comprehension of lengthy or complex narratives, where LLMs retain a distinct advantage (Lu et al., 2024). Therefore, our findings advocate for a shift from size-centric scaling to purpose-driven optimization in mental-health AI. As efficiency, transparency, safety, and user privacy (Zhang et al., 2021) gain prominence in clinical machine learning, the balance between performance and practicality will shape the future of digital mental-health tools. Notably, SLMs are already addressing emerging needs for in-situ, low-latency, and privacy-preserving early detection of mental-health issues (Harari et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2018), while simultaneously aligning with the growing demand for transparent and accountable AI in healthcare settings (Wies et al., 2021; Smith et al., 2023). By demonstrating that fine-tuned SLMs, optimized through adaptive cross-dataset training strategies, can match or surpass LLMs in predicting mental-health outcomes, this study highlights the potential of smaller, task-specific models as a more sustainable and ethically sound alternative for real-world mental-health monitoring. This raises the question: what tasks are best suited for SLMs? Our findings reveal that cross-dataset training is not only feasible but advantageous for predicting depression, stress, and suicidal ideation, particularly when paired with an adaptive loss function. Moreover, this aligns with observations by Yao et al. (Benton et al., 2017), who found that shared latent representations across related affective tasks can enhance predictive performance. Unlike their multi-task framework, however, our approach achieves comparable cross-task benefits through a parameter-efficient fine-tuning strategy (LoRA), which significantly reduces computational overhead and memory requirements. This underscores that even lightweight models can effectively leverage diverse datasets, provided their optimization objectives are carefully calibrated.
