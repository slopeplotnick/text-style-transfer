EMOPIA. The research literature on affective music generation often highlights a lack of large-scale, human-labeled MIDI datasets. Most available datasets are small, typically containing fewer than 200 MIDI files paired with emotion labels. We utilize the largest dataset available, EMOPIA, which comprises 1,087 music clips from 387 songs, annotated for emotion by four human annotators. This dataset includes four unique emotion labels, each corresponding to different quadrants in the valence-arousal plane, with more information provided in the Appendix (Section B). GiantMIDI-Piano. Another dataset employed in this study is GiantMIDI-Piano, which has been used in various research areas such as classical music analysis and symbolic music generation. It contains 10,855 solo piano works composed by 2,786 different composers. To integrate our text data with EMOPIA files, we categorized the 27 emotion labels into four AV quadrants. This categorization was achieved using the NRC VAD lexicon, a comprehensive lexicon of human-annotated arousal and valence scores for 55,000 words. Using this lexicon, we assigned each emotion label to one of the four quadrant categories, constructing GoEmotionsAV. The resulting mappings and their positions on the AV plane are illustrated in Figure 1, with further details on the VAD lexicon and its relevance to EMOPIA provided in Appendix A. We use the Musical Instrument Digital Interface (MIDI) as our format for encoding and generating symbolic music data due to its capacity to encode musical performance data through discrete parametric events. Moreover, it is a compact representation that effectively conveys emotion-related aspects of music. The widespread availability of MIDI datasets is advantageous for training purposes. In our training process, we view each MIDI file as a sequence of discrete musical events, enabling the use of sequence-to-sequence models for effective learning and generation.
