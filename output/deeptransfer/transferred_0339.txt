EMOPIA. Affective music generation research often suffers from a lack of large-scale human-labeled MIDI datasets. Most existing datasets are relatively small, typically containing fewer than 200 MIDI files annotated with emotion labels [32], [33]. To address this limitation, we utilize EMOPIA [31], the largest available dataset in this domain. It comprises 1,087 music clips derived from 387 songs, with emotional annotations provided by four human annotators. The dataset includes four distinct emotion categories, each corresponding to a quadrant of the valence-arousal plane. Additional details about EMOPIA can be found in the Appendix (Section B). GiantMIDI-Piano. Another dataset used in this study is GiantMIDI-Piano [34], which has been widely employed in research areas such as classical music analysis and symbolic music generation. This dataset contains 10,855 solo piano compositions created by 2,786 composers. To align our text data with the EMOPIA files, we categorized 27 emotion labels into four AV quadrants. This categorization was achieved using the NRC VAD lexicon [35], a comprehensive resource that provides arousal and valence scores for 55,000 words. Each emotion was systematically assigned to one of the four quadrant labels, resulting in the construction of GoEmotionsAV. We use the Musical Instrument Digital Interface (MIDI) format to encode and generate symbolic music data due to its ability to compactly store detailed musical performance information while effectively capturing emotion-related aspects of music. Moreover, the widespread availability of MIDI files makes them particularly advantageous for training purposes. In our training process, each MIDI file is represented as a sequence of discrete musical events, enabling the application of sequence-to-sequence models for effective learning and music generation.
