Our investigation encompassed three interrelated domains: methodologies incorporating external feedback, strategies to minimize reliance on human annotations, and theoretical frameworks concerning the impact of representational structures on the quality of generated outputs. Typically, the alignment of large language models with human preferences is achieved through Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF). This approach employs human preference data to train a model for reward assignment, subsequently refining the policy to achieve optimal outcomes while maintaining proximity to a reference model. This methodology underpins models such as ChatGPT and Llama. However, challenges persist, including the high cost and potential inaccuracies of the data, as well as the susceptibility of reward models to manipulation and their variable efficacy across different contexts. To address these limitations, methodologies such as Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), KTO, Bayesian RLHF, and MaxMin-RLHF endeavor to enhance the policy directly through pairwise comparisons, obviating the need for an explicit value network, albeit still necessitating meticulously curated preference datasets. Several models have been developed to provide scores and feedback for reasoning and evaluation tasks, maintaining supervision external to the model's primary mechanisms. Alternatively, automatic supervision or self-generated signals from the model itself have been proposed as strategies to mitigate reliance on human-annotated labels. Notably, certain methodologies, such as DeepSeek-R1 and TÜLU 3, employ programmatic feedback mechanisms in lieu of human input.
