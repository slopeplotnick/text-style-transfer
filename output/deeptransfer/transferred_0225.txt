Recent advancements in reasoning techniques have enhanced the cognitive capabilities of large language models (LLMs), raising expectations for their potential to deliver precise and dependable information. However, current findings indicate that iterative reasoning processes may contribute to the reinforcement of pre-existing beliefs rather than promoting truth-seeking behavior. In this study, we introduce a systematic evaluation framework to assess belief entrenchment in LLM reasoning by employing the Martingale property from Bayesian statistics. This concept implies that, under rational belief updating, future beliefs should align with current beliefs, rendering future belief states unpredictable solely based on present beliefs. To identify deviations from this principle, we developed the Martingale Score, an unsupervised, regression-based metric designed to detect instances where models fail to update their beliefs in accordance with Bayesian principles. Across diverse domains, including event forecasting, subjective inquiries, and academic peer review, we observed a consistent tendency for models to adhere rigidly to their existing beliefs, a phenomenon we term belief entrenchment. Through comprehensive experiments, we identified which models, reasoning techniques, and domains are more prone to belief entrenchment. Furthermore, the Martingale Score was demonstrated to possess predictive capabilities regarding model accuracy in domains where ground truth labels are established. This finding underscores the utility of the Martingale Score in assessing model efficacy in truth-seeking endeavors, despite its design to function independently of known correct answers. Consider an individual who persistently refuses to acknowledge their errors or a sovereign who exclusively heeds the counsel of sycophants. Such individuals frequently exhibit a tendency to adhere to pre-existing beliefs, a phenomenon often perpetuated by selective exposure to information that corroborates their viewpoints or due to external influences.
