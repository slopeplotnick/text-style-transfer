System prompts provide task-agnostic control over LLM behavior by defining roles, styles, or policies applicable across various tasks. Prior research has shown that elements such as personas, emotions, and jailbreak triggers can influence response style and reasoning, although they do not always enhance task accuracy. Despite their increasing use, there remains a significant gap in understanding the structured optimization of system prompts. Recent disclosures of system prompts from Grok and Claude reveal the presence of intricate, manually constructed rules. While contemporary methodologies indicate that system prompts can be automatically optimized through genetic algorithms, such optimization has been limited to monolingual and single-metric contexts. The implementation of multilingual systems presents challenges, including language-specific degradation and cross-lingual inconsistencies. Multilingual LLMs frequently produce divergent responses to identical queries posed in different languages due to the unequal distribution of knowledge across these languages. Techniques such as Cross-Lingual Thought Prompting and multilingual self-consistency provide partial mitigation; however, they typically necessitate multi-turn prompting strategies. Despite these advancements, a comprehensive framework for optimizing system prompts to ensure consistent and accurate performance across all languages remains elusive. In addition to conventional evaluation metrics, recent research has increasingly emphasized examining reasoning processes in LLMs by decomposing their responses into comprehensible reasoning components. However, these investigations have predominantly concentrated on surface-level phenomena, such as employing text prefill to regulate output language, without thoroughly probing the underlying reasoning mechanisms of the models. This study addresses this limitation by establishing a connection between system prompt design and multilingual reasoning processes, demonstrating how these reasoning patterns align with cross-lingual generalization performance.
