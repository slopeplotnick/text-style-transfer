To evaluate the impact of a verifier on a solver's performance, we introduce a metric that predicts the expected improvement achieved through the use of a verifier in rejection sampling. To validate this metric, we conduct experiments across various solver-verifier combinations, selecting 12 models from four post-training families, specifically the three smallest models from each family. In these experiments, the solver iteratively generates solutions for each problem in the dataset until the verifier identifies one as correct, with a maximum of nine attempts allowed. If no correct solution is identified, the final attempt is retained. The results, along with the predicted improvements, are presented in Figure 4. While minor improvements may be less noticeable due to their subtlety and the nine-attempt constraint, the overall trends align with our theoretical expectations. Notably, self-assessment tends to be less effective than evaluations conducted by models from different families. This observation underscores the robustness of our hypothesized improvements, which require minimal additional effort for validation. A cognitive bias, wherein individuals perceive answers resembling their own as more likely to be correct, appears to parallel the interactions between solvers and verifiers. Specifically, models with strong reasoning capabilities exhibit limited improvement from self-verification but demonstrate significant gains when evaluated by distinct models, highlighting a comparable bias. Ultimately, when a solver and verifier share similar reasoning patterns, the verifier is more likely to accept incorrect solutions, thereby reducing the potential for improvement. By contrast, employing a verifier with a divergent reasoning approach mitigates this issue and enhances performance.
