We propose a method to estimate the potential improvement in a solver's performance when employing a verifier for rejection sampling, introducing the concept of verifier gain. This metric simulates the improvement obtained from a verifier during test-time rejection sampling, addressing the limitations of relying solely on verifier accuracy. To evaluate the predictive accuracy of this metric, we conduct systematic rejection sampling experiments across various solver-verifier combinations derived from a subset of 12 post-trained models. This subset comprises the three smallest models from each of four distinct post-training families. For each problem instance, the solver iteratively generates candidate solutions until the verifier identifies one as correct, with a maximum of nine attempts permitted. In instances where a correct solution is not identified, the final attempt is retained. The obtained results, alongside anticipated enhancements, are illustrated in Figure 4. Occasionally, minor improvements may not be immediately apparent, and the constraint of a nine-attempt limit may obscure comprehensive analysis. Nevertheless, the overall findings align with our initial expectations. Moreover, it has been observed that self-assessment of one's work is less effective compared to peer evaluation, particularly when the evaluator belongs to a different demographic group. This observation underscores the robustness of our method of hypothesizing improvements, which does not necessitate extensive additional effort for validation. There exists a prevalent cognitive bias wherein individuals tend to perceive answers resembling their own as more likely to be correct. Our findings indicate a parallel phenomenon in the interaction between solvers and verifiers. As previously demonstrated, models proficient in reasoning derive minimal benefit from self-verification but exhibit enhanced performance when evaluated by distinct models, thereby revealing a similar bias. The principal insight is that when the cognitive processes of a solver and verifier are aligned, the verifier is predisposed to accept erroneous solutions, consequently diminishing the potential for improvement. Employing a verifier that employs divergent reasoning methodologies mitigates the issue at hand.
