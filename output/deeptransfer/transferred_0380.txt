As we strive to enhance chatbots using language models, it is crucial to address their ability to accommodate diverse linguistic expressions—not just for fairness, but to achieve the robustness and versatility necessary for real-world applications. While voice-based language models have made significant progress, their limitations in handling diverse linguistic variations—such as accents, dialects, and sociolects—pose ongoing challenges and opportunities for improvement. Historically, speech processing models have struggled with the complexities of accent and dialect diversity. Despite the impressive capabilities of language models, they still reflect the biases inherent in the datasets on which they are trained. These models often prioritize standard accents or high-resource languages, leading to suboptimal performance for speakers with less-represented accents. Therefore, it is imperative to develop models that can generalize across diverse phonetic and prosodic patterns. Research on in-context learning-based adaptation and retrieval-augmented clustering could significantly enhance the ability of these models to accommodate previously unencountered accents, even with limited data availability. Conventional evaluation metrics, such as word error rate (WER), are often inadequate for capturing the true performance disparities across diverse linguistic groups. Thus, we must expand our evaluation frameworks to include fairness-driven metrics that assess model performance across different demographics, accents, and languages. Furthermore, error analysis should extend beyond merely quantifying inaccuracies to identifying the specific demographic groups disproportionately impacted by model biases. Such an evaluative approach is essential for fostering the development of more equitable and inclusive models. Moreover, the inherent diversity within a language, as reflected in variations across age, gender, socioeconomic status, and professional domains, introduces further complexity that must be addressed. Traditional speech models frequently exhibit a tendency to overemphasize homogenous data, thereby predominantly capturing the speech patterns characteristic of dominant groups. Rectifying this issue extends beyond merely incorporating a wider array of diverse data; it necessitates the implementation of advanced methodologies such as style transfer and speaker adaptation. These approaches enable models to effectively process a broad spectrum of linguistic and paralinguistic cues. Particularly, an emphasis on prosody, intonation, and speaker emotion is crucial for accurately interpreting speech that conveys social or emotional nuances.
