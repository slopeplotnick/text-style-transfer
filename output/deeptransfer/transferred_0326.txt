A journal entry, in accounting terms, represents the fundamental record of a financial transaction, typically involving a debit and a credit entry across one or more accounts. Each entry is enriched with attributes such as the transaction date, a unique identifier, the currency used, the individual responsible for the entry, applicable tax rates, monetary amounts, and, in some cases, a descriptive annotation. These heterogeneous features—spanning numerical, categorical, and textual data—make journal entries an ideal testbed for anomaly detection systems designed to handle multi-type data environments. Furthermore, synthetic and anonymized datasets, which share a common feature space encompassing these attributes, enable consistent model development and evaluation across various contexts. In light of this, researchers have increasingly turned to machine learning (ML) techniques to enhance anomaly detection in accounting datasets. Unsupervised algorithms, such as clustering and Isolation Forest, have proven effective in identifying journal entries that deviate from established patterns, as demonstrated by Herreros-Martinez et al. [2025]. Moreover, studies leveraging synthetic general ledger data have shown that these methods outperform traditional rule-based systems in detecting anomalies, as noted by Gronewald et al. [2024]. However, traditional ML models, which primarily operate on structured datasets, often struggle to capture the nuanced contextual information needed to distinguish genuinely high-risk anomalies from benign outliers. In contrast, the emergence of Large Language Models (LLMs), such as GPT-4, has introduced new possibilities for auditing. These models, with their advanced natural language understanding and reasoning capabilities, have shown potential as AI auditors or co-pilots, assisting humans in analyzing financial data, as highlighted by Gu et al. [2023]. Recent advancements further suggest that LLMs can effectively perform anomaly detection tasks, with models like SigLLM excelling in time-series data analysis (Alnegheimish et al. [2024]) and AnoLLM addressing anomalies in tabular datasets (Tsai et al. [2025]). Notably, these models achieve robust performance without requiring extensive preprocessing or feature engineering. Nevertheless, deploying LLMs in high-stakes domains such as auditing necessitates careful consideration. By their nature, LLMs can produce inconsistent responses, exhibit biases, or even fabricate information. To address these limitations, the research community has developed methodologies to systematically evaluate and govern the outputs of these models. For instance, tools like AuditLLM (Amirizaniani et al. [2024a]) and LLMAuditor (Amirizaniani et al. [2024b]) underscore the importance of oversight and governance when employing LLMs as decision-support mechanisms in critical domains like auditing.
