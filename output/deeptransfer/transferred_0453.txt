Overall, these findings demonstrate that reasoning-based safety alignment not only enhances the efficacy of omnimodal guardrails across diverse input modalities but also facilitates a deeper understanding and generalization in complex safety scenarios where label-only supervision proves inadequate. To assess whether safety knowledge acquired from seen modalities can be effectively applied to unseen ones, we conducted cross-modal evaluations. Specifically, for each experimental setup, OMNIGUARD was trained using data from three modalities for both training and evaluation within seen modalities, while one modality was excluded to evaluate its performance in an unseen context. We report the averaged F1 scores and accuracy metrics across all tests in Table 5. This analysis yields two principal insights. Firstly, OMNIGUARD demonstrates effective transferability of learned knowledge across varying input modalities. The performance on novel input modalities closely approximates that of the trained modalities (79.4 vs. 81.8 F1 on average), indicating that OMNIGUARD effectively discerns and aligns harmful semantic patterns across text, image, video, and audio inputs, thereby acquiring modality-invariant safety representations. Secondly, an anomaly was observed in the audio domain. The F1 score for the audio on which the model was trained was 64.6%, slightly lower than the 65.1% achieved for audio not included in the training dataset. This discrepancy can be attributed to the version of OMNIGUARD that was trained without textual data, which exhibited suboptimal performance on the WildGuard-TTS evaluation. WildGuard-TTS utilizes an audio dataset synthesized from text via a text-to-speech model. Therefore, the absence of textual data during training resulted in diminished model performance in the audio domain, indicating that semantically equivalent harmful content across different modalities, such as text and speech, can exert mutual influence during safety alignment. Moreover, this observation underscores the potential for cross-modal knowledge transfer, wherein information from one modality can be effectively utilized in another modality that conveys equivalent semantic content.
