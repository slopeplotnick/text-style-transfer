A different dataset, AI2-THOR, was employed instead of the TEACH dataset used in the benchmarking phase. This transition was methodologically necessary as it enabled direct replication for feasibility assessment and established a common ground for fair algorithmic comparison. Moreover, it provided an indirect performance comparison with tasks of a similar nature. In contrast, the benchmarking of LTAA against traditional MRTA approaches utilized the same dataset, ensuring consistency in evaluation. Additionally, the authors are working on testing SMART-LLM with the TEACH dataset to achieve a more balanced benchmarking system. Secondly, this study adopted a relatively small evaluation scale. While this facilitated systematic baseline comparisons and thorough capability analysis, real-world construction encompasses significantly more tasks with intricate spatial and temporal constraints that were not fully captured in the evaluation dataset. To enhance generalizability, the study employed abstract features such as elemental/compound and heavy/dexterous to represent construction tasks rather than focusing on specific tasks. This abstraction was also necessitated by the absence of a large-scale database of real-world construction operations. The authors argue that the availability of such a database would enable evaluations of the LTAA framework in additional scenarios involving dynamic replanning and resource conflicts, thereby reinforcing confidence in the framework’s robustness. Furthermore, the inherent variability in LLM outputs raises reliability concerns for safety-critical construction operations. While multi-stage validation processes mitigated execution failures, the controlled stochasticity in LLM reasoning, though enabling nuanced decision-making, may lead to allocation variations. Construction environments requiring absolute precision may demand additional verification mechanisms beyond those currently implemented. Finally, when evaluating computational resource requirements, SMART-LLM’s token and runtime costs could not be measured per stage due to the use of a single concatenated prompt across all three stages. To enable comparison with LTAA, which focuses solely on allocation strategies, the study approximated SMART-LLM’s stage-level costs by dividing its total tokens and runtime by three. While this approximation is reasonable, it may not fully capture the true allocation-specific overhead, and future investigations incorporating stage-level instrumentation are necessary for more precise analysis.
