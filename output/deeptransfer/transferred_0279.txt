A different dataset, AI2-THOR, was employed instead of the previously used TEACH dataset. This transition was methodologically justified as it allowed direct replication for feasibility assessment and provided a consistent foundation for fair algorithmic comparison. Additionally, it enabled an indirect evaluation of performance across tasks of a similar nature. However, the benchmarking of LTAA against traditional MRTA methodologies utilized the same dataset to ensure consistency in evaluation. Furthermore, the SMART-LLM framework was tested using the TEACH dataset to establish a more balanced basis for comparison. This study was conducted on a relatively small scale, which facilitated a systematic baseline comparison and an in-depth analysis of system capabilities. However, real-world construction processes involve a significantly broader range of tasks with intricate spatial and temporal complexities that were not fully captured in the evaluation dataset. To enhance generalizability and representation, the study employed abstract features, such as elemental/compound and heavy/dexterous, to describe construction tasks rather than focusing on task-specific details. This abstraction was necessary due to the absence of a large-scale database of real-world construction operations. The authors believe that the development of such a database would enable the evaluation of LTAA in a wider range of scenarios involving dynamic replanning and resource constraints, thereby reinforcing confidence in the framework's robustness. Moreover, the inherent variability in LLM outputs raises reliability concerns in safety-critical construction contexts. While multi-stage validation procedures were implemented to eliminate execution failures, the controlled stochasticity, though enabling more nuanced reasoning, introduces the risk of allocation discrepancies. Construction environments requiring precise execution may necessitate more rigorous verification mechanisms than those currently employed. Additionally, when assessing computational resource requirements, the evaluation of SMART-LLM's stage-specific costs was hindered by the use of a single, unified prompt across all stages. To facilitate comparison with LTAA, which focuses exclusively on allocation, the researchers approximated SMART-LLM's costs by dividing the total tokens and runtime across three stages. While this approximation is reasonable, it may not fully capture the allocation-specific costs. Future research with more detailed stage-level evaluations is needed to achieve a more precise understanding.
