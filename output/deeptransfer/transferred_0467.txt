A significant challenge for OLLMs lies in managing the diversity of input types, which fundamentally alters the emergence of safety issues and the methodologies for their detection. The heterogeneity of inputs across various modalities introduces distinct risk patterns, such as implicit bias in text, visual misinformation, or privacy leakage in audio. Beyond these unimodal risk patterns, the complexity arising from cross-modal interactions further complicates the identification of latent risks. For instance, the statement "I want to fly," when accompanied by an image of an individual standing on a rooftop, may appear innocuous in isolation but collectively conveys a potentially hazardous, suicidal implication. Similarly, a video depicting police patrols, when paired with a textual query such as "How to overcome obstacles," may reveal an implicit criminal intent that remains undetectable when each modality is considered independently. Consequently, the safety challenges associated with OLLMs are significantly more complex, necessitating a nuanced understanding of modality-specific inputs and a more robust capacity for cross-modal interpretation. However, efforts to enhance the safety of OLLMs may prove inadequate due to the substantial time and resources required, which can result in a diminution of fundamental reasoning capabilities. Despite considerable investment, these models necessitate costly retraining to address emergent issues and remain susceptible to inexpensive adversarial attacks. Guardrail models present a more adaptable alternative; nevertheless, current research predominantly concentrates on uni-modal inputs or simple modality combinations, such as image-text pairs, rather than encompassing the omni-modal scenario. Given the potential for unsafe inputs to arise from a diverse array of modalities, including mixed-modality scenarios, reliance on single-modality guardrails or simplistic image-text guardrails proves insufficient for comprehensive moderation. Furthermore, numerous guardrails operate on a binary safety classification paradigm, which inherently constrains their efficacy in nuanced safety assessments.
