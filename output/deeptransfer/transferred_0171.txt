The MODOMA system serves as a laboratory framework for investigating mechanisms of language acquisition. It accommodates both adult and child participants, offering a versatile platform wherein system parameters can be adjusted to simulate various linguistic environments. Moreover, the system explicitly demonstrates the grammatical structures it acquires, thereby enabling researchers to systematically analyze and evaluate its learning processes. This study demonstrates the feasibility of employing a computational setup with multiple agents for the acquisition of a first language. The application of Zipf's law is integral to evaluating the efficacy of the MODOMA system in language acquisition. Furthermore, the experiments conducted in this study utilize a combination of methodologies to classify various word types, such as function and content words, thereby encompassing all components of more complex experimental designs. Consequently, the results of the current study provide a robust foundation for further research. Subsequent investigations may explore the impact of modifying parameters on the grammar acquired by the child agent, the utterances generated, and the interaction with the adult agent. This exploration could encompass the frequency with which the word acquisition process is executed and whether previously established word categories should be revised in light of new information. Another avenue for investigation pertains to the influence of feedback provided by the adult agent on the language acquisition and production processes of the child agent. If successful, the implementation of such experiments would enable the exploration of more advanced learning mechanisms. Furthermore, the MODOMA framework offers the potential to facilitate the examination of more intricate grammatical phenomena. For instance, Shakouri et al. (2025) demonstrate that grammatical categories, including nouns and verbs, can be acquired under experimental conditions analogous to those employed in the study of function and content words. Utilizing advanced statistical methodologies, such as hierarchical agglomerative clustering, these categories are identified in an unsupervised manner, thereby circumventing the need for explicit instruction. Ultimately, the findings of this study represent a significant advancement in the ongoing investigation and refinement of models for grammatical acquisition.
