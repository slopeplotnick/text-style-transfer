The summary is then evaluated against each segment, and the final SummaC score is calculated by averaging the entailment scores across all segments, resulting in a value within the range [0,1]. After visualizing the results with heatmaps and conducting a one-way ANOVA, we examined whether the positional arrangement of the input significantly influences the generated summaries. Figure 3 illustrates the mean scores for the three evaluation metrics across six input orders and three stance categories. For each condition, the mean score is derived by averaging the metric values across all article triplets sharing the same input order and stance. The observed variation in shading and evaluation scores underscores the influence of input positioning on the similarity patterns of LLM-generated summaries. Specifically, summaries consistently achieve higher ROUGE-L and BERTScore values when evaluated against the document presented first in the sequence. Moreover, BERTScore values surpass those associated with Position 2 and Position 3, suggesting that documents occupying the initial position contribute more substantially to the content of the generated summaries. In addressing RQ1, our findings reveal a consistent pattern: the generated summaries exhibit greater similarity to the first article in the input sequence. The metric values observed in our study diverge from those typically reported in the field of summarization research. This discrepancy arises from our methodological approach, which involves comparing the generated summaries directly with the complete original articles, as opposed to evaluating them against human-written reference summaries, as is customary in prior studies. Given that the summaries generated by the model average approximately 220 words, while the source articles vary between 300 and 1600 words, this length discrepancy inherently reduces token-overlap and embedding-based similarity scores. Consequently, we focus on the relative scores under different input conditions rather than the absolute values of the metrics. Additionally, a methodological challenge is posed by the limited availability of metrics specifically designed to evaluate the similarity between a summary and its source documents. Most evaluation frameworks rely on reference-based methodologies, offering limited mechanisms for assessing source alignment. SummaC, which evaluates factual consistency at the sentence level through entailment analysis, tends to yield relatively low scores and demonstrates minimal sensitivity to positional variation. Furthermore, abstractive summaries frequently omit specific details from the source text, and entailment-based scoring frameworks often penalize statements that are unsupported or implicitly inferred. Therefore, even if the model prioritizes information from the initial article, it is improbable that it will replicate its surface forms verbatim. Consequently, SummaC is unable to account for nuanced content biases unless the corresponding explicit supporting sentences are retained.
