Figure 3 delineates the mean values of three evaluative metrics across six distinct input sequences and three stance categories. The mean for each condition is derived by computing the average metric scores across all article triplets sharing identical input sequences and stance orientations. The observed variations in shading and scoring metrics underscore the influence of input positioning on the similarity patterns of summaries generated by large language models (LLMs). Specifically, the summaries exhibit elevated ROUGE-L and BERTScore values when evaluated against the document positioned first in the sequence. Furthermore, BERTScore values surpass those associated with Position 2 and Position 3, indicating that documents presented initially contribute more significantly to the generated summaries. Our findings for Research Question 1 reveal a consistent pattern: the generated summaries exhibit greater similarity to the initial article within the input sequence. However, the metric values obtained in our study diverge from those typically reported in existing summarization literature. This discrepancy arises from our methodological approach, which involves direct comparison of the generated summaries with the complete original articles, as opposed to the conventional practice of evaluating against human-authored reference summaries. The summaries typically contain approximately 220 words, whereas the source articles range between 300 and 1600 words. This discrepancy in length inherently reduces token overlap and similarity scores. Consequently, the analysis emphasizes relative scores across varying input conditions rather than absolute metric values. Moreover, a significant limitation arises from the scarcity of evaluation metrics specifically designed to measure the alignment between a summary and its source documents. Existing evaluation frameworks predominantly rely on reference-based methodologies, thereby offering limited avenues for assessing source alignment. SummaC, which evaluates entailment at the sentence level, tends to yield consistently low scores that exhibit minimal sensitivity to positional variations. Abstract summaries inherently omit certain details from the source material, and scoring systems based on entailment tend to penalize statements that are unsupported or merely implied. Therefore, even if the model prioritizes information from the initial article, it is improbable that it will replicate its precise phrasing. Consequently, SummaC is unable to acknowledge nuanced content biases unless explicit supporting sentences are retained.
