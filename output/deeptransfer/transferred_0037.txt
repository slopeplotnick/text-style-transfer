Self-Consistency (SC) involves aggregating multiple outputs through majority voting. Soft Self-Consistency (Soft-SC) selects the minimum, mean, or product of confidence scores within each category. Generative Self-Aggregation (GSA) prompts the language model to synthesize a novel response based on the contextual information derived from multiple samples. Universal Self-Consistency employs the language model to select the most appropriate response from a set of alternatives. In this study, we examine several advanced language models, including gemini-2.5-flash, qwen3 next-80b, and gpt-oss-120b. Our analysis encompasses two machine translation tasks alongside six tasks derived from the Reward Bench 2 benchmark. This benchmark is designed to evaluate reward models across six domains: factual accuracy, adherence to instructions, mathematical reasoning, safety, task relevance, and resolution of ties. To conduct this evaluation, preference pairs were constructed by generating all possible pairwise combinations from the source dataset associated with each task, which includes both accepted and rejected responses. These pairs were subsequently categorized into 'non-tie' pairs, comprising one accepted and one rejected response, and 'tie' pairs, consisting of either two accepted or two rejected responses. From this extensive dataset, we selected 1000 examples for each of the six tasks to construct the final benchmark. A comprehensive explanation of the vote distributions for each task is provided in Appendix B. The results for various Thinking LLMs, including gemini-2.5-flash, gpt-oss-120b, and qwen3 next-80b, exhibit a consistent qualitative pattern, indicating that our approach is effective across these models. The structural characteristics of the problem, including the frequency with which tasks generate ambiguous instances, the prevalence of directional versus tied votes produced by the language model, and the interaction of these attributes with the MAE-aligned Davidson framework, play a critical role in shaping calibration landscapes. Consequently, certain tasks yield smooth and well-behaved calibration landscapes that demonstrate strong portability, whereas others produce more pronounced and irregular landscapes, resulting in fitted parameters with limited generalizability. Therefore, developing rigorous assessments to anticipate the transferability of one task to another—and to evaluate robustness under significant distribution shifts or out-of-distribution targets—remains a compelling avenue for future investigation.
