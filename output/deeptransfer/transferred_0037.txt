Self-Consistency (SC) involves aggregating multiple outputs through majority voting. Soft Self-Consistency (Soft-SC) selects the minimum, mean, or product of confidence scores within each category. Generative Self-Aggregation (GSA) prompts the language model to synthesize a new response based on the context of multiple samples. Universal Self-Consistency employs the language model to select the most appropriate answer from a set of alternatives. In this study, we examine several advanced language models, including gemini-2.5-flash, qwen3 next-80b, and gpt-oss-120b. Our evaluation encompasses two machine translation tasks alongside six tasks derived from the Reward Bench 2 benchmark. This benchmark is designed to evaluate reward models across six domains: factuality, precise adherence to instructions, mathematical reasoning, safety, concentration, and equivalence. For the purpose of our evaluation, we constructed preference pairs by generating all possible combinations from each task's source dataset, which includes both accepted and rejected responses. These pairs are categorized into 'non-tie' pairs (comprising one accepted and one rejected response) and 'tie' pairs (comprising either two accepted or two rejected responses). From this comprehensive dataset, we select 1,000 instances for each of the six tasks to construct the final benchmark. A detailed exposition of the vote distributions for each task is provided in Appendix B. Results obtained from various advanced language models, including gemini-2.5-flash, gpt-oss-120b, and qwen3 next-80b, exhibit a consistent pattern, thereby demonstrating the efficacy of our approach across these models. The structural characteristics of the problem include the frequency with which the task induces ambiguous cases, the propensity of the language model to produce distinct versus tied votes, and the interaction of these attributes with the MAE-aligned Davidson model. Certain tasks yield smooth and well-behaved calibration landscapes that are readily transferable, whereas others result in more acute landscapes whose fitted parameters exhibit limited generalizability. Developing rigorous methodologies to predict the conditions under which one task can effectively transfer to another—and to evaluate robustness under significant distributional shifts or out-of-distribution targets—represents a compelling avenue for future research.
