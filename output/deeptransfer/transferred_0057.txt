Both CNNs and ViTs underwent identical adversarial training protocols to accurately assess the impact of such training on attack transferability. Contrary to the intuitive expectation that robust models would produce less transferable attacks, our findings consistently reveal the opposite. Adversarially trained (AT) models generate adversarial examples that transfer significantly more effectively than those from standard trained (ST) counterparts. This outcome suggests that the robust features acquired through adversarial training may inadvertently introduce universal vulnerabilities that extend across different architectures, as summarized in Figure 1. Our key contributions are as follows. First, we conducted large-scale adversarial training on both CNN and ViT architectures, covering a total of 36 models. All trained models, along with the training code, will be publicly released in a GitHub repository to support reproducibility and further research. Furthermore, we establish that AT models generate more transferable attacks, revealing a security paradox that challenges current assumptions about robustness. Moreover, a novel attack methodology, termed Momentum Integrated Gradient (MIG), has been developed, building upon the integrated gradient (IG) framework. MIG iteratively refines the attack using a momentum-based approach directed by IG, strategically focusing on the most critical features identified by IG, thereby increasing the likelihood of inducing misclassifications. A critical feature of IG, known as implementation invariance, allows MIG-crafted adversarial examples to be effectively transferred across distinct model architectures, including CNNs and ViTs, as well as between these two types. This level of transferability was unattainable with earlier adversarial methods such as PGD, as illustrated in Figure 2. IG serves as an axiomatic interpretability framework designed to identify the most salient features that contribute to a model's predictive outcomes. It operates by accumulating gradients along a trajectory from a baseline input to the actual input, where the baseline serves as a reference point.
