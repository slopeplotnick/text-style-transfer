While advancements in model innovation continue, the practical deployment of these models often hinges on considerations that are frequently underemphasized in academic discussions: how virality is operationalized, which data types are feasible to collect (e.g., textual versus numeric/metadata), and how evaluation protocols can best reflect real-world constraints such as access limitations, temporal censoring, and class imbalance. This paper adopts an evaluation-first perspective to address these critical design decisions. Specifically, we demonstrate across two contemporary datasets that the formulation of labels and the choice of input modalities—whether relying solely on textual data or augmenting it with additional signals—can be as impactful as architectural modifications. To make these trade-offs explicit, we release reproducible baselines and provide concrete examples. While our primary focus is on evaluation design rather than model innovation, we show that lightweight architectures can achieve, and in some cases surpass, the performance of recent multimodal and graph-based systems on established benchmarks such as FAKENewsNET. We examine two key tasks: fake-news detection and early virality prediction, with an emphasis on identifying the data modalities and operational definitions that are most critical for achieving robust performance under real-world constraints. To this end, we compare textual representations, such as sentence-level transformer embeddings, with lightweight numeric signals, including user statistics, early engagement metrics, and temporal features. These tasks are evaluated on datasets with distinct configurations: one comprising standalone news articles and the other consisting of temporally ordered social media posts. Across these datasets, we address two supervised classification objectives, resulting in four experimental scenarios: (i) fake-news detection and (ii) virality prediction on EvONS and FAKENewsNET, respectively. For fake-news detection, the goal is to classify whether a given item—such as a news article or a series of tweets—is truthful or fabricated. This task is conducted on both EvONS articles and FAKENewsNET tweet series, with results reported using standard classification metrics. For FAKENewsNET, the tweet series are constructed from the repository, with data anonymized through the use of IDs and requiring API hydration. While the Politifact split was obtained through prior academic sharing, the GossipCop dataset could not be retrieved due to current constraints. Each instance represents a sequence of tweets associated with a single news article, illustrating the process of dissemination.
