This approach can be applied across various disciplines: geologists describe conceptual models through diagrams found in academic papers and reports, mathematicians translate these into constraints on prior probability models, and geophysicists use these priors to perform Bayesian inversion. Another possible application involves incorporating additional data, especially since the initial conceptual framework might be fundamentally flawed. This issue highlights a broader challenge in science: how can we prove that the assumptions underlying our methods are correct? The Austrian philosopher Karl Popper dedicated his life to examining such philosophical questions. Popper argued that science should avoid induction, where theories are derived from observations. Instead, he proposed that theories are speculative constructs created by human intellect, often to address the shortcomings of previous theories. Once formulated, these theories must be rigorously tested against observations. Theories that fail to align with observational evidence should be rejected, while those that survive represent the best current explanations. In the context of uncertainty quantification, models can be viewed not as true representations of reality but as hypotheses. Each model corresponds to a distinct hypothesis, constrained by prior knowledge. However, real-world data should not be used to confirm a model but rather to falsify it if it fails to conform to observed data. Popper’s framework offers a way to address—though not entirely resolve—the subjectivity inherent in Bayesian reasoning, which relies on inductive inference, as opposed to the deductive approach of falsification. Specifically, if the prior distribution cannot predict the observed data within the bounds of measurement error, the Bayesian prior is falsified. This means that no matter how many models are sampled from the prior, none align with the empirical data. While Popper’s falsification approach may reduce the subjectivity of the prior after empirical testing, it does not provide a systematic method for revising the prior, leaving such adjustments to human judgment.
