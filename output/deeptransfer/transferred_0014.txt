(1) Knowledge Injection and Reasoning Foundation: Domain knowledge serves as the cognitive backbone for interpreting user queries and identifying item attributes, making its integration essential at the outset. By leveraging this foundational knowledge, the model can deeply comprehend query intent, accurately infer users' core requirements, and extract the corresponding attribute features from item information. (2) Multimodal Information Extraction: Visual representations of items often encapsulate critical attributes that are difficult to fully articulate through text alone, such as aesthetic details and material texture. To address this, this stage incorporates item image information, synthesizing attribute extraction processes that integrate both textual and visual modalities. Through the complementarity and cross-verification of multimodal information, the model is able to construct more comprehensive and precise item representations. (3) Rule-Oriented Discrimination: After successfully recognizing query intent and extracting item attributes, the process of attribute matching and verification relies on objective rules. At this stage, the model synthesizes rule-aware reasoning chains, enabling it to explicitly understand, adhere to, and apply these rules throughout the reasoning process. Step 1: Knowledge and Reasoning. In Section 2.3, knowledge is categorized into general world knowledge and domain-specific e-commerce knowledge. For general knowledge, large language models have already accumulated substantial understanding through large-scale pre-training, allowing them to grasp common concepts and relationships. However, e-commerce scenarios present numerous phrases and expressions that are challenging to interpret based solely on their standalone semantic content. These challenges can be divided into two primary dimensions: (1) Long-tail Queries: These queries pertain to highly specific and uncommon items, for which existing models often lack the necessary prior knowledge to establish effective semantic mappings. For instance, when the query is "strange things are also loved by someone" and the item is described as "novel and interesting toys," the query actually refers to the title of a book. Without this prior knowledge, the model struggles to make accurate relevance judgments based solely on literal semantics. Similar challenges arise with brand-specific terminology, product nicknames, and marketing language unique to the e-commerce domain. (2) Semantically Ambiguous Items: Certain items are so niche that the model lacks sufficient information to interpret them accurately. For example, the term "lying gate" cannot be properly identified or categorized based solely on its textual title without additional contextual understanding of its attributes. Addressing such cases requires the incorporation of supplementary domain-specific e-commerce knowledge to ensure accurate interpretation.
