Prior research is consistent with findings (Sengupta et al., 2025; Subramanian et al., 2025) that performance degradation in smaller models is not always smooth as size decreases. Similarly, our study aligns with prior work on MentalQLM (0.5B) (Shi et al., 2025), which demonstrated strong predictive performance across various mental health outcomes, including depression, stress, and suicidal ideation. These results underscore the viability of fine-tuned smaller language models (SLMs) for mental health prediction, offering a favorable trade-off between computational efficiency and predictive accuracy. Moreover, consistent with recent studies (Ren et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2024a), we observe that core reasoning and question-answering capabilities are preserved post fine-tuning, enabling these models to function as versatile tools with enhanced efficacy in mental health tasks. In summary, our experiments reveal that SLMs within the 3-7 billion parameter range strike an optimal balance between computational efficiency and predictive performance when fine-tuned. While smaller models often struggle with generalization and task adaptation, models within this range effectively classify psychological signals in both zero-shot and few-shot settings. Furthermore, targeted fine-tuning allows our model, Menta, to establish a clear lower bound for model size, achieving robust performance on complex mental health tasks while maintaining computational efficiency. When comparing fine-tuned SLMs to larger language models (LLMs), we find that fine-tuned SLMs deliver comparable or superior accuracy in predicting depression and stress levels, albeit with slightly lower performance in suicidal ideation prediction. Nevertheless, relative to non-fine-tuned smaller models, fine-tuned variants exhibit substantial performance improvements, achieving a more balanced accuracy across diverse mental health prediction tasks. Our findings demonstrate that well-optimized lightweight SLMs can retain essential reasoning and contextual understanding required for mental health inference. The competitiveness of SLMs in this domain stems from the reliance of mental health tasks on localized lexical, affective, and syntactic cues—such as expressions of self-reference, emotional polarity, and temporal framing—which can be effectively captured by moderate-sized models without requiring the extensive world knowledge encoded in larger LLMs. Consistent with prior research (Ji et al., 2021; Shen et al., 2018; Benton et al., 2017), fine-tuning on domain-specific corpora significantly reduces the performance gap between smaller and larger architectures. Extending this evidence, our study demonstrates that parameter-efficient fine-tuning techniques (e.g., LoRA), combined with adaptive, weighted loss functions, enable SLMs to learn discriminative representations for mental health prediction at a fraction of the computational cost.
