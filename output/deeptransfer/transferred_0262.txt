To govern these systems, safety evaluation must transition from mere adherence to regulations to comprehensive system-level observation. This entails assessing how influence propagates among agents, determining whether oversight signals effectively penetrate collective behavior, and identifying when information feedback surpasses the threshold of controllability. The central thesis posited here is that while fundamental safety mechanisms are indispensable, they are insufficient when models are integrated into networks of interaction. Even under optimal local conditions, emergent failures remain inevitable when feedback mechanisms, network topology, and collective incentives predominate. Recently, scholarly investigations have examined the risks inherent in multi-agent systems, offering valuable frameworks for their organization: the Multi-Agent System failure Taxonomy (MAST) and Multi-Agent Risks from Advanced AI (MARAI). MAST categorizes failures into three distinct types, concentrating on risks that originate from the system's architectural design, agent coordination, and verification processes. Conversely, MARAI identifies three failure modes predicated on the modality of inter-agent interaction—namely, miscoordination, conflict, and collusion—that may engender misaligned behaviors. Our novel contribution is twofold: first, to integrate both approaches into a structured, system-centered framework, transitioning from agent-level failures to risks that cannot be attributed to individual system components; second, to formalize this approach through the concept of the Emergent Systemic Risk Horizon (ESRH), which delineates the boundary at which localized reliability transitions into collective instability. The ESRH provides a lexicon and a set of empirical indicators for examining LLM ecosystems as complex adaptive systems rather than as independent agents. This reframing establishes the groundwork for the subsequent analysis. Section 2 examines the empirical and theoretical progression toward multi-agent systems and elucidates the regulatory ramifications of distributed causality. Section 3 articulates the ESRH taxonomy alongside its corresponding evaluative metrics. Finally, Section 4 introduces the Institutional AI architecture as a conceptual framework for embedding governance directly within networks of interacting model collectives.
