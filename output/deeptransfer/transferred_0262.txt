To govern such systems, safety evaluation must shift from rule-based compliance to comprehensive system-level analysis. This requires assessing how influence propagates among agents, determining whether oversight signals effectively shape collective behavior, and identifying when feedback dynamics surpass the threshold of controllability. The central premise is that foundational safety mechanisms, while necessary, are insufficient once models operate within networks of interaction. Even under conditions of flawless local performance, emergent failures remain unavoidable when feedback dynamics, network topology, and collective incentives dominate system behavior. Recently, two frameworks have been introduced to address risks in multi-agent systems: MAST (Multi-Agent System failure Taxonomy) and MARAI (Multi-Agent Risks from Advanced AI). MAST categorizes failures into three types, focusing on risks arising from system architecture, including design limitations, agent coordination challenges, and verification constraints. Conversely, MARAI identifies three failure modes based on inter-agent interaction modalities—miscoordination, conflict, and collusion—that can lead to misaligned behaviors. Building on these insights, our contribution is twofold: 1) integrating both approaches into a structured, system-centered framework that transitions from agent-level failures to risks that cannot be reduced to individual system components; 2) formalizing this framework through the concept of the Emergent Systemic Risk Horizon (ESRH), which marks the boundary where localized reliability gives way to collective instability. The ESRH introduces a lexicon and empirical indicators for analyzing LLM ecosystems as complex adaptive systems rather than as isolated agents. This reframing establishes the foundation for the subsequent discussion.
