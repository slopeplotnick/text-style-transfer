Statistical tests indicate that the sequence in which articles are presented to the model significantly influences the generated summaries. Specifically, these models exhibit a consistent preference for the first article, thereby shaping both the framing and semantic similarity patterns. Previous research has documented a tendency for these models to prioritize earlier segments of extended inputs. Our findings extend this observation to the multi-document setting, revealing a clear primacy effect in information ranking scenarios. Notably, BERTScore consistently demonstrates this primacy effect across all three positions. This pattern arises because the model predominantly incorporates lexical and argumentative elements from the first article. Consequently, similarity metrics yield higher scores for the initial article. In contrast, metrics such as ROUGE-L, which primarily assess lexical overlap, demonstrate this effect to a lesser extent, while SummaC, which evaluates factual consistency, does not account for framing or positional influence. Therefore, these methods do not exhibit the first-article bias as prominently as BERTScore. In all three analytical approaches, the first article is distinctly prioritized over the second and third articles, although the latter two do not exhibit significant differentiation. This indicates that the primacy effect arises not from a diminishing interest in subsequent articles over time but from the model's inherent bias towards the initial article. The presence of such bias can have tangible implications in practical applications, particularly in systems designed to synthesize information for end-users. For example, the AI-generated summaries in Google Search, utilizing Gemini models, may inadvertently prioritize the initial articles processed, thereby diminishing the perceived significance of subsequent articles. This bias has the potential to skew the representation of viewpoints and influence user comprehension of the subject matter, particularly in scenarios where users place substantial trust in the AI's output.
