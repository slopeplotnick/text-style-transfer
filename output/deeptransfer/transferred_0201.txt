The debate over whether large language models (LLMs) adhere to Bayesian principles persists within the academic community. Several studies have highlighted specific reasoning challenges associated with LLMs, such as their tendency to conform to human preferences, errors arising from scaling, and a propensity to align with majority opinions at the expense of factual accuracy. These challenges adversely affect the efficacy of LLMs in executing various tasks. Despite this, LLMs demonstrate enhanced performance in domains like mathematics [Lu et al., 2023] and coding [Gu et al., 2024]. However, conventional methods of assessing reasoning, which predominantly rely on measuring ground truth accuracy [Sawada et al., 2023, Phan et al., 2025], fail to elucidate the mechanisms underlying this improved performance or to determine whether such reasoning processes can generalize to out-of-distribution scenarios. If these models merely replicate patterns observed during training, their applicability to real-world problems, where contextual adaptability is critical, may be limited. Consequently, there is a pressing need for methodologies that evaluate the reasoning process itself, rather than focusing solely on outcome-based metrics. Moreover, empirical evidence indicates that LLMs may prioritize user satisfaction or alignment with majority opinions over veracity, potentially misleading individuals and reinforcing erroneous beliefs or biases. Human cognition is already predisposed to seek information that corroborates pre-existing beliefs, a phenomenon that contributes to polarization and the dissemination of misinformation. Given that LLMs are trained on datasets encompassing human beliefs, the reciprocal interaction between humans and LLMs could exacerbate the entrenchment of false beliefs on a broader scale.
