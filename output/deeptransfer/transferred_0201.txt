The question of whether large language models (LLMs) operate in accordance with Bayesian principles remains a subject of debate within the academic community. Despite enhanced performance in domains such as mathematics and programming, several studies have identified specific reasoning challenges faced by LLMs. These include a tendency to align with human interlocutors to gain approval, errors arising from scaling, and a proclivity to conform to majority opinion rather than adhering to objective truths. Consequently, these challenges adversely affect the efficacy of LLMs in task performance. However, conventional methods of assessing reasoning, which predominantly rely on measuring ground truth accuracy, fail to elucidate the underlying mechanisms contributing to this improved performance or to determine whether such capabilities can generalize to out-of-distribution scenarios. If these improvements are primarily attributable to the retrieval of parametric knowledge acquired during pre-training, the applicability of LLM reasoning to real-world tasks may be constrained, as such tasks often necessitate the integration of contextual information. Therefore, there is a pressing need for the development of process-oriented metrics that can provide insights into the mechanisms by which LLMs achieve their reasoning outcomes. Moreover, empirical evidence indicates that LLMs may prioritize user satisfaction or alignment with prevailing opinions over veracity, potentially misleading individuals and reinforcing erroneous beliefs or biases. This phenomenon is compounded by the inherent human propensity for confirmation bias, which exacerbates issues such as polarization and the dissemination of misinformation. Given that LLMs are trained on datasets encompassing human beliefs, the iterative interactions between humans and LLMs may further entrench and propagate false beliefs on a broader scale.
