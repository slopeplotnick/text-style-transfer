Probabilistic programming languages. Probabilistic programming languages (PPL) are specialized programming languages that include first-class constructs for probabilistic elements, such as probability distributions, their samplers, and mechanisms for probabilistic estimation and assertions. Numerous prominent examples of PPL exist, spanning both imperative [Bingham et al. 2018; McCallum et al. 2009; Sampson et al. 2014] and functional paradigms [Dash et al. 2023; Goodman et al. 2008; Scontras et al. 2021]. However, a significant limitation of existing probabilistic programming languages is their inability to seamlessly adapt to text-rich graphs, which are critical data structures for defining specifications in language model systems (LMS). This limitation necessitated the development of LUMOS. These languages are typically generative, enabling the creation of entities based on user-defined probability distributions. For instance, [Fremont et al. 2019] describes a PPL designed to generate random images or scenes for autonomous driving scenarios using user-defined scene graphs [Chang et al. 2023]. To construct real-world specifications, however, these systems rely on a fixed underlying graph, from which subgraphs are sampled to define scenarios. DSLs for LLMs. Domain-Specific Languages (DSLs) for Large Language Models (LLMs) [Beurer-Kellner et al. 2023; Khattab et al. 2023; Okuda and Amarasinghe 2023; Sharma et al. 2024] are query languages that lack probabilistic constructs but provide interfaces for writing, optimizing, and securing prompts to achieve desired responses from target LMS. These languages complement LUMOS, which programmatically specifies distributions of prompts to assess LMS behaviors. LMS alignment. Prior research has exposed LMS vulnerabilities by benchmarking their performance [Liang et al. 2023; Mazeika et al. 2024; Xie et al. 2025] or conducting adversarial attacks [Perez et al. 2022; Purpura et al. 2025; Vega et al. 2023; Xu and Parhi 2025]. More recently, probabilistic certifiers have been introduced to formally guarantee specific LMS properties [Chaudhary et al. 2024, 2025; Wang et al. 2025a]. Certification offers a more reliable evaluation of LMS properties [Singh and Chawla 2025]. In this work, we extend LMS certification through LUMOS, enabling the specification and formal certification of arbitrary desirable properties.
