While machine learning and generative techniques are popular and ubiquitous, the application of AI in decision-making is less common. Indeed, decision-making requires the irrevocable allocation of resources, which is not typically within the realm of academic research. To date, the only significant and impactful application of decision-making AI in mineral exploration is the characterization of the high-grade portion of the Mingomba deposit in Zambia. In this case, historical drilling data were re-analyzed using advanced analytical methodologies, leading to the formulation of novel hypotheses that identified a higher-grade mineralized zone previously unrecognized. AI, operationalized as a partially observable Markov decision process (POMDP), was employed to systematically optimize the drilling sequence. The primary objective of this approach was to enable the AI to reduce uncertainties associated with geological hypotheses concerning the spatial distribution of high-grade mineralization. This method was shown to be much more efficient than traditional grid-based drilling methodologies, as it prioritizes the falsification of geological hypotheses over merely attempting to identify mineralization. In Part III, the discussion will center on the potential of AI to expedite the discovery and characterization of critical mineral deposits. The discussion will commence with an examination of data analysis and prediction, subsequently transitioning to AI-based decision-making. Initially, the introduction of AI was met, in certain circles, with a degree of apprehension. This reaction stemmed from the perception that AI might supplant or diminish the significance of domain expertise. The prospect of employment displacement is a prevalent concern in contemporary discourse. There was an anticipation that AI would independently generate novel discoveries. However, it is not the case that AI will autonomously designate a location on a map for robotic exploration and resource extraction. Rather, AI is perceived as an instrument to augment the capabilities of domain experts by assuming responsibility for tasks that are either outside their expertise or unnecessary, thereby enabling them to concentrate on the creative aspects of process-oriented thinking within the fields of geology and geochemistry.
