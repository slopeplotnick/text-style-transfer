Despite the growing prominence of this approach, research into solver-verifier interactions remains relatively underexplored. Previous studies have primarily investigated how a single model verifies its own solutions (self-verification) and improves its performance (self-improvement) (Song et al., 2025). However, self-verification is not always reliable: models often exhibit biases toward their inherent reasoning patterns, and their training can amplify these biases. Moreover, the degree to which verification benefits different tasks varies considerably. Additionally, focusing solely on self-verification provides limited insight into the dynamics that arise when the solver and verifier are distinct entities. With the availability of open-source model families featuring base and post-trained pairs, size variants, reproducible inference pipelines, and explicit reasoning traces, it becomes possible to systematically investigate verification across diverse models. Therefore, we expand our analysis to encompass both intra-family and cross-family verification and pose the following key question: Under what conditions does verification yield meaningful improvements, and how do factors such as model family, model size, post-training, solver-verifier similarity, or task type shape the effectiveness of verification in enhancing solver performance? To address this question, we evaluate verifiers across a broad spectrum of tasks, including synthetic tasks designed to test precise logical reasoning or symbolic computation (3-SAT, Sudoku, and matrix multiplication), mathematical reasoning tasks (AIME (Mathematical Association of America, 2025), GSM8K (Cobbe et al., 2021)), commonsense and factual reasoning (CSQA (Talmor et al., 2019), GPQA (Rein et al., 2024)), and extensive domain knowledge tasks spanning STEM and social sciences (MMLU (Hendrycks et al., 2021)). Using 37 models from 7 distinct model families, our findings reveal that self-verification is not universally effective: models often favor solutions aligned with their intrinsic reasoning patterns (Sections 5.1, 5.3), while post-training can intensify this bias (Section 5.4). Furthermore, the extent to which tasks benefit from verification varies significantly (Section 5.5). Based on these insights, we offer actionable recommendations for leveraging verifiers effectively.
