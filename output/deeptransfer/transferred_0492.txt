Because concepts are not explicitly defined in the data features, probing is commonly used to identify neurons, or combinations of neurons, that best capture target concepts. These probes are typically linear, though they may also be non-linear when the concept is not linearly separable. Additionally, probes can be trained in either a supervised or unsupervised manner. The supervised approach requires labeled data for the human-specified concept, whereas the unsupervised approach uncovers concepts that are significant to the model but may not be interpretable to humans. This work extends the modeling of structural equations beyond observation-based estimation under general assumptions. It explores different levels of modeling, from FSCMs, which are designed to be interpretable based on domain-specific knowledge, to PSCMs, which encompass all FSCMs consistent with the data. This highlights the importance of selecting appropriate modeling strategies and demonstrates the utility of partial information, such as counterfactual intervals, when complete FSCMs are unavailable. Galhotra et al. [2021] introduce an XAI method that calculates probabilities of sufficiency and necessity using FSCMs. Their approach, which focuses on tabular data, integrates causal modeling with data features, assuming these features represent high-level concepts while allowing for the inclusion of additional concepts not present in the model input. Although this method is only applicable when features correspond to meaningful concepts, it does not rely on a causal generator. Furthermore, it assumes a preference for explaining the model's output, framing explanations in terms of which subsets of input values are sufficient to change a prediction to a more favorable outcome or which values are necessary to maintain a desirable prediction when the outcome is preferred to remain unchanged.
