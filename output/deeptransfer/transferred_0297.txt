Experiment 2 demonstrated that implementing a single system prompt could enhance performance across all tested languages, indicating a systematic change in model behavior. To better understand how model outputs have evolved, we analyzed the intermediate reasoning processes and assessed how changes in specific reasoning behaviors relate to multilingual performance. Specifically, we sought to determine whether prompt optimization influences the model's language preferences. We began by evaluating the languages used within the reasoning steps generated by the model. As shown in Figure 7, even when using identical random prompts, the model exhibits different language preferences depending on the language of the input question. These variations correlate with the availability of training data for each language. For instance, due to the abundance of Chinese data in model families like Qwen, the model responds to Chinese inquiries in Chinese almost as frequently as it does in English. After prompt optimization, these language preferences become even more pronounced: the model predominantly generates responses in the language of the input question, rather than defaulting to English as observed with the original system prompt. However, for languages with limited training data, such as Hindi, the proportion of English responses remains largely unaffected by the optimization process. Furthermore, we observed that languages other than the task language and English contribute minimally to the model's performance, as their influence diminishes significantly after prompt refinement. Further details regarding language identification are provided in Appendix A.9, with additional data presented in Tables 6 and 7. In Figure 8, we analyze the distribution of reasoning behavior types across various languages. Although the overarching patterns remain largely consistent, certain languages exhibit distinct preferences. For instance, English demonstrates a propensity for Reframing, Chinese for Logical Reasoning, and Spanish for calculation. Conversely, languages with limited training data, such as Hindi, exhibit a greater prevalence of the Other category, indicating restricted exposure to structured reasoning examples during training. Although these trends are consistent, they appear to be less influenced by prompt design and more reflective of language-specific patterns inherent in model pretraining.
