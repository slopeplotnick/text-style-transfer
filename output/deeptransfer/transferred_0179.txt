The purpose of this paper is to provide a forward-looking perspective on the use of artificial intelligence (AI) in critical mineral exploration. This is a personal essay, inherently subjective, as it reflects personal opinions and observations drawn from three decades of professional experience in data science, decision science, and AI applied to Earth resource exploration and development. In 1998, I authored a paper (Caers and Journel, 1998) on the use of neural networks trained on outcrop data to enhance geological realism in reservoir models. As a junior professor aiming to make a significant impact, I presented this work at the Society of Petroleum Engineers Annual Technical Conference and Exhibition in New Orleans, even as Hurricane George loomed over Louisiana. The imposition of geological realism is essential for accurately predicting fluid flow in porous media, which, in turn, leads to better decision-making in the exploration and development of oil and gas fields. In a subsequent publication (Caers and Ma, 2002), I demonstrated that neural networks could be leveraged to learn the relationship between borehole and geophysical data. When applied to areas without boreholes, this approach improved the prediction of critical reservoir properties such as porosity. A key contribution of this research was addressing the challenge of reconciling the differing scales of information, from borehole core data to seismic amplitude signals at substantial subsurface depths. This issue of scale discrepancies will be explored in detail in this paper. While the oil and gas industry routinely incorporates geological uncertainty into its planning processes, particularly before constructing offshore platforms, the mining industry continues to rely heavily on deterministic models. In mineral exploration, deterministic modeling and the inversion of geophysical data remain standard practices, reflecting a notable lag in adopting quantitative risk assessment methodologies that have become commonplace in the oil and gas sector.
