Hate speech recognition in low-resource languages remains a challenging problem due to the lack of sufficient datasets, orthographic diversity, and linguistic variability. Bangla, spoken by over 230 million people in Bangladesh and India (particularly in West Bengal), is significantly underrepresented in computational resources. Despite the growing demand for automated moderation on social media platforms, Bangla still lacks adequate computational support. In this work, we focus on Subtask 1A and Subtask 1B of the BLP 2025 Shared Task on hate speech detection. We reproduce the official baselines, including Majority, Random, and Support Vector Machine, and also explore additional baseline methods such as Logistic Regression, Random Forest, and Decision Tree. Furthermore, we utilize transformer-based models, including DistilBERT, BanglaBERT, m-BERT, and XLM-RoBERTa, for hate speech classification. While all transformer-based models outperformed the baseline methods for the subtasks, DistilBERT was the exception. Among the transformer-based models, BanglaBERT achieved the best performance for both subtasks. Despite its smaller size, BanglaBERT outperformed both m-BERT and XLM-RoBERTa, highlighting the importance of language-specific pre-training. These findings emphasize the potential and necessity of pretrained language models tailored to the low-resource Bangla language. Over the past few decades, the internet has experienced significant growth in popularity, with social media platforms becoming increasingly prevalent worldwide. Improved internet access has enabled people to share their thoughts and information online more freely. The rapid rise in social media usage shows no signs of slowing down. While social media has become a vital medium for public discourse, it has also facilitated the spread of hateful content. The proliferation of toxic and harmful material, such as hate speech, raises critical safety concerns for these platforms. Hate speech is defined as harmful, offensive, or derogatory language targeting individuals or groups (Ishmam and Sharmin, 2019). Consequently, the automatic detection of hate speech has emerged as a crucial task within Natural Language Processing (NLP). Although significant progress has been made in hate speech detection for high-resource languages like English, advancements in low-resource languages such as Bangla remain limited (Subramanian et al., 2023). This limitation is primarily due to the scarcity of high-quality annotated datasets and the complex linguistic characteristics of the language.
