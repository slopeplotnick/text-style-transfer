To address this critical challenge, the research community has developed two distinct paradigms: Fact Verification (FV) and Hallucination Detection (HD). Fact Verification is a well-established, text-centric research area rooted in domains such as information retrieval and fake news detection. Typically, FV assesses the factual accuracy of statements by leveraging external knowledge sources, such as web documents or structured databases. A defining characteristic of FV is its origin-agnostic nature: it evaluates a statement's veracity regardless of whether it is authored by humans or generated by machines. In this paradigm, the text is treated as an isolated, static artifact, and factuality is determined based on the consistency between the claim and retrieved evidence. In contrast, Hallucination Detection is a more recent paradigm that has emerged alongside the proliferation of large language models (LLMs). The fundamental distinction lies in the object of evaluation: HD specifically targets content generated by LLMs. Unlike FV, which evaluates text in isolation, HD introduces an introspective dimension by analyzing the generative process itself. While HD methods can incorporate external knowledge retrieval similar to FV, mainstream research primarily focuses on model-centric signals, such as token-level uncertainty, activation patterns, and consistency across sampled generations. Consequently, HD is inherently model-centric, enabling it to identify hallucinations even in the absence of external corroboration. Despite sharing the ultimate goal of detecting factual inaccuracies, these two research directions have evolved largely independently, as evidenced by their distinct benchmarks, evaluation metrics, and publication venues. This divergence stems from a fundamental incompatibility between their evaluation paradigms: FV is intrinsically text-centric, with benchmarks such as FEVER treating claims as discrete textual units.
