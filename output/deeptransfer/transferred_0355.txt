We conducted a series of comprehensive experiments on diverse benchmarks, including machine translation evaluation (WMT23) and reward model assessment (Reward Bench 2). Our findings reveal that our distribution-calibrated approach significantly outperforms strong self-consistency baselines. By carefully analyzing the entire vote distribution, our method transforms noisy individual model judgments into more reliable ratings, achieving performance that matches or even exceeds that of individual human raters when evaluated against a human-consensus gold standard. Our contributions are threefold: (1) We demonstrate that conventional aggregation methods used for inference-time computations by LLM judges are suboptimal, underscoring the need for a more robust aggregation framework. (2) We introduce an Expected Risk Minimization (ERM)-based Bradley-Terry-Davidson aggregation model, calibrated on a small validation set, and show that it consistently outperforms existing aggregation techniques across a range of tasks, including reward benchmarks and machine translation evaluation. (3) Specifically for machine translation, we adopt a consensus-based meta-evaluation approach to generate higher-fidelity ground truth labels in noisy data scenarios, enabling fair comparisons with human raters and uncovering cases where LLM judges achieve or surpass human-level evaluation quality. Recently, large language models (LLMs) have emerged as effective "judges" for evaluating generated text, offering a scalable alternative to traditional quality assessment metrics. This paradigm has shown a strong alignment with human judgments across various domains. Approaches in this space vary: some leverage general-purpose LLMs directly, such as G-Eval and JudgeLM, while others fine-tune specialized models for evaluation tasks, exemplified by Prometheus and Auto-J. However, these LLM-based methods face notable challenges, including sensitivity to prompt design and inherent biases, such as positional bias or a preference for longer outputs. Furthermore, LLM judges exhibit variability in decision-making, with some models being stricter in resolving subtle distinctions or ties. To address these challenges, our study focuses on mitigating inconsistencies and improving the reliability of judgments through a principled aggregation framework.
