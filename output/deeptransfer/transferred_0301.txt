Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models have demonstrated remarkable performance and strong generalization capabilities in robotic manipulation tasks, benefiting from large-scale, multimodal datasets collected via human teleoperation or scripted policies. During pretraining, these models employ methodologies such as flow-matching or diffusion-based objectives to effectively learn multimodal behavioral policies that span diverse manipulation modes, scenes, and tasks. Subsequently, the pretrained VLA model is adapted to downstream tasks through supervised fine-tuning on small-scale, task-specific datasets. Utilizing a pretrained VLA model for novel tasks typically yields superior results compared to initiating training from scratch, as the model possesses extensive pre-existing knowledge regarding visual perception, language comprehension, and spatial recognition. However, despite their generally robust performance, a notable limitation emerges when applied to real-world scenarios. This limitation arises from the persistence of redundant action modes that are irrelevant to the success of the downstream task after fine-tuning. Specifically, during pretraining, VLA models acquire a broad spectrum of action modes from diverse data sources, which poses challenges in constraining their output distribution to the subset of behaviors necessary for a specific downstream task. Consequently, even after supervised fine-tuning, these models often retain extraneous action modes that are irrelevant to task success. Moreover, the fine-tuning datasets, typically sourced from varied human operators or scripted policies, often exhibit inconsistencies in style and quality, with some data modalities being suboptimal for effective task adaptation. The introduction of these additional actions results in a discrepancy between the actual performance of the VLA model and its intended task-specific functionality. This discrepancy becomes evident during the model's deployment.
