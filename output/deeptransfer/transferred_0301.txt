Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models have demonstrated significant efficacy in robotic tasks by leveraging large-scale, multimodal datasets collected through human teleoperation or scripted policies. During pretraining, these models employ methodologies such as flow-matching or diffusion-based objectives to effectively learn a comprehensive understanding of diverse manipulation strategies. Subsequently, the pretrained VLA model is adapted to specific tasks through supervised fine-tuning on smaller, task-specific datasets. Utilizing a pretrained VLA model for novel tasks typically results in superior performance compared to training from scratch, as the model leverages extensive pretrained knowledge, including visual perception, language comprehension, and spatial and object recognition. However, despite their generally robust performance, VLA models exhibit a notable vulnerability during real-world application. This fragility arises from the persistence of redundant action modes that remain inconsequential to the successful execution of the task after fine-tuning. Specifically, during pretraining, VLA models assimilate a wide array of action modes from diverse data sources, complicating the refinement of their output to align with the specific action modes requisite for a particular downstream task. Consequently, even after supervised fine-tuning, these models retain extraneous action modes unrelated to the task at hand. Moreover, the datasets employed for fine-tuning may exhibit stylistic heterogeneity due to their derivation from varied human operators or methodologies, some of which may not be optimal for the intended task. This introduction of additional actions results in a discrepancy between the actual performance of the VLA model and its intended task-specific functionality, which becomes evident during deployment.
