Large language models (LLMs) are primarily trained on next-token prediction, raising the question of whether they inherently develop mechanisms for processing and integrating information akin to human perceptual strategies. Understanding how these models handle uncertainty is crucial for developing robust multimodal systems that can accommodate variable input quality. To explore this, we employ methodologies from psychophysics—a field dedicated to understanding human perceptual processes—to systematically investigate the computational strategies of these models. Treating the models as opaque systems, we analyze their external behavior to infer underlying information processing strategies. By modulating input uncertainty and evaluating evidence of Bayesian inference, we assess whether these models exhibit perceptual behaviors similar to humans, despite lacking explicit training for such tasks. Notably, several classical psychophysical paradigms effectively elicit meaningful patterns of behavior, highlighting intriguing parallels between model outputs and human perceptual phenomena. These tasks provide robust assessments of the models' capabilities in processing diverse input types, particularly in controlled environments that allow precise measurement. Unlike real-world tasks, where noise is often unquantifiable, this framework facilitates the evaluation of specific integration strategies and the identification of vulnerabilities undetected in conventional benchmarks. Our contributions are threefold: Firstly, we have developed a psychophysics framework tailored for LLMs, encompassing four synthetic tasks designed to evaluate magnitude estimation in terms of length, location, distance, and duration. This experimental framework allows precise manipulation of variables such as noise and contextual information to examine variations in model behavior. Additionally, we introduce a novel benchmark, BayesBench, designed to evaluate model performance, efficiency in information integration, and adherence to Bayesian reasoning principles through a newly developed scoring methodology. Furthermore, our analysis reveals that while certain advanced models exhibit behavior consistent with Bayesian reasoning, significant limitations persist, as even highly accurate models fail to employ robust strategies under specific conditions. Psychophysics, the quantitative study of perception, has elucidated systematic relationships between physical stimuli and perceptual judgments, formalized in established principles such as Weber-Fechner's logarithmic scaling and other foundational laws.
