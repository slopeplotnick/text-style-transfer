Significant risks emerge at the macro level when a system begins to operate in ways that diverge from its creators' intentions, instead exhibiting behavior governed by its own internal dynamics. At this point, interventions targeting individual components or connections fail to restore balance, as the system has crossed a critical threshold—the Emergent Systemic Risk Horizon—beyond which straightforward control is no longer viable. In this regime, the system operates autonomously, sustaining its own logic and functioning independently of external inputs. Consequently, oversight shifts from being preventive to reactive, as traditional safety measures lose their effectiveness. Importantly, the risks under discussion do not require the presence of malicious intent. Many systemic failures can arise spontaneously from structural interactions, such as coordination, imitation, or recursive utilization of outputs, even when all participants adhere to prescribed protocols. These non-adversarial failures stem from intrinsic properties of the system—feedback loops, shared contexts, or aligned incentives—rather than deliberate interference. Nonetheless, adversarial agents can exploit these same mechanisms to accelerate instability, provoke maladaptive coordination, or manipulate collective reasoning. By inserting messages, altering directives, or disrupting communication pathways, attackers can amplify or even initiate systemic risks. The Emergent Systemic Risk Horizon framework thus conceptualizes adversarial and non-adversarial disruptions as points along a continuum: one reflects the spontaneous emergence of instability, while the other demonstrates its deliberate induction or exacerbation by external actors. Ultimately, these phenomena underscore that system-level risks cannot be reduced to isolated component failures. As interactions scale, the distinction between cause and effect blurs; actions that optimize individual components can inadvertently drive global instability. Traditional evaluation approaches, which focus on static assessments or isolated outputs, are insufficient in this context. They measure compliance with standards but fail to capture behavior in dynamic, real-world scenarios. Addressing safety within this new paradigm demands a fundamental shift in perspective: rather than evaluating individual components in isolation, we must analyze the system holistically, emphasizing the interplay of communication structures, feedback mechanisms, and adaptive behaviors that emerge when components interact.
