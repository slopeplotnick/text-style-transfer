Systemic risks emerge when the overall system exhibits behaviors that deviate from the intended objectives established by its designers, instead evolving according to its own internal dynamics. At this juncture, interventions aimed at individual components or connections prove insufficient to restore equilibrium, as the system surpasses the threshold of manageable control. Once this stage is reached, the system operates autonomously, independent of external influence. Consequently, the prevention of issues becomes increasingly unfeasible; one can only respond to them, as conventional safety protocols prove inadequate. Importantly, the risks discussed herein do not necessitate intentional malfeasance. Indeed, numerous systemic complications may arise autonomously due to the inherent interactions, mimicry, or recursive utilization within the system, notwithstanding the adherence of all participants to their prescribed roles. Such issues arise from the structural dynamics governing interactions, such as feedback loops, shared contexts, or aligned objectives, rather than from deliberate interference. However, malicious actors can exploit these same mechanisms that naturally lead to system disruptions. By altering communications, manipulating directives, or interfering with connection pathways, such individuals can accelerate destabilization, foster maladaptive collaboration, or influence collective perceptions in detrimental ways. Therefore, this framework examines both naturally occurring and intentionally induced disruptions as points along a continuum: the former illustrates how systemic failures may arise autonomously, while the latter highlights the potential for deliberate exacerbation or initiation of such failures. The analysis underscores that large-scale systemic dysfunction cannot be solely attributed to localized errors or individual components. As interactions within the system intensify, the distinction between causal factors and resultant effects becomes increasingly ambiguous; actions that appear beneficial or rational at the micro-level may inadvertently precipitate significant adverse outcomes at the macro-level. Consequently, conventional evaluation methodologies, which focus on discrete outputs or static benchmarks, are inadequate. They assess rule adherence rather than contextual behavior. To comprehend safety within this novel framework, a paradigm shift in analytical perspective is requisite. Rather than evaluating the functionality of individual components in isolation, it is imperative to examine the interactions among these elements, including their communication mechanisms, feedback processes, and adaptive behaviors in response to the complexity introduced by the integration of multiple interconnected parts.
