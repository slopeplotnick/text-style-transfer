In this study, several classical machine learning classifiers were employed to analyze EEG data, with a focus on classifying motor imagery tasks. The classification process began with a combination of preprocessing and feature extraction techniques to identify salient features, followed by the application of various classification algorithms. Logistic regression with elastic-net regularization, which combines L1 and L2 penalties, was one of the methods applied. This regularization approach is particularly effective for handling sparse data and mitigating overfitting by balancing the two penalties. To optimize the model, the saga solver was used due to its suitability for large datasets, with the intercept scaling parameter set to 1000 to accommodate features of varying magnitudes. Additionally, a random state of 42 was applied to ensure reproducibility. Furthermore, a random forest classifier was implemented, leveraging an ensemble of decision trees to enhance predictive accuracy and reduce overfitting. This algorithm is particularly effective at managing complex feature interactions and is less prone to overfitting compared to individual decision trees. Support vector machines (SVMs) were also employed, with both linear and radial basis function (RBF) kernels. Specifically, the linear SVM was applied to datasets that were linearly separable, such as those derived from common spatial pattern (CSP) features, while the RBF kernel SVM was utilized for datasets requiring transformation into higher-dimensional space to capture non-linear relationships. In addition, Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) was employed as a classification method. LDA is well-suited for linearly separable datasets as it optimizes the ratio of between-class variance to within-class variance, thereby improving class separability. Beyond these classical methods, a range of multilayer perceptrons (MLPs) with varying architectures was explored for EEG data classification. These configurations included simpler models with a single hidden layer as well as more complex architectures with three hidden layers, such as (10, 30, 10) or (20,) neurons. The MLPs utilized ReLU and logistic activation functions and were trained using either stochastic gradient descent (SGD) with constant learning rates or Adam optimizers, which adaptively adjust learning rates. To prevent overfitting, L2 regularization was incorporated through the alpha parameter, with different alpha values systematically tested to determine the optimal level of regularization. Notably, some MLP configurations maintained a constant learning rate to ensure consistent convergence during training. Both SGD and Adam optimizers were fine-tuned to achieve optimal performance across the various feature extraction methods. Finally, to ensure reproducibility and facilitate systematic evaluation, each unique MLP architecture was assigned an index (MLP_1 to MLP_17) and consistently applied across all experimental configurations.
