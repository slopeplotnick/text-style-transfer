In 2024, instructional content is segmented into coherent functional and semantic sections, then summarized in multiple languages and made available for interactive question-answering tasks. To support these processes, the transcribed multimodal speech translation (ST) output is used as contextual input. While modern large language models (LLMs) can process extensive contexts, their limited context windows necessitate a structured approach. Therefore, lectures are first translated into multiple languages and then divided into thematic chapters. This segmentation not only prevents context overflow but also ensures that summaries remain conceptually clear, as each chapter focuses on a single topic and avoids the topic drift often seen in global summaries. For each chapter, we generate several compressed representations, including transcripts compressed at ratios of 50 percent, 70 percent, and 90 percent, as well as summaries whose lengths are proportionate to the original section. Initially, summaries are created in English to leverage the superior performance of LLMs with English text and are subsequently translated into the target languages. A similar approach is applied to the question-answering task: the English transcript, organized by chapters, is used with Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) to query an LLM, and the resulting answers are translated into the target languages. Another key challenge in making lectures accessible is translating slides into multiple languages, as slides often contain both editable text and text embedded within images. For editable text, a Python-based PowerPoint parser is employed to extract text blocks, which are then translated using standard unimodal machine translation techniques, as multimodal models are computationally expensive. However, text embedded within images poses additional challenges, as it cannot be directly extracted, often lacks surrounding linguistic context, and relies on visual elements for interpretation, necessitating multimodal translation. After translation, it is crucial to reintegrate the text into the original image to preserve its layout and visual meaning. To address this, we propose an image-translation pipeline that detects, recognizes, translates, and re-renders text within slide images.
