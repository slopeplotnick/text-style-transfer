In our investigation, we examined a category of attack wherein a backdoor may be probabilistically activated during user interactions with a tool, thereby establishing the vulnerability from the outset. However, a more pernicious variant of this attack exists, wherein the tool initially appears innocuous. In this scenario, attackers abstain from executing overtly malicious actions immediately, allowing the tool to operate in its standard manner. Once the tool achieves widespread adoption, the adversary can remotely initiate the hijacking. This deferred activation strategy, akin to supply chain attacks, significantly enhances the potential impact and complicates attribution. Moreover, the attack's stealthiness can be augmented through selective targeting. For instance, adversaries may scrutinize user profiles and selectively target individuals who frequently utilize the tool, particularly those who successfully navigate security evaluations. This strategy renders the attack more elusive during the testing phase. In this paper, we delineate a category of attacks termed implicit toxicity, which exploits tools that possess legitimate permissions, yet these permissions are subverted to inflict harm. To demonstrate this, we developed a mechanism termed LeechHijack, which clandestinely commandeers a system's computational resources through standard tool utilization. LeechHijack is compatible with any MCP tool provider and infiltrates the system without necessitating elevated privileges, thereby reallocating the system's resources for unauthorized purposes. Our findings highlight a critical vulnerability within the MCP tool-sharing paradigm, where malicious tools can compromise systems without elevated access privileges. We conducted comprehensive experiments across various agent frameworks, substantiating the efficacy of LeechHijack and demonstrating its capacity to circumvent existing defensive measures. Furthermore, we explored potential mitigation strategies and established a foundational framework for enhancing future defense mechanisms. The findings of this study underscore a critical security vulnerability inherent in systems integrating large language models (LLMs), emphasizing the necessity of rigorous scrutiny when incorporating third-party tools. All experimental procedures adhered strictly to established ethical and security protocols. The Leech-Hijack attack presented herein serves solely as a controlled demonstration of the identified systemic issue, with no intent to facilitate or endorse malicious activities. All experimental procedures were conducted exclusively within controlled and secure environments specifically designed for this purpose. Interaction with real-world systems, platforms, or services was deliberately avoided, and no connections to external servers were established to mitigate the risk of unintentional data exfiltration.
