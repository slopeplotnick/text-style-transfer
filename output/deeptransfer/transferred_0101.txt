Understanding high-resolution images remains a significant challenge for large language models (LLMs). A recent investigation addresses this issue by segmenting the image into smaller sections and evaluating the semantic similarity of each section to a given query using a specialized model. The most pertinent sections are then selected to identify the primary object while disregarding extraneous elements. However, segmenting the image may disrupt the alignment between the segments and the query. Empirical evaluations indicate that segments of varying sizes yield optimal results at different levels of detail. Therefore, we developed a method to interpret detailed images without necessitating additional training. To tackle the issue of fragmented object representation, we employ a technique that integrates similarity maps across varying levels of detail to enhance information retrieval and maintain object integrity. Furthermore, to facilitate direct object detection within the entire image, we implement a model that identifies object regions using a sliding window approach. Experiments demonstrate the efficacy of our method in comprehending high-resolution images. Large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated proficiency in integrating and comprehending multimodal inputs, facilitating tasks that necessitate the synthesis of visual and linguistic information. By leveraging visual cues, these models decode intricate images, bridging raw data with semantic understanding. However, the prevalent approach among these models is to utilize a fixed resolution for image processing, which, while simplifying computational demands, introduces significant limitations. Specifically, reducing the resolution of high-detail images can distort shapes and introduce blurring, thereby compromising the fidelity of intricate details. Recent studies indicate that existing methodologies are inadequate for tasks requiring detailed image analysis, as demonstrated by their suboptimal performance on assessments designed to evaluate the comprehension of high-resolution images.
