Understanding high-resolution images poses a significant challenge for large language models (LLMs). A recent study addresses this issue by segmenting the image into smaller sections and computing the semantic similarity between each segment and a query using a specialized model. The most relevant segments are prioritized to identify the primary object while disregarding extraneous elements. However, this segmentation-based approach can fragment objects across multiple segments, thereby disrupting the alignment between the segments and the query. Empirical evaluations reveal that segments of different sizes are better suited for capturing varying levels of detail. Building on this observation, we propose a training-free framework for high-resolution image understanding that leverages multi-resolution semantic fusion to address the fragmentation issue. This method integrates semantic similarity maps derived from different resolutions, producing more accurate semantic information and preserving the integrity of target objects. Furthermore, to enable direct localization of objects at a global scale, we employ an open-vocabulary object detection model that identifies object regions using a sliding-window approach. Experiments on high-resolution image understanding benchmarks demonstrate the effectiveness of our method. Large language models (LLMs) have shown remarkable progress in integrating and interpreting visual and textual information, enabling robust multimodal understanding. By leveraging visual inputs, these models bridge pixel-level data with high-level semantic meaning. However, the reliance on fixed-resolution visual inputs, while computationally efficient, introduces significant limitations. Specifically, resizing high-resolution images to a fixed low resolution often distorts structural features and obscures fine-grained details, compromising the fidelity of visual information. Empirical evidence highlights that existing methods struggle with high-resolution image comprehension tasks, as reflected in their suboptimal performance on dedicated benchmarks.
