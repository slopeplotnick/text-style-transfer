Researchers are striving to understand the training and inference dynamics of diffusion models, particularly focusing on the balance between memorization (i.e., the tendency to replicate training samples) and generalization (i.e., the ability to generate novel samples). Earlier studies have explored this phenomenon through various approaches, such as deriving quantitative metrics to characterize model behavior and analyzing the factors that drive memorization or generalization. Notably, most of these works primarily (1) investigate model behavior when sampling from an initial state and (2) validate their findings on small-scale, low-resolution datasets like FFHQ and CIFAR-10. While these insights are valuable in low-data regimes, the likelihood of direct memorization diminishes significantly as datasets scale to ImageNet-level or beyond. For instance, Song et al. [35] observed that, in large-scale datasets, resuming sampling from earlier timesteps facilitates the generation of novel samples, whereas later timesteps tend to replicate training data. This observation raises a critical question: what principles govern the interplay between memorization and generalization in models trained on extensive datasets? To address this, we trace the origins of model behavior back to its training objective. Specifically, we revisit flow matching (FM) and its gradient-equivalent counterpart, conditional flow matching (CFM), which are often considered challenging due to the absence of ground-truth velocity fields. However, we demonstrate that under a Gaussian prior and a finite training dataset, the marginal velocity field of rectified flow admits a closed-form expression (Sec. 2). This enables us to compute the oracle velocity, as defined by the FM objective, at any point in the sample space. Through the lens of the oracle velocity field, the training process of flow-based diffusion models reveals two distinct stages (Sec. 3). In the early stage, termed the navigation stage, the oracle velocity integrates information from multiple data points to guide the model toward a mixture of relevant data modes. In the later refinement stage, the velocity field becomes dominated by a single data point. Furthermore, we identify data dimensionality and sample size as the two critical factors determining the timing of this stage transition.
