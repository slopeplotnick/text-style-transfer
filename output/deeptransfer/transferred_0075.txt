Sensory language processing, or language comprehension, follows a sequential process in which speech is perceived, phonemes are recognized, words are formed from these phonemes, and the meanings of the words are integrated according to grammatical rules to derive the overall meaning of a sentence. In this discussion, we focus specifically on language comprehension after word recognition, a high-level cognitive processing step unique to humans, to compare the mechanisms in the human brain with those employed by advanced language AI systems. Evidence suggests that neocortical language processing functions through hierarchical predictive coding, where lower layers handle basic tasks such as word prediction, and higher layers process abstract and complex linguistic content. Moreover, the neocortex builds a world model of language through prediction-error learning, enabling it to refine its comprehension and predictive capabilities iteratively. This predictive framework is not only fundamental to human language processing but also mirrors the computational strategies utilized by AI language models, underscoring a shared principle for modeling and understanding linguistic information. For example, the Google Neural Machine Translation (GNMT) system employs a structured translation mechanism comprising three core components: an encoder, an attention mechanism, and a decoder. The encoder, a stacked-LSTM circuit, sequentially processes the words of a source sentence, integrating them to generate a signal that encapsulates the sentence’s semantic meaning. This eight-layer structure includes a bidirectional layer that ensures context is incorporated even for the first words, with the final outputs combined into a vector representing the sentence’s overall meaning. The attention mechanism, a two-layer neural network, determines the importance of individual words in the source sentence for translation by generating weight coefficients, which are then passed to the decoder. The decoder, also an eight-layer LSTM, predicts the next word in the translation sequence, refining its parameters through supervised learning by comparing its outputs with human-generated translations. In contrast, GPT-2 employs a distinct mechanism for language generation. Unlike GNMT, GPT-2 lacks an encoder-decoder structure and instead operates through an integrated framework that predicts the next word in a sequence based on the context of preceding words. During training, GPT-2 is presented with sentences containing masked words and tasked with predicting the missing elements. After training, this predictive capability is repurposed for sentence generation, where the model iteratively forecasts one word at a time. Its architecture consists of stacked repeating blocks, each comprising attention mechanisms and feedforward neural networks, integrated across multiple layers. The input is a sparse representation of a word sequence, while the output is a probabilistic distribution of next-word candidates. GPT-2’s training paradigm relies on unsupervised learning through next-word prediction, eliminating the need for manually curated examples and enabling the model to refine its linguistic capabilities autonomously.
