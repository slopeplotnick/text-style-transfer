Sensory language processing, or language comprehension, unfolds as a sequential process in which speech is perceived, phonemes are recognized, words are formed from these phonemes, and the meanings of these words are integrated according to grammatical rules to derive the overall meaning of a sentence. This discussion focuses specifically on language comprehension following word recognition, a higher-order cognitive function unique to humans, to draw comparisons between the brain and advanced artificial intelligence systems in language processing. Notably, neocortical language processing operates through hierarchical predictive coding, where lower layers handle simpler tasks such as word prediction, while higher layers engage in the understanding of abstract concepts. Moreover, the neocortex refines its linguistic capabilities through prediction-error learning, enabling it to construct a world model of language that supports both prediction and comprehension by extracting abstract information. This computational principle of prediction-error learning is shared between the neocortex and AI language models, establishing a striking parallel in their mechanisms. For example, Google Neural Machine Translation (GNMT) employs a modular architecture consisting of an encoder, an attention unit, and a decoder. The encoder, structured as a stacked-LSTM circuit, sequentially processes the words of a source sentence to generate a signal encapsulating the sentence's semantic content. With eight layers, including one bidirectional layer, the encoder synthesizes a unified representation of the sentence, ensuring contextual integration even for the earliest words. The attention unit, a two-layer neural network, identifies the most relevant words in the source sentence by calculating weight coefficients and transmits this information to the decoder. The decoder, an eight-layer LSTM, generates probabilities for the next word in the target language, with the entire system trained through supervised learning by comparing generated translations to human-annotated references and refining parameters via backpropagation. In contrast, GPT-2 employs a unified structure without an encoder-decoder framework, relying instead on next-word prediction based on preceding context. During training, GPT-2 is presented with sentences containing masked words and tasked with inferring the missing word. Subsequently, this capability is applied to sentence generation, where the model iteratively predicts each subsequent word. Its architecture consists of repeating blocks, primarily composed of attention mechanisms and feedforward neural networks, stacked 48 times in GPT-2. The model processes input sequences of up to 2048 words in sparse representation and outputs a probabilistic distribution over potential next-word candidates. Unlike GNMT, GPT-2 learns through unsupervised training, relying solely on next-word prediction without curated examples.
