This study examines whether a computational simulation of language acquisition can autonomously identify grammatical structures and lexical categories through interaction with Delilah, a language model that provides examples of adult linguistic usage. Following this acquisition, the daughter model, as the simulation is referred to, can utilize the learned grammatical framework to engage in dialogue with the mother model. Specifically, the daughter model applies the acquired grammatical rules to generate novel sentences, which it then presents to the mother model during their interaction. If the parameters allow, the mother agent can offer evaluative feedback on the grammaticality of the daughter agent’s output, enabling the daughter to refine its linguistic model. Furthermore, the daughter agent employs its acquired knowledge to parse new utterances produced by the mother agent, such as by identifying and categorizing lexical items used by the latter. Notably, prior research in corpus linguistics has demonstrated that lexical items serving grammatical functions tend to occur with high frequency, whereas content words are less frequent and often appear as hapax legomena—words that occur only once or a few times in a text (see Powers, 1998, for an analysis of closed and open class words). This observation aligns with Zipf's law, which posits that the frequency of word usage in a language is inversely proportional to its rank in frequency (cf. Mandelbrot, 1954; Zipf, 1949). Moreover, there is evidence of a correlation between the distribution of words in human languages and the grammatical categories identified by linguists. This study explores whether a similar correlation can be observed in language generated by a machine.
