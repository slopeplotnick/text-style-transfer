This study investigates whether a computational simulation of language acquisition can autonomously discern grammatical structures and lexical categories through interaction with Delilah, the language model providing exemplars of adult linguistic usage. Subsequently, the daughter model can employ this newly acquired grammatical framework to engage in dialogue with another model, referred to as the mother model. On the one hand, the daughter model can utilize the acquired grammatical knowledge to generate novel sentences, which can then be exchanged with the mother model during their interaction. If the parameters permit, the mother model can provide evaluative feedback regarding the adequacy of the grammar employed, allowing the daughter model to enhance its linguistic capabilities. On the other hand, the daughter model is able to apply its acquired knowledge to comprehend novel sentences generated by the mother model, such as discerning the types of lexical items employed by the latter. Interestingly, empirical evidence indicates that lexemes fulfilling grammatical functions tend to appear with higher frequency, whereas those bearing specific semantic content occur less frequently. Many content words are used as hapax legomena, appearing only once or a limited number of times within a given text. This phenomenon aligns with Zipf's law, which posits that the frequency of word usage in a language is inversely proportional to its rank in frequency lists, a concept extensively examined by scholars such as Mandelbrot and Zipf. Moreover, there exists a correlation between the distribution of lexical items in languages and the grammatical categories identified by linguists. This study assesses whether a comparable relationship can be identified in language generated by computational systems.
