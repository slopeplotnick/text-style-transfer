The fine-tuned model Menta demonstrates superior performance in detecting depression and stress compared to suicide risk. Similarly, both fine-tuned and standard language models, including larger variants, tend to perform better on depression and stress detection tasks than on suicide risk classification. For instance, a systematic review by Bauer et al. (2024) highlights that language models often misclassify nuanced expressions of suicidality due to class imbalance and their reliance on contextual or temporal dependencies, which are not adequately captured in single posts. These findings are further supported by Lamichhane (2023), who reported F1-scores of 0.86 for depression detection but only 0.37 for suicidality detection using social media data. This disparity suggests that depression and stress are more readily identifiable due to their distinct linguistic patterns and the availability of more extensive datasets. In contrast, suicidality is less prevalent, more context-dependent, and characterized by subtler linguistic cues, making it more challenging for models to detect reliably. Consequently, language models, including larger variants, may be better suited for general screening tasks such as detecting depression or stress, rather than the nuanced stratification of suicide risk, unless supplemented with additional multimodal or temporal data.  

Despite the promising performance of models like Qwen-3 (4B) in predicting mental health conditions, several limitations constrain the generalizability and interpretability of these findings. Notably, our analysis was limited to textual social media posts, excluding critical multimodal features such as temporal variations in linguistic expression, user metadata, and interaction patterns, which are crucial for a comprehensive understanding of mental health dynamics. Incorporating these elements could enhance the robustness and ecological validity of future research (Yazdavar et al., 2020; Khoo et al., 2024). Furthermore, the scope of our study was restricted to three mental health tasks—depression, stress, and suicidal ideation—derived from publicly available social media datasets. While these benchmarks are widely used in affective computing, they represent only a narrow subset of the broader psychological spectrum. Real-world mental health concerns often encompass more complex and overlapping conditions, such as anxiety, bipolar disorder, and emotional dysregulation, which were not addressed in this study (Cao et al., 2024). Additionally, the reliance on publicly available, anonymized datasets rather than clinical data ensures privacy and reproducibility but limits the ability to draw conclusions about clinical safety, ethical considerations, and the longitudinal reliability of findings in real-world healthcare contexts (Seyed et al., 2024).
