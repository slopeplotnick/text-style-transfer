Understanding real-world scientific laws is crucial for accurately modeling physical outcomes under diverse conditions. However, existing video benchmarks predominantly focus on physical commonsense, offering limited insights into the scientific reasoning capabilities of video models. To address this gap, we introduce VideoScience-Bench, a benchmark designed to evaluate undergraduate-level scientific understanding in video models. Each test prompt encodes a composite scientific scenario that necessitates comprehension and reasoning across multiple scientific concepts to generate the correct phenomenon. The benchmark comprises 200 meticulously curated prompts spanning 14 topics and 103 concepts in physics and chemistry. Moreover, we conduct expert-annotated evaluations of seven state-of-the-art video models in T2V and I2V settings, assessing their performance across four dimensions: Immutability, Correct Dynamism, Spatio-Temporal Continuity, and Phenomenon Congruency. By employing a VLM-as-a-Judge to evaluate video outputs, we observe a strong alignment with human assessments. To the best of our knowledge, VideoScience-Bench is the first benchmark to assess video models not only as generators but also as reasoners, requiring their outputs to reflect scientific understanding consistent with expected physical and chemical phenomena. Our data and evaluation code are publicly available at github.com/hao-ai-lab/VideoScience. The releases of Sora-2 and Veo-3.1 signify a transformative leap in the video-generation landscape, with contemporary models achieving cinematic-level visual fidelity and demonstrating unprecedented world-modeling capabilities. As video models continue to evolve, the criteria for evaluating "good" video generation are shifting beyond traditional distributional quality metrics, such as Fréchet Video Distance (FVD) and Fréchet Video Motion Distance (FVMD), toward assessments that probe adherence to fundamental physical principles and the capacity for commonsense reasoning about the physical world. Notably, recent models have exhibited emergent zero-shot reasoning abilities, enabling them to tackle tasks requiring scientific, mathematical, and spatial reasoning without additional task-specific training. Human reasoners trained in scientific disciplines understand the world through a comprehensive set of scientific principles, allowing them to predict phenomena such as the efficacy of citric acid in removing iron stains or the consequences of powering an LED with or without a resistor. This prompts our central inquiry: beyond visual fidelity and basic physical commonsense, can video models accurately depict scientific phenomena and demonstrate scientific reasoning about the real world? World modeling grounded in scientific principles is essential for advancing discovery, as it enables models to forecast the outcomes of real-world processes. Unlike commonsense-based world modeling, true scientific understanding requires reasoning across multiple interconnected concepts.
