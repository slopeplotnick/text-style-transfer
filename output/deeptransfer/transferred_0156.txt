The transition from deterministic to stochastic inversion necessitates the inclusion of a falsification step to eliminate erroneous modeling assumptions, thereby mitigating the risk of inaccuracies. Geological realism is integrated from the outset, eschewing the post-hoc incorporation typical of deterministic approaches, and the focus remains firmly on geological accuracy, devoid of the ambiguous regularization terms often employed in deterministic inversion. We advocate for the formulation of drilling plans through collaborative efforts with experts, utilizing quantitative metrics for uncertainty quantification. Drilling strategies are formulated with specific objectives, such as maximizing the targeted area or volume, employing quantitative methodologies to optimize decision-making processes. These strategies are not arbitrary; rather, they are grounded in a deliberate and systematic approach to data analysis, model development, goal setting, and decision-making, informed by a longstanding tradition of scientific inquiry. In the subsequent section, this perspective will be further elaborated through the lens of philosophy of science, with particular emphasis on two foundational frameworks: Falsification and Bayesian reasoning. The practice of scientific inquiry necessitates reflection on its methodologies, prompting critical examination of the principles that constitute an effective scientific method. The evolution of scientific inquiry has transitioned from mere observation to the exploration of the interplay between theoretical models and empirical data. Furthermore, it examines the collaborative dynamics within the scientific community, as various forms of social interactions establish the prevailing standards and norms, which may subsequently be deemed erroneous. Practitioners within the field may fail to recognize such epistemic errors, as these have been assimilated into the community's accepted practices. Scientists may exhibit an excessive degree of confidence in their established knowledge (Bond et al., 2007). Thomas Kuhn examined the collective dynamics within scientific communities, introducing the concept of "paradigms" to describe the frameworks underpinning scientific practice. According to Kuhn, paradigms encompass a set of shared beliefs, methodologies, rules, and applications that are collectively endorsed by members of a scientific community, such as the foundational principles of evolutionary theory or the framework of plate tectonics.
