We evaluate a diverse set of recent language models spanning both closed- and open-weight releases (see Appendix A.4 for details). Where possible, we disable extended-thinking or reasoning controls to probe the models' natural, emergent behavior. This was feasible for all models except GPT-5 mini, which only allows adjusting reasoning depth; therefore, we set this to the lowest level. Moreover, we conducted a human baseline study for comparison across all our tasks under a small number of ablations (see Appendix A.5 for details). Human results are included in the left panel of Figure 8. This experiment serves only as a reference point, as our main focus is on comparing language models against each other. Extensive human psychophysics studies, including the magnitude estimation effects examined here, are well-documented in the literature, such as in Jazayeri & Shadlen (2010) and Petzschner & Glasauer (2011). Most models perform better in the text modality than in the image modality, except on the maze distance estimation task. The text input for this task is a long, detailed path description—much more complex than the ASCII prompts used in other tasks (see Fig. 2). While most models perform worse in the text modality for this task, GPT-5 Mini is an exception: it achieves near-perfect text performance due to the residual reasoning capabilities, which we can attenuate but not fully disable, illustrating the task-dependent nature of behavior. Across tasks, the factor evidence for Bayesian behavior is consistently higher in the image modality than in text (see Appendix A.11). Transitioning from unimodal to multimodal inputs does not uniformly improve performance. However, some models are better able to leverage information from the additional modality: Llama-4 Maverick attains its best performance under multimodal conditions across all tasks, and Claude 3.7 Sonnet and GPT-4o improve on two of the three tasks.
