However, current video model evaluations predominantly emphasize basic physical knowledge, limiting assessments to everyday scenarios that necessitate only rudimentary comprehension of physical principles. Therefore, we introduce VideoScience-Bench, a benchmark specifically designed to evaluate the scientific understanding and reasoning capabilities of video models. Each instance encompasses multiple scientific concepts and requires undergraduate-level knowledge, spanning 103 concepts across the domains of physics and chemistry. We assess whether video models can serve as zero-shot scientific reasoners, capable of comprehending complex experimental setups and predicting expected outcomes. The evaluation framework examines performance along five critical dimensions: prompt consistency, prediction of expected phenomena, dynamism, adherence to immutable principles, and overall coherence. Moreover, we introduce VideoScience-Judge, a checklist-based automated evaluation system designed to systematically assess the reasoning capabilities of video models. VideoScience-Judge initially formulates a prompt-specific checklist to guide the evaluation process, subsequently selecting pivotal frames to identify causally significant moments within each video. These elements collectively facilitate the VLM-based scoring mechanism, with the resultant scores being consolidated into a singular weighted metric indicative of the model's reasoning proficiency. Upon validation, it was observed that the automated judgments produced by VideoScience-Judge exhibited a significantly stronger alignment with domain-expert annotations compared to other benchmarks that emphasize basic physical knowledge or visual fidelity. In summary, this study examines the capability of video models to reason over multiple interacting scientific concepts. To this end, we make three primary contributions: First, we introduce VideoScience-Bench, a high-quality benchmark designed to evaluate zero-shot scientific reasoning in video models, encompassing real-world phenomena across 103 physics and chemistry concepts. Second, we developed VideoScience-Judge, an evaluation framework that employs a checklist-based approach, incorporates key-frame selection, and utilizes computer-vision tools to detect physical cues such as motion and object interactions. This system provides quantitative assessments of both the scientific validity and the perceptual quality of the videos. Third, a detailed empirical study was conducted utilizing VideoScience-Bench. Despite the ability of contemporary video models to generate high-quality, photorealistic, and temporally coherent videos, VideoScience-Bench remains a formidable challenge. Many models exhibit deficiencies in their capacity to comprehend and model complex scientific concepts, frequently resulting in violations of fundamental physical laws. Among the models evaluated, Sora-2 and Veo-3 demonstrated superior performance, exhibiting preliminary capabilities in scientific reasoning.
