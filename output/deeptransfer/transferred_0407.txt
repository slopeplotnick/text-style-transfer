Flow-based diffusion models have emerged as a leading framework for training generative models in the domains of image and video synthesis. However, their mechanisms for balancing memorization and generalization remain insufficiently explored. In this work, we revisit the flow matching (FM) objective and analyze its marginal velocity field, which admits a closed-form expression, enabling the exact computation of the oracle FM target. This analysis reveals that flow-based diffusion models inherently adopt a two-stage training objective: an initial stage guided by a mixture of data modes, followed by a later stage dominated by the nearest data sample. These two stages result in distinct learning behaviors: the early stage promotes generalization across data modes to capture global patterns, while the later stage increasingly focuses on memorizing fine-grained details. Building on these insights, we elucidate the effectiveness of practical techniques, such as timestep-shifted schedules, classifier-free guidance intervals, and strategic latent space design choices. Our findings deepen the understanding of diffusion model training dynamics and provide guiding principles for future architectural and algorithmic advancements. Diffusion models have become a powerful class of generative methods, capable of synthesizing high-quality samples across diverse domains, such as images and videos. These models learn complex data distributions by progressively transforming a prior distribution, such as a Gaussian, into the target data distribution through a process parameterized by denoising or transport directions. Various approaches have been proposed to characterize the diffusion process, including probabilistic models, score-based ODEs/SDEs, and flow matching. These frameworks yield distinct training objectives, such as noise prediction, score estimation, or velocity field approximation, and correspond to different parameterizations of the probability flow ODE. Recent advances have predominantly converged on the flow matching formulation, where the model is trained to predict the velocity field under a simplified linear schedule. Due to its simplicity and stable training dynamics, this canonical formulation has become the standard for training state-of-the-art diffusion models.
