We establish baseline performance on the Swivuriso dataset by fine-tuning three speech recognition architectures: Whisper-large-v3-Turbo, Wav2Vec-BERT 2.0, and Massively Multilingual Speech (MMS). Table 6 summarises the Word Error Rate (WER) achieved by each model at early (1,000 steps) and final (10,000 steps) training checkpoints. Continued fine-tuning yields substantial improvements across all models, with Whisper-large-v3-Turbo achieving the largest absolute reduction of 0.22, followed by Wav2Vec-BERT at 0.27 and MMS at 0.15. To further examine the impact of training data volume on fine-tuning, the Whisper-v3-Turbo model was trained for a single epoch using subsets of 50 hours, 150 hours, and 250 hours of training data. These experiments demonstrate that the Swivuriso dataset supports measurable improvements in ASR performance across diverse architectures and languages. Moreover, fine-tuning consistently reduced error rates, and results indicate that the dataset provides stable learning signals even at early training stages. The analysis of training hours further confirms that additional data contributes to performance gains for most languages. Therefore, these findings position Swivuriso as a valuable resource for advancing South African ASR research and for refining multilingual speech modelling methodologies. While significant progress has been made in developing high-quality datasets for Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR), particularly in high-resource languages, the development of resources for African and other low-resource languages has lagged behind. Notably, Abdulmumin et al. conducted a study on the FLORES dataset, which involved a meticulous review and correction of its dev and devtest splits for four African languages: Hausa, Northern Sotho (Sepedi), Xitsonga, and isiZulu. Native speakers were engaged to identify and address inconsistencies, thereby improving the dataset’s linguistic accuracy and reliability for downstream tasks such as machine translation. This work underscores the critical role of native speaker involvement in ensuring linguistic and cultural relevance in low-resource language datasets. Similarly, we collaborated extensively with native speakers to enhance the precision and reliability of our dataset, aligning with the recommendations of Abdulmumin et al. Furthermore, another study informed our approach to sentiment analysis, highlighting the disparity in benchmark datasets between high-resource and low-resource languages. For instance, researchers addressed challenges in sentiment analysis for Hindi by introducing a balanced corpus annotated with sentiment categories—positive, negative, and neutral—focused on socially relevant domains. This study provided valuable insights into addressing similar challenges in low-resource language contexts.
