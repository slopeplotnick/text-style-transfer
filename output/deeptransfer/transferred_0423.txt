Nevertheless, through task-specific supervision and systematic fine-tuning, our Menta model achieves strong performance across both binary and multi-level mental health classification tasks, including stress detection, depression severity assessment, and suicidal ideation and risk evaluation. It effectively manages a diverse range of mental health outcomes within a unified, efficient framework. Notably, our Menta model performs competitively with significantly larger language models, particularly in depression and stress tasks, where it occasionally surpasses these larger models. These findings underscore the practical viability of well-optimized smaller language models (SLMs) for continuous mental health monitoring, offering a cost-effective yet high-performing solution for early detection in digital mental health applications. In this section, we address three key questions regarding the use of SLMs and our fine-tuned Menta model in predicting mental health outcomes, while also outlining current limitations and proposing directions for future research. To what extent can SLMs effectively predict mental health status? Our findings reveal that SLMs exhibit substantial capability in predicting mental health conditions from text-based social media data. Despite their smaller parameter sizes, the well-trained Menta model successfully handles complex classification tasks, highlighting its potential to deliver accurate mental health predictions in low-resource or deployment-constrained settings. While prior research (Jia et al., 2025) has examined SLMs within the 2–7 billion parameter range in the context of mental health, our study specifically investigates models strictly below 7 billion parameters. The results indicate that models with fewer than approximately 3.8 billion parameters (e.g., around 1 billion) exhibit significant performance deficiencies, often producing blank outputs or random responses in zero- or few-shot scenarios. This suggests that scaling language models below a certain parameter threshold leads to a catastrophic decline in task performance rather than a gradual degradation, thereby highlighting the existence of a sharp lower-bound capacity limit.
