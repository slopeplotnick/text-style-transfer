While enhanced taxonomic frameworks recognize that deploying robots with specialized capabilities to execute complex tasks presents unique MRTA challenges in construction environments, these challenges set construction apart from controlled settings where traditional approaches have succeeded. Construction sites are characterized by unstructured layouts, dynamic material flows, weather-related uncertainties, and intricate interdependencies among various trades. Moreover, these environments necessitate allocation systems proficient in managing spatial constraints, temporal dependencies, and safety-critical operations. Furthermore, construction scenarios demand human oversight that cannot be predetermined or easily programmed. These challenges necessitate adaptable coordination methodologies capable of accommodating dynamic conditions and complex environments. Traditional optimization methodologies have endeavored to address these MRTA challenges through diverse algorithmic strategies. Classical optimization techniques constitute the cornerstone of established multi-robot coordination frameworks, providing mathematical rigor and performance assurances via various computational methodologies. Kuhn (1955) laid the theoretical groundwork for optimal assignment problems through the pioneering Hungarian method, illustrating that bipartite matching between tasks and resources could be resolved efficiently using linear programming with polynomial time complexity guarantees. However, the Hungarian method was constrained to basic one-to-one assignments and was inadequate for addressing heterogeneous robot capabilities or task dependencies. In construction environments, robots are equipped with specialized capabilities such as heavy lifting or precision assembly, necessitating workflows that require coordinated task sequences with explicit dependencies. Consequently, these limitations necessitated the development of more advanced optimization methodologies. Atay and Bayazit (2006) pioneered mixed-integer linear programming (MILP) formulations for multi-robot task allocation, incorporating robot capability constraints, task precedence relationships, and communication limitations. Military and aerospace applications further advanced MILP-based coordination through sophisticated formulations addressing heterogeneous capabilities and timing constraints. MILP provided significant advantages in modeling complex multi-robot scenarios with explicit constraint satisfaction. Nevertheless, MILP methodologies are contingent upon centralized optimization frameworks that encounter scalability challenges when tasked with the coordination of extensive robotic teams. Construction projects encompass numerous robots dispersed across expansive, unstructured sites with constrained communication infrastructure, rendering centralized coordination impractical. Therefore, these scalability constraints have consequently prompted the development of distributed coordination strategies.
