In recent years, brain-computer interfaces (BCIs) have garnered significant attention as both invasive and non-invasive tools for enabling "direct" communication between the brain and external devices or digital environments, capturing the interest of the scientific and medical communities as well as mainstream media. BCIs are multidisciplinary systems that empower individuals with severe motor disabilities, such as locked-in syndrome, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, spinal cord injuries, or severe movement or speech disorders following a stroke, to communicate and sometimes even regain mobility despite these debilitating conditions. Consequently, benchmarking a broader spectrum of EEG-derived features has become essential for improving the performance and accuracy of non-invasive BCIs. Feature extraction techniques are diverse and play a critical role in EEG analysis, with approaches ranging from one-dimensional methods, such as time-domain, frequency-domain, and decomposition-domain features, to multi-dimensional techniques, underscoring their importance across numerous applications. Moreover, transforming raw EEG signals into meaningful representations, including time-domain statistics, frequency-domain features like power spectral density, and time-frequency methods such as wavelet transforms, is crucial for accurate signal classification and robust performance. It is also important to note that cross-user generalization depends on diverse and transferable features, as non-stationarity and dataset shifts in EEG-based emotion recognition require transfer learning and domain generalization strategies built upon robust feature representations. A critical and open question remains: Which combinations of feature extraction and classification techniques yield truly robust and generalizable performance, especially given the substantial individual variability? Addressing this requires evaluation protocols that minimize optimistic bias. In practice, this means using participant-independent validation strategies, such as leave-one-subject-out cross-validation, employing performance metrics robust to class imbalances, like balanced accuracy and AUROC, assessing statistical significance via nonparametric or permutation testing, and rigorously guarding against data leakage by ensuring that operations like filter estimation and feature normalization are confined strictly to the training folds and never informed by the test data.
