To ensure statistical comparability across studies, it is crucial that independently gathered samples exhibit dimensional invariance. Therefore, we assessed this by applying Multigroup-CFA, treating our two samples—real-world and simulated—as independent groups while incrementally introducing constraints. This analysis was segmented into distinct components: evaluating configural invariance (H2.1), metric invariance (H2.2), scalar invariance (H2.3), and residual invariance (H2.4). If configural invariance is confirmed, it indicates that the pattern of factor loadings is consistent across groups. Moreover, metric invariance allows for the comparison of variances between latent variables. Scalar invariance, when verified, facilitates the comparison of latent scale means. Finally, residual invariance enables further comparisons of manifest means and analyses with manifest scale scores (Putnick & Bornstein, 2016; Vandenberg & Lance, 2000). Achieving high degrees of measurement invariance between real-world and simulated samples would suggest that the simulated data reliably mirrors the properties of real-world datasets. Consistent with our hypotheses, we anticipated substantial agreement between responses from the real-world dataset and those from the simulated dataset (H3), and that the score distributions across both datasets would not significantly differ (H4). To evaluate the extent to which the simulated data mirrored the characteristics of real-world data, we initially employed the Mann-Whitney U test to assess differences in central tendency. Subsequently, we utilized the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test to examine disparities in the distributional characteristics of the datasets.
