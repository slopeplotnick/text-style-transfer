Recent licensing models, such as the Nwulite Obodo Open Data License (NOODL) and the Esethu License, illustrate this paradigm shift in operational practices. NOODL, for instance, differentiates between users in Africa or developing countries and those from other regions, enabling tailored benefit-sharing and requiring that modifications be disseminated locally. Similarly, the Esethu License reinvests licensing revenue into resource expansion and local employment support, thereby maintaining community agency and ensuring resource sustainability. However, our experiences with Indigenous and low-resource language data have demonstrated that mere openness can exacerbate existing inequities without robust ethical guidelines. Therefore, the contemporary approach to responsible data sharing extends beyond the application of a generic license; it necessitates a focus on consent, privacy, fairness, and mechanisms for sustained community benefit. Our decision to employ the CC BY 4.0 license for Swivuriso was motivated by a commitment to openness, yet it also highlighted the inadequacies of conventional licenses when applied to datasets from African and low-resource languages. Consequently, innovative frameworks such as NOODL and the Esethu License exemplify the transition towards more ethical and equitable governance. In the context of dataset publication, merely declaring an open license is insufficient; authors are now expected to articulate their strategies for ethical, equitable, and community-empowering data stewardship. Swivuriso represents a significant advancement in the development of automatic speech recognition and other speech technologies for South African languages. The dataset encompasses seven linguistically and geographically diverse languages, incorporating both domain-specific and culturally grounded unscripted speech. Thus, it supports a wide array of applications, including health communication, agricultural advisory tools, and multilingual spoken language systems. Moreover, it is meticulously designed to facilitate not only benchmark-driven evaluations, such as enhancements in word error rate and related performance metrics, but also the development of speech systems that are culturally informed and socially responsive.
