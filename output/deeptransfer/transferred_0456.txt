Question Formulation. Following the categorization outlined in Section 2.1, eight domain-specific graduate student experts selected subsets of categories and independently developed 20 prompts each. These prompts were intentionally designed to necessitate reasoning that integrates at least two scientific concepts, thereby ensuring an appropriate level of complexity. To guarantee scientific validity and feasibility for representation in videos of no more than 10 seconds, the design of the prompts was informed by examples of public educational experiments. Among the contributors, two graduate students curated a set of 40 Image-to-Video (I2V) questions, each accompanied by a reference video that accurately depicted the intended experiment as the ground truth. These reference videos were sourced from public datasets and online resources, with a key frame extracted from the initial segment serving as the conditional input image to illustrate the complete experimental setup. Verification and Review. Upon completion of the comprehensive test set, the graduate student cohort convened as a review panel to conduct a systematic evaluation. The panel meticulously examined each expert's set of 20 prompts to ensure specificity, procedural precision, and clarity in the articulation of expected outcomes. As a final validation step, we utilized advanced video generation models, Sora-2 and Wan-2.5-T2V-Preview [36], to synthesize the initial seconds of each prompt, ensuring that the critical experimental components and configurations were accurately represented. Leveraging VLMs as evaluative tools, recent studies have demonstrated that high-performing VLMs, when appropriately prompted and calibrated, are capable of producing judgments closely aligned with human evaluations [10, 23]. In VideoScience-Bench, a VLM-as-a-Judge framework is employed to investigate the disparity in performance between reasoning-oriented VLMs and non-reasoning models, such as GPT-4o. It is observed that directly prompting VLMs, particularly those lacking reasoning capabilities, to evaluate video outputs frequently results in insufficiently justified evaluations and an increased incidence of false-positive ratings (refer to Appendix E). Consequently, this observation necessitated the development of a quantitative auto-evaluation mechanism incorporating a checklist-based grading methodology, wherein each score is substantiated by salient video frames and an evidence table derived from computer vision analysis of the videos.
