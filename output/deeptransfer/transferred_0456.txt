Question Making. According to the categorization outlined in Section 2.1, eight domain-specific graduate student experts selected subsets of categories and developed 20 prompts each. These prompts were crafted to require reasoning involving at least two scientific concepts, thereby ensuring a substantial level of difficulty. To guarantee scientific rigor, the design of the prompts was informed by examples of public educational experiments, ensuring that each target phenomenon could be effectively represented within a video duration of no more than 10 seconds. Among the contributors, two graduate students curated a set of 40 I2V questions, each accompanied by a reference video accurately depicting the target experiment as the ground truth. These reference videos were sourced from publicly available datasets and online repositories, with a key frame extracted from the initial segment of each video serving as the conditional input image to illustrate the complete experimental setup. Panel Review and Quality Check. Upon completing the comprehensive test suite, the graduate students convened as a review panel. The panel meticulously examined each expert's set of 20 prompts to ensure specificity, procedural accuracy, and clarity of the intended outcomes. As a final step in our quality assurance process, we utilized advanced video generation models, specifically Sora-2 and Wan-2.5-T2V-Preview, to synthesize the initial seconds of each prompt. This was done to verify that the principal experimental components and configurations were adequately represented. VLM-as-a-Judge. Recent investigations have demonstrated that robust VLMs can produce judgments aligned with human perspectives when appropriately prompted and calibrated. In VideoScience-Bench, we employ a VLM-as-a-Judge framework to examine the disparity in performance between reasoning-oriented VLMs and non-reasoning models, such as GPT-4o. Our observations indicate that directly prompting VLMs, particularly those lacking reasoning capabilities, to evaluate video productions frequently results in inadequately justified assessments and false-positive ratings. Consequently, this observation prompted the development of a quantitative auto-evaluation system incorporating a checklist-based grading scheme, wherein each rating must be substantiated by salient video frames and an evidence table derived from comprehensive computer-vision analysis of the videos.
