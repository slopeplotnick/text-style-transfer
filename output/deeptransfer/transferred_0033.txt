Our study demonstrates that prompt-engineered LLMs, when integrated with Isolation Forest scores, can surpass traditional JETs and classical ML methods by providing robust anomaly detection alongside natural-language explanations. However, challenges persist, such as false positives, cost implications, and practical deployment. This research underscores the potential of AI-augmented auditing in enhancing both accuracy and interpretability. Importantly, we present the inaugural evidence that LLMs can be effectively applied to tax ledger data, a domain where transparency and trust are of paramount importance. Our findings indicate that integrating LLMs with classical anomaly signals provides an effective approach to achieving a balance between precision, recall, and explainability. Future research will extend these insights to larger and more diverse datasets and will investigate the robustness of this approach against variations in prompts and model updates. Notably, our current evaluation was conducted using a single anonymized tax ledger and a synthetic benchmark. Real-world ledgers exhibit variability across industries, ERP systems, and jurisdictions, and obtaining ground-truth labels remains challenging. Pseudo-labels derived from JETs may incorporate rule-based biases, while synthetic anomalies are determined by simulators. Consequently, it is necessary to establish external validity through the application of larger, multi-source datasets and independent audit labels. The stability and clarity of the model are contingent upon the phrasing employed and the specific model utilized; minor modifications can significantly impact outcomes, and discrepancies may arise among different models. Although our AuditCopilot framework aims to mitigate these variances, it is not impervious to alterations in phrasing, complex inputs, or updates to the model. Furthermore, while auditors may find plain language explanations beneficial, these may not consistently align with the underlying decision rules. The application of LLMs in financial auditing exhibits promising results in experimental settings; however, it is accompanied by potential risks, including errors, biases, prompt-related issues, and alterations in model weights. Therefore, it is imperative to underscore that while our method is intended to facilitate decision-making processes, it necessitates rigorous human oversight, comprehensive controls, and established governance frameworks prior to its deployment in practical scenarios.
