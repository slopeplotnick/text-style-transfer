Advanced reinforcement learning techniques incorporate domain randomization and reward shaping to address simulation-reality gaps. Tobin et al. (2017) demonstrated that domain randomization could enable successful sim-to-real transfer for robotic tasks, while Grzes and Kudenko (2010) developed reward shaping approaches that enhance learning efficiency in multi-agent scenarios. These techniques improve robustness to environmental variability in construction robotics. However, they require extensive computational resources and careful hyperparameter tuning. In dynamic construction environments, where conditions change frequently, the computational overhead and tuning complexity become impractical for real-time coordination. Furthermore, learned policies remain difficult to interpret and validate, which has motivated the exploration of game-theoretic approaches. Game-theoretic methods provide sophisticated mathematical frameworks for modeling strategic interactions among autonomous agents. Martin et al. (2023) developed a multi-robot task allocation framework based on game theory, employing Shapley values to measure individual robot contributions. Their approach demonstrated superior performance while providing formal analysis of equilibrium solutions and stability properties. Nevertheless, game-theoretic approaches still lack interpretability in decision-making processes and assume rational agent behavior, which may not hold in uncertain construction environments. Moreover, they require precise utility function specifications that are difficult to define for complex construction tasks. Despite these advances, all learning-based and AI-driven approaches face critical limitations when applied to construction coordination scenarios requiring explainable decision-making. The black-box nature of deep learning and game-theoretic models prevents a clear understanding of coordination decision processes. Construction environments require human supervisors to understand and validate coordination rationales for safety oversight and project management. These explainability limitations have motivated the exploration of Large Language Model-based coordination approaches capable of providing natural language reasoning and transparent decision-making processes. Large Language Models represent a transformative paradigm for multi-robot coordination through their capabilities in natural language understanding, contextual reasoning, and adaptive decision-making. Unlike optimization methods requiring complete problem specification, LLM-based approaches enable intuitive human-robot interaction and address the explainability limitations of learning-based methods by providing transparent, natural language reasoning.
