In today's rapidly evolving field of Natural Language Processing (NLP), large language models (LLMs) like GPT and BERT have become the main drivers of change. These models can generate text that resembles human language, answer complex questions, and engage in fluent conversation. When a method produces an output that makes sense beforehand, it can be likened to navigating north on a compass. However, while responses from LLMs may be relevant to the input and follow logical reasoning, they often lack truthfulness because they are not based on factual information. This phenomenon, known as hallucination, is a significant barrier to their widespread adoption. To illustrate, consider an experienced storyteller who occasionally embellishes their narrative with fictional elements while trying to convey the truth. Similarly, LLMs can produce content that appears accurate and coherent but is not grounded in the data they were trained on. A simpler analogy would be a GPS system that confidently provides directions but occasionally leads to nonexistent destinations. Technically, hallucinations occur when the output generated by the model deviates from factual information or fails to align with the expected results derived from its training data. These models are prone to generating inaccurate information, fabricating references, or employing erroneous logic. Just like the misguided GPS, LLMs seem to know where they're going but end up in the wrong place. Addressing these challenges is a focal point within the AI research community. Even state-of-the-art models are susceptible to generating hallucinations. Despite their impressive capabilities, these models do not consistently demonstrate reliability, particularly in producing factual information. This limitation is especially critical in high-stakes domains such as healthcare, legal practice, and academic research.
