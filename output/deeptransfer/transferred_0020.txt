Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly being employed as automated judges for evaluating the outputs of other generative systems, a paradigm referred to as "Thinking-LLM-as-a-Judge." This approach offers a scalable and cost-effective alternative to human evaluation, which is often constrained by significant time and financial demands. To address the inherent stochasticity and errors of single-pass judgments, a common strategy involves leveraging substantial computational resources to generate multiple independent reasoning and evaluation samples for each item under review. However, the reliability of the final judgment critically depends on how these multiple outputs are aggregated. Conventional aggregation methods, such as majority voting or heuristics based on confidence scores, often lack statistical robustness and are particularly fragile in the presence of ties. For example, a simple majority vote fails to distinguish between a narrow 5-to-4 decision and a unanimous 9-to-0 consensus, thereby discarding valuable information about the strength of evidence embedded within the vote distribution. This insensitivity to evidential strength undermines the reliability and robustness of evaluations. Therefore, we argue that the aggregation step is not merely a procedural necessity but a pivotal component for effectively utilizing computational resources. To this end, we propose a principled framework called Distribution-Calibrated Aggregation, which transcends basic vote-counting methodologies. Our approach directly analyzes the full counts of positive, negative, and tie votes, preserving the complete signal within the sample distribution. Specifically, we model the three-way preference outcomes using a Bradley-Terry-Davidson formulation, which explicitly accounts for both the preference margin and the global propensity for ties. By calibrating parameters through maximum likelihood estimation on a preliminary test set and employing the Mean Absolute Error (MAE) Bayes action during inference, our method aligns with the evaluation metric while ensuring a well-calibrated probabilistic fit. This alignment avoids mismatches between the decision process and the evaluation objective, ultimately yielding more accurate and robust judgments.
