The anonymity of online platforms often reduces social restraint and amplifies anger or frustration, with group dynamics further intensifying polarization. While computational methods have advanced in detecting and flagging toxic behavior, they remain predominantly reactive, focusing on moderation through content removal or punitive measures rather than addressing the root causes. Consequently, although platforms may temporarily suppress harmful discourse, they fail to engage with the underlying social dynamics, leaving room for hostility to resurface. In this work, we propose an alternative approach centered on mediation rather than moderation. Instead of merely identifying and labeling harmful content, we explore whether large language models (LLMs) can monitor conflict dynamics and contribute to de-escalation. We envision LLMs as potential mediators capable of interpreting multi-turn interactions, evaluating the fairness and relevance of arguments, and generating responses that acknowledge concerns, reduce hostility, and guide discussions toward more constructive outcomes. Achieving this requires reasoning about context, emotional responses, escalation patterns, and conversational norms. To this end, we divide the task into two complementary components: judgment and steering. Judgment involves identifying elements that contribute to perceived injustice, emotional distress, or escalation, while steering focuses on generating conciliatory responses that calm tensions and redirect participants toward addressing the core issues. To evaluate the effectiveness of LLMs as mediators, we construct a large-scale dataset of real-world online disputes from Reddit and develop a multi-stage evaluation framework. This framework incorporates three perspectives: principle-based evaluation, which assesses mediation quality against conversational norms; user simulation, which examines post-intervention behavior to determine whether hostility is reduced; and human comparative assessment, which benchmarks model-generated mediations against human-written ones. Extensive experiments reveal that API-based models consistently outperform open-source systems in both judgment and steering tasks, demonstrating greater coherence and stability. Moreover, these models effectively mitigate toxicity and emotional intensity in simulated conflicts. However, while they excel in maintaining neutrality and structural precision, they exhibit relative weaknesses in readability and human-like engagement.
