Energy efficiency is a critical consideration in various domains, including mobile application development and software systems in data centers. Understanding and reducing the energy consumption of even simple software systems is challenging, particularly due to the complexities introduced by modern runtime environments that employ dynamic optimizations. For instance, statically typed JVM-based programming languages such as Java, Scala, and Kotlin, which use a platform-independent intermediate representation like Java bytecode, exemplify these challenges. The energy consumption of software is contingent upon several factors, including the compiler and its optimizations, the Java Virtual Machine (JVM), the garbage collector, the operating system, and the underlying hardware. Therefore, to comprehensively understand the impact of software design decisions on energy consumption, a software engineer must possess in-depth knowledge of these abstraction layers and the specific execution platform. Previous research has investigated methodologies for workload-based energy profiling, wherein developers execute software with a designated workload within a specialized environment that measures energy consumption and determines the extent to which individual subroutines or lines of code contribute to the overall energy usage. However, implementing this in practical scenarios necessitates specialized hardware, thereby limiting its applicability in routine software engineering. While certain hardware platforms can provide insights into the energy consumption of specific components, such as the CPU, few possess the capability to assess the total energy consumption of the platform. The model constructs a Gaussian distribution for each quadruple that characterizes a source code statement, concurrently organizing each element of the quadruple to elucidate their individual impacts on energy consumption, including the effects of operations, data types, data sizes, and devices. Moreover, this model elucidates the impact of altering data types within an algorithm on energy consumption. It specifically addresses the worst-case energy consumption (WCEC) of individual code statements, employing predictive distributions to ascertain the energy consumption of functions and entire programs. These distributions encapsulate the WCEC, as a singular point estimate fails to encompass the range of potential consumption values arising from stochastic variations in energy, such as those induced by temperature fluctuations or electromagnetic interference. Furthermore, the methodology retains its statistical characteristics, such as presenting the mean, skewness, and mode, and facilitates the calculation of entropy and probability density functions, which single point estimates fail to provide. Employing worst-case energy consumption predictions aids in forecasting energy requirements, thereby ensuring that software adheres to energy constraints, which is crucial for battery-operated devices and cost-effective software deployment in data centers. Consequently, this information enables program analysis and software verification tools to utilize this approach for estimating energy consumption.
