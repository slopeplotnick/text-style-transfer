Energy efficiency is a critical concern across various domains, such as mobile application development and software used in data centers. Understanding and reducing the energy consumption of even simple software systems is a complex task, further complicated by modern language run-time systems that employ dynamic optimizations. For example, statically typed JVM-based programming languages like Java, Scala, or Kotlin rely on a platform-independent intermediate representation, Java bytecode, which is executed on a virtual machine. The energy consumption of such software is influenced by multiple factors, including the compiler and its optimizations, the Java Virtual Machine (JVM), the garbage collector, the operating system, and the underlying hardware. Consequently, to evaluate the energy impact of software design decisions, a software engineer must possess a deep understanding of these abstraction layers as well as the specifics of the intended execution platform. Previous research has explored workload-based energy profiling methodologies, wherein developers execute software with a defined workload in a specialized environment that measures energy consumption and estimates the contribution of individual subroutines or even specific lines of code. However, this approach often requires custom hardware configurations, which limits its practicality for routine software engineering tasks. While some hardware platforms provide performance counters that expose energy usage metrics for specific components, such as the CPU, relatively few can measure the total energy consumption of the entire platform. To address this, a model has been proposed that constructs a Gaussian distribution for each quadruple describing a source code statement, while also categorizing each element of the quadruple to analyze their individual effects on energy consumption, such as the impacts of operations, data types, data sizes, and devices. This approach offers several advantages, including the ability to explain how changes to data types within an algorithm influence energy consumption. The methodology specifically targets the worst-case energy consumption (WCEC) of individual code statements and uses the convolution of predictive distributions to estimate the energy consumption of functions and entire programs. These distributions are essential for representing WCEC, as a single point estimate cannot capture the full range of potential consumption values, which may vary due to stochastic factors such as temperature fluctuations or external interferences. Furthermore, the methodology retains key statistical properties, including the mean, skewness, and mode, while enabling the calculation of entropy, cumulative distributions, and probability density functions—features that point estimates for WCEC do not preserve. By leveraging WCEC predictions, software systems can ensure compliance with energy specifications, which is particularly crucial for battery-powered devices and for reducing operational costs in data centers. This information provides a foundation for program analysis and software verification tools to effectively estimate energy consumption.
