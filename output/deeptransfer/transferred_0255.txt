Compared to traditional algorithmic models, language models offer not only strong predictive capabilities but also human-readable interpretations and the flexibility to support multiple downstream tasks within a unified framework. Small Language Models and Mental Health. To address the application bottlenecks associated with large-scale models, Small Language Models (SLMs) have emerged as a pragmatic alternative to Large Language Models (LLMs) (Van Nguyen et al., 2024). SLMs are optimized for efficiency through techniques such as quantization (Frantar et al., 2022), knowledge distillation (Kang et al., 2023), and lightweight architectures (Wang et al., 2025a). Designed to deliver core Natural Language Processing capabilities such as text classification (Pecher et al., 2024), summarization (Wang et al., 2025b), and question-answering (Lee et al., 2024), SLMs are particularly well-suited for resource-constrained settings, including mobile devices, embedded systems, and edge computing. Offering a compelling alternative to LLMs, SLMs significantly reduce computational overhead while maintaining robust performance in many specialized tasks. Moreover, recent studies indicate that in classification settings with limited data, well-tuned smaller models can match or even outperform larger models in focused and domain-specific tasks, such as text classification (Lepagnol et al., 2024; Luo et al., 2023) and healthcare applications (Gondara et al., 2025). These findings underscore the potential of SLMs as an efficient, scalable, and domain-optimized solution, particularly for mental health detection from social media. However, prior research has predominantly focused on single-task or single-domain applications, often neglecting multi-domain settings that integrate multiple mental health conditions, such as depression, stress, and suicidality. Unlike earlier LLM-based approaches (Sarkar et al., 2022; Xu et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2024b; Kim et al., 2024; Shi et al., 2025), which range from 7B to 70B parameters and are often impractical for deployment in resource-constrained or privacy-sensitive environments, lightweight SLMs emphasize deployability by offering lower inference latency, reduced memory requirements, and cost efficiency. Comparative evaluations of SLMs against LLM baselines across various mental health tasks using social media datasets have demonstrated that few-shot prompting significantly enhances the performance of SLMs (Jia et al., 2025). For instance, Kim et al. developed an SLM called mhGPT, which outperforms larger models such as MentaLLaMA and Gemma in mental health tasks, despite having far fewer parameters and utilizing less data (Kim et al., 2024). Furthermore, another study suggests that SLMs hold promise for integration into clinical workflows, enabling structured and clinically meaningful tasks beyond generic detection (Aich et al., 2024). Nonetheless, these studies often lack systematic task balancing and are rarely optimized with parameter-efficient fine-tuning techniques, such as LoRA, resulting in either minimal prompting or heavy full fine-tuning approaches.
