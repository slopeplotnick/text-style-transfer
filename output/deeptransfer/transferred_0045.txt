Large language models (LLMs) demonstrate proficiency in explicit reasoning; however, their mechanisms for processing information in the absence of explicit instructions remain insufficiently understood. Decades of psychophysics research have established that humans inherently integrate noisy signals and employ near-optimal strategies to interpret perceptual information. This study seeks to investigate whether LLMs exhibit analogous behavior, specifically their ability to perform optimal multimodal integration without prior training or explicit guidance. Adopting methodologies derived from psychology, we analyze the cognitive processes of these models by examining their behavior. We introduce a benchmark, BayesBench, comprising four magnitude estimation tasks—pertaining to dimensions such as size and time—utilizing both textual and visual stimuli, inspired by classical psychological studies. This benchmark facilitates a comparative analysis of nine language models against human judgments to establish a calibration standard. By systematically varying noise levels, contextual information, and instructional prompts, we assess the models' performance, behavioral patterns, and their efficiency in multimodal cue integration. Beyond evaluating accuracy and efficiency, we propose a Bayesian Consistency Score that assesses whether models exhibit Bayes-consistent behavioral adaptations even when performance metrics have reached a plateau. Our findings indicate that although certain models demonstrate adaptation in a Bayes-consistent manner, accuracy alone does not equate to robustness. For instance, while GPT-5 Mini achieves optimal text accuracy, it exhibits inefficiencies in processing visual information. This reveals a discrepancy between the capabilities of models and the methodologies they employ, suggesting that assessments focusing solely on accuracy may overlook their management of uncertainty. These findings elucidate the models' strategies for handling uncertainty and the relationship between accuracy and intelligent decision-making. We introduce our evaluation framework and scoring system as instruments to facilitate the design of more sophisticated systems that integrate diverse informational inputs. The estimation of magnitudes, such as size, duration, or distance, constitutes a fundamental cognitive and computational ability in both biological and artificial systems. Humans achieve this through the integration of noisy sensory inputs, assigning greater weight to more reliable cues and leveraging prior expectations to minimize errors. This process emerges spontaneously across diverse cultural and developmental contexts, indicating that it represents an intrinsic mechanism for managing uncertainty in information processing.
