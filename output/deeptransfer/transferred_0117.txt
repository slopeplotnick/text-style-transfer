Multi-agent autonomy emerges through the integration of large language models with orchestration layers, shared memories, and external tools. Frameworks such as AutoGen and CAMEL establish role-based dialogues—comprising planners, critics, and executors—within a unified environment. Recent advancements, exemplified by systems like SWE-agent and Voyager, extend this paradigm by implementing continuous learning loops wherein agents generate tasks, evaluate outcomes, and adapt strategies accordingly. Consequently, the process is not a static pipeline but rather a dynamic configuration where each model's output reshapes the context for subsequent models, thereby generating feedback that accumulates progressively. Although humans are ostensibly integrated into the system, their authority is delayed, indirect, and circumscribed. Conventional safety evaluations presuppose that behavior is both observable and interpretable; however, within expansive multi-agent frameworks, these assumptions are rendered inadequate. Communication frequently lacks structure, manifesting as free-form natural language exchanges, JSON payloads, or API calls devoid of semantic clarity. When meaning becomes endogenous to the network, traceability diminishes not due to the absence of data but owing to the instability of interpretation. Therefore, technical methodologies endeavor to address this deficiency in visibility. Multi-agent sandboxes now document interaction graphs and compute metrics such as divergence, semantic coherence, influence centrality, and consensus rate to analyze system-level behavior. Similarly, the Inspect framework developed by the UK AI Security Institute incorporates provenance metadata into each message, enabling the post hoc reconstruction of causal chains. These advancements reflect a paradigm shift from output-focused validation to the examination of information propagation within the network. As systems scale, the conceptualization of autonomy undergoes a fundamental shift. Within supervised paradigms, autonomy traditionally referred to the capacity for independent action in the absence of direct human intervention. In contrast, within multi-agent systems, autonomy is redefined as a relational construct, wherein agents engage in collaborative coordination, strategic competition, and the inference of one another's intentions. Even when agents are initialized with identical objectives, minor divergences in reward structures or contextual parameters can precipitate polarization, collusion, or unrestrained escalation. Thus, autonomy ceases to be an intrinsic attribute of individual components and instead emerges as a systemic property of the network as a whole.
