The Self-Taught Multimodal Reasoner (STL) leverages the model's existing perceptual and reasoning abilities to iteratively enhance its performance by generating and learning from its own rationales. A central question in this process is: What should the structure of these rationales be? Answering visual questions effectively requires strong perceptual and reasoning skills. To strengthen both, STL introduces a structured rationale format grounded in the principle of "see before thinking." Specifically, the model is guided to (1) first describe the visual elements in the image, (2) then reason about them in context, and (3) finally produce an answer. This structured approach mirrors natural human cognitive processes and helps the model better organize its reasoning. However, relying solely on rationales derived from correct answers provides limited supervision, as the model is exposed only to successful reasoning paths. This limitation hinders performance on complex tasks where distinguishing correct from incorrect reasoning is crucial. To address this, STL incorporates negative rationales—explanations of why certain answers are incorrect—alongside positive ones. This approach parallels human learning, where understanding deepens by analyzing both successes and mistakes. Negative rationales reveal flawed reasoning patterns and highlight contrasts between correct and incorrect answers, enabling the model to avoid pitfalls such as hallucinations and unsupported inferences. We operationalize this concept in STL, a scalable self-training framework where a vision-language model learns to generate both positive and negative rationales and iteratively improves through retraining on these structured annotations. Empirical evaluations across domains such as commonsense reasoning, scientific inquiry, and linguistic tasks demonstrate that STL consistently outperforms models trained solely on final answers and achieves performance comparable to models trained with human-annotated rationales (Table 4), albeit with some variability across datasets. These findings underscore STL's potential as a practical and scalable alternative to annotation-heavy approaches for enhancing reasoning capabilities.
