Our methodology uses a hierarchical model, which integrates multiple statistical distributions to separately examine different aspects of the underlying statistical process, thereby allowing us to express statistical independence assumptions. For instance, while various CPUs with identical specifications and differing instructions exhibit variations in energy consumption, these discrepancies are statistically independent. Our methodology establishes a Worst-Case Energy Consumption (WCEC) model to ascertain a device's total energy utilization, as depicted in Figure 1. This model comprises two integral components: (1) the measurement component, which evaluates the energy consumption of the benchmark executed on the target hardware; and (2) the modeling component, which constructs a Bayesian model to represent the energy consumption of the programming language. Several statically typed programming languages are compatible with the Java Virtual Machine (JVM). To facilitate a unified modeling approach for these languages, we assess the energy consumption of bytecode patterns, which involves translating the programming language's source code into its bytecode form by the compiler, as illustrated in Figure 2. A microbenchmark encompasses a singular version of a source code statement representing all potential data types and sizes pertinent to a specific operation, as shown in Figure 3. Each microbenchmark measurement is conducted within a measurement cycle that assesses the current, voltage, and execution time associated with a single microbenchmark. The initiation of each measurement cycle involves configuring the multimeter to ensure its readiness for accurate measurement. Furthermore, to mitigate memory interference, the garbage collector is invoked to deallocate memory during each measurement cycle. Each microbenchmark cycle is executed ten times to account for variability in the observed measurements. Finally, a split plot approach is employed, wherein the execution order of the microbenchmark cycles is randomized to minimize potential biases arising from sequential ordering.
