By integrating related events and tracing their antecedents, structured events form coherent and logically connected chains that consolidate dispersed information flows into a unified narrative. This approach surpasses mere summarization, which often results in fragmented and causally ambiguous representations, even after clustering. Moreover, structured events preserve fine-grained semantic details during abstraction—such as specific technological parameters in a "new tech release"—whereas generalized summaries risk oversimplifying critical insights into ambiguous labels, thereby obscuring features essential for evaluating business outcomes. Furthermore, precise temporal anchoring through granular timestamps enables the accurate analysis of event durations and their subsequent effects. In contrast, unstructured summaries frequently lack clear temporal cues, hindering the ability to establish causal relationships between events and market fluctuations, which ultimately compromises both predictive accuracy and interpretability. To examine the significance of systematic event tracking and incremental informational updates, we compared the complete framework with an ablated version that excluded these components. Case analyses further illustrate the importance of this mechanism: for instance, a modestly received new service can generate strong positive returns if subsequent reports reveal unexpected advancements. Conversely, repeated news of a major shareholder divesting shares—without accompanying novel information—elicits minimal market response, as no new deviation in expectations occurs. Therefore, while isolated events may exert limited influence, the incremental shifts in market expectations over time—captured through systematic event tracking—demonstrate substantial predictive efficacy. Finally, to assess the effectiveness of our historical sequence referencing methodology, we conducted a comparative analysis of four distinct approaches. The results clearly highlight the superior performance of our strategy. For example, relying solely on a company's historical data may yield reasonable outcomes; however, this approach proves inadequate for companies with limited historical records or innovative business models, where identifying high-quality analogous past events becomes particularly challenging.
