Psychometric scales and questionnaires are indispensable tools in quantitative research across diverse fields such as psychology, medicine, social sciences, education, and human-computer interaction. When a novel construct or theoretical framework is introduced and becomes the focus of empirical investigation, the development of corresponding psychometric tools often becomes essential. However, this process is highly resource-intensive, requiring careful planning, significant time, materials, and substantial financial investment (Stefana et al., 2025). This study explores the potential to streamline the scale development process by employing in silico simulations, utilizing psychometric data generated by Large Language Models (LLMs) to prototype scales. In the following sections, we outline the theoretical rationale and hypotheses underpinning this approach, describe four empirical studies designed to test its feasibility, and conclude with a discussion of the findings alongside recommendations for future research. Recruiting representative samples from the target population is often costly and time-consuming, leading researchers to frequently rely on convenience samples, such as university students, which significantly limits the external validity of findings (Hanel & Vione, 2016; Wild et al., 2022). Recently, online platforms such as Prolific.co and Amazon MTurk have emerged as valuable tools for participant recruitment (Buhrmester et al., 2018; Douglas et al., 2023; Huang et al., 2015). These platforms have substantially reduced the time and financial resources required for recruitment and may improve external validity by enabling access to more representative samples, particularly for specific demographics such as those in the UK and US. Nevertheless, ethical concerns have been raised regarding the socioeconomic backgrounds of participants, as some may come from disadvantaged groups (Huang et al., 2015; Huff & Tingley, 2015; Moss et al., 2023). Furthermore, when researchers investigate a novel construct for which no validated scale exists, or when conducting a validation study is impractical, they often resort to developing ad hoc questionnaires informed solely by theoretical considerations. While this approach reduces financial and time expenditures, it compromises the external validity of findings, limiting their applicability to populations beyond the study sample (M. Lee et al., 2020).
