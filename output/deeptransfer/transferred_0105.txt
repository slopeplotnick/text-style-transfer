Psychometric scales and questionnaires are indispensable tools in quantitative research across diverse fields, including psychology, medicine and health sciences, social sciences, education, and human-computer interaction studies. When a novel construct or theoretical framework is introduced and becomes the focus of empirical research, the development of corresponding psychometric tools often becomes essential. However, this process is resource-intensive, requiring careful planning, significant time, substantial funding, and various materials (Stefana et al., 2025). This article explores the potential of streamlining the scale development process by employing in silico simulations to prototype psychometric scales, utilizing simulated data generated by Large Language Models (LLMs). Specifically, we outline the theoretical rationale and hypotheses underpinning this approach, describe four empirical studies designed to evaluate its feasibility, and conclude with a discussion of the findings and recommendations for future research. Recruiting representative samples for psychometric research, however, is often both costly and time-consuming. Consequently, researchers frequently resort to convenience samples, such as university student volunteers, which significantly limits the generalizability of their findings (Hanel & Vione, 2016; Wild et al., 2022). In recent years, online platforms like Prolific.co and Amazon MTurk have emerged as efficient alternatives, enabling faster and more cost-effective participant recruitment (Buhrmester et al., 2018; Douglas et al., 2023; Huang et al., 2015). These platforms also offer the advantage of recruiting samples that are more representative of specific populations, such as individuals from the UK or US. Nevertheless, concerns persist regarding the generalizability of findings obtained through these platforms, as well as ethical considerations, particularly given that some participants may come from socioeconomically disadvantaged backgrounds (Huang et al., 2015; Huff & Tingley, 2015; Moss et al., 2023). Furthermore, when researchers investigate a novel construct for which no validated measurement scale exists, or when conducting a validation study is impractical, they often rely on ad hoc questionnaires developed solely based on theoretical frameworks. While this approach can reduce costs and save time, it frequently compromises the validity and generalizability of the findings, which may not extend beyond the specific study population (M. K. Lee et al., 2020).
