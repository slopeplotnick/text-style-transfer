The consideration of energy conservation is increasingly significant within the realm of mobile applications and software utilized in data centers. One of the challenges in this domain is the identification and reduction of energy consumption by even relatively simplistic software, a task compounded by the dynamic optimization mechanisms inherent in contemporary systems. Programming languages such as Java, Scala, and Kotlin, which operate on virtual machines employing Java bytecode, exemplify this complexity. Here, the software's energy consumption is contingent upon multiple factors, including the compiler, the Java Virtual Machine (JVM), the garbage collector, the operating system, and the underlying hardware.  To thoroughly understand the impact of software design decisions on energy consumption, software engineers must possess substantial expertise regarding these various layers and the specific platform on which the software will execute. Previous research has explored methodologies for profiling energy consumption based on workloads, wherein developers execute the software under a designated workload within a controlled environment that measures energy consumption and assesses the energy utilization of different code segments. Nevertheless, the practical implementation of such profiling necessitates specialized hardware, limiting its widespread application in software engineering. While certain hardware components are capable of monitoring energy consumption for specific elements such as the CPU, comprehensive measurement of energy usage across the entire platform remains scarce.  A Gaussian distribution model is constructed to encapsulate four descriptors for each code segment, categorizing these segments to elucidate their influence on energy consumption. Parameters such as operations, data types, data sizes, and devices are analyzed to understand their impact. This model facilitates an examination of how modifications in data types within an algorithm can affect energy utilization. It emphasizes the worst-case energy consumption of individual code statements and employs predictive analysis to estimate the energy usage of functions and entire programs. This approach accounts for worst-case scenarios by acknowledging the variability inherent in energy consumption resulting from factors like temperature fluctuations or external interference. It preserves statistical characteristics such as average, skewness, and mode, and enables the calculation of entropy and probability functions—analyses unattainable through single estimates.  The utilization of worst-case energy consumption predictions is instrumental in forecasting energy demands, thereby ensuring that software can satisfy energy requirements. This is particularly critical for battery-operated devices and contributes to cost reduction in software applications within data centers. Equipped with this information, tools for program analysis or software verification can leverage this methodology to estimate energy consumption effectively.
