The effective deployment of these models in diverse and dynamic scenarios remains a significant challenge. Despite being trained on extensive and varied datasets, such models frequently underperform when confronted with novel camera angles, changes in lighting conditions, or other unanticipated scene variations. This limitation reduces their utility in real-world environments, which are inherently dynamic and unpredictable. To address this issue, researchers commonly adopt two overarching strategies: data-centric approaches and representation-centric approaches.  The data-centric approach involves augmenting training datasets to include a broader range of visual perspectives, as exemplified by datasets such as Libero-Plus. By exposing models to a wider array of scenarios, this method seeks to improve their generalization capabilities. However, the collection and curation of such diverse real-world datasets are both resource-intensive and costly, rendering this approach difficult to sustain over time. In contrast, the representation-centric approach focuses on enhancing the model’s spatial reasoning capabilities. This is achieved by incorporating mechanisms such as multi-view supervision or architectural innovations specifically designed to improve 3D spatial awareness. While these techniques have demonstrated improvements in handling variations in viewpoint, the models continue to exhibit vulnerabilities when faced with cluttered backgrounds, fluctuations in lighting, and other complex visual conditions. Consequently, these methods often fall short of achieving full adaptability to all possible visual scenarios.  A prevailing assumption in the field is that increasing the diversity of training data or designing more sophisticated 3D-aware architectures is necessary to enhance model robustness. However, relatively few studies have explicitly investigated whether the root cause lies in the representation of spatial information within the models themselves. To address this knowledge gap, we propose an approach that disentangles the model's spatial and physical reasoning components. Specifically, the spatial modeling component, primarily executed by the visual encoder, is responsible for interpreting spatial information such as object locations, orientations, interactions, and occlusions. This spatial representation serves as the foundational input for downstream reasoning processes. The physical modeling component, on the other hand, integrates this spatial information with task-specific instructions and historical action data to inform decision-making and action generation.  Empirical observations suggest that changes in camera angles predominantly affect the spatial layout of a scene rather than the semantics of the task or the execution of task-related actions. This indicates that performance degradation in such situations is primarily attributable to misalignments in the spatial modeling component, while the physical modeling component remains functional. In essence, the model’s ability to reason and plan is preserved, but inaccuracies in spatial representation introduce errors that disrupt its ability to perform actions correctly.
