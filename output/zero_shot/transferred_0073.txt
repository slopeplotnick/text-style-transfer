This paper examines the limitations of current safety mechanisms designed for interactions involving large language models (LLMs), particularly in scenarios where multiple models communicate with each other. Presently, most safety protocols emphasize controlling the behavior of individual models through methods such as prompt engineering, fine-tuning, and moderation to ensure responsible outputs. However, these frameworks largely overlook the dynamics and risks associated with inter-model interactions. Existing approaches assume that a single model engages with a single user under consistent human supervision, yet advancements in research and industry are rapidly progressing toward systems where models interact autonomously, processing one another's outputs in iterative chains. In such configurations, even when each model adheres to its intended operation, the aggregate system may exhibit failure due to emergent phenomena.  This paper argues for a paradigm shift from focusing exclusively on individual model safety to prioritizing systemic, multi-agent safety. To address these challenges, the concept of the *Emergent Systemic Risk Horizon* (ESRH) is introduced, serving as a framework to highlight where risks arise from model interactions rather than isolated instances of model behavior. Specifically, the paper contributes (i) a theoretical framework to analyze risks stemming from inter-model interactions, (ii) an approach to link various levels of system failure, and (iii) a proposal for *Institutional AI*, a governance approach aimed at ensuring robust oversight in systems with multiple interacting agents.  Traditional safety methodologies operate under the assumption that individual models function within a controlled environment, where undesired behaviors can be mitigated through localized interventions such as adjusting prompts, leveraging human feedback, or moderating outputs. While these techniques have proven effective in enhancing reliability and ethical alignment on a model-by-model basis, they are not well-suited for ensuring safety in complex systems composed of interacting agents. As LLMs are increasingly integrated into tools, autonomous agents, and multi-agent systems, the one-model-centric safety paradigm becomes insufficient. Models that are optimized for individual performance can collectively produce unanticipated outcomes due to feedback loops, reciprocal copying, or emergent coordinated behaviors—issues that are not explicitly captured by their individual training objectives. This disconnect highlights the need for a systemic approach to safety that bridges the gap between ensuring the performance of individual models and maintaining reliable control over integrated multi-agent systems.
