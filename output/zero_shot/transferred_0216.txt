To develop an effective relevance model, it is essential to comprehend item characteristics, understand queries, and establish pathways for mapping user interactions. The model must be capable of processing a substantial volume of items and accommodating diverse user requirements, ranging from straightforward to more abstract needs. To achieve these objectives, the model must integrate several critical components: it should leverage external knowledge and apply logical reasoning to disambiguate queries and discern abstract user intentions during path mapping. Additionally, the model must effectively handle both textual and visual data to ensure precise matching and alignment, which is particularly pertinent for items heavily reliant on visual elements. Furthermore, the model is required to assimilate and adhere to complex rules to maintain consistency and reliability in the path-following process.  In the context of training large-scale models for relevance tasks, the process fundamentally mirrors a classification problem. Initial methodologies employed prompts to define the task and responses to illustrate outcomes, subsequently refining models through supervised learning. However, this rudimentary approach proved inadequate when encountering data that diverged from the model's training parameters. Researchers discovered that encouraging the model to engage in problem-solving deliberation prior to generating answers significantly enhanced its performance in difficult scenarios. Given constraints related to computational resources and practical deployment, smaller models are typically preferred, despite their diminished capacity for reasoning and instruction compliance when solely utilizing prompts. Consequently, the prevalent strategy now involves employing larger teacher models, such as GPT-4 or DeepSeek-R1, to generate reasoning datasets, followed by training smaller models through supervised learning to acquire these reasoning capabilities. Some researchers further incorporate a reinforcement learning phase subsequent to this, employing methods such as offline reinforcement learning (Direct Preference Optimization or Knowledge Transfer Optimization) or online reinforcement learning (Group Relative Policy Optimization).
