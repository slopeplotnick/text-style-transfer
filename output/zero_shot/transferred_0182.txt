Large Language Models (LLMs) demonstrate high sensitivity to subtle variations in prompt phrasing and linguistic characteristics, which renders them particularly useful for investigating their performance across multiple languages through modifications in prompts. In this study, we evaluate LLMs using three distinct benchmark tests, each targeting specific domains: (1) MMLU-Pro, which assesses knowledge and reasoning across 14 academic subjects; (2) MATH500, a dataset comprising 500 challenging mathematical problems; and (3) UniMoral, a collection of questions addressing moral judgment and requiring nuanced social reasoning. These evaluations are conducted in five languages—English, Chinese, Spanish, French, and Hindi—selected to represent diverse linguistic families, geographical regions, and resource availability.  A critical consideration in multilingual tasks involves the choice of language for the system prompt. Specifically, the question arises as to whether system prompts should be presented in English (typically the primary training language for most LLMs) or in a language matching the task at hand. To examine this, two experimental configurations are implemented: (1) the English-prompt setup, where English prompts are used regardless of the language of the task, and (2) the same-language setup, where the system prompt is translated into the same language as the task. To deepen our understanding of how different factors influence model behavior, this study investigates the relationships between four performance metrics using regression analysis. This analysis isolates the impact of prompt components and language settings on multilingual performance measures. First, certain components, such as Chain-of-Thought, emotional framing, and scenario-based elements, consistently yield positive effects on model performance. These components are associated with improvements in both accuracy and consistency, and they also contribute to reducing performance disparities across languages. This suggests that these components effectively guide the model toward stable performance across linguistic contexts. A close examination of model outputs reveals that prompts incorporating these elements facilitate more structured and coherent reasoning, likely contributing to the observed performance enhancements.
