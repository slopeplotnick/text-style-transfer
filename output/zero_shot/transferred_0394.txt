Despite its significance, the measurement and analysis of power and energy consumption in the operation of large language models (LLMs) remain inadequately addressed, particularly when considering varying model sizes, hardware, software, and deployment environments (e.g., single versus multiple computational units). While some initiatives aim to tackle this challenge, none provide a comprehensive solution. Notably, the MLPerf Inference benchmark (Reddi et al., 2020) establishes a standard for evaluating performance across various machine learning tasks; however, it falls short in assessing overall power consumption or adapting energy metrics to LLM-specific tasks, such as joules per token or prompt. Similarly, the MLPerf Power benchmark (Tschand et al., 2025) expands upon these measurements but is generally limited by its focus on single-computer setups, the consideration of only smaller models relative to contemporary large-scale LLMs (e.g., the Llama 3 405B-class, which lacks comprehensive investigation), and reliance on sophisticated, costly equipment that poses challenges for widespread application. Furthermore, existing benchmarks often neglect to consider user-modifiable settings, such as batch size, parallel processing, context length, and quantization levels, each of which significantly influences instantaneous power consumption and average energy usage per token.  To address these gaps, we propose TokenPowerBench, a novel and user-friendly benchmark designed to accurately assess the power and energy consumption of LLMs. TokenPowerBench integrates with vendor-provided tools, including power sensors for GPU/CPU/Memory, facilitating energy assessments at the machine level and, where feasible, at the rack or facility level. Our methodology aligns each power measurement with the two primary inference phases (prefill and decode), enabling precise identification of energy expenditure. Through extensive testing, we examine the relationships between energy consumption and variables such as batch size, maximum context length, task distribution, and quantization.
