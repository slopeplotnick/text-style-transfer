In our study, we conducted a series of experiments utilizing various benchmarks, such as the assessment of machine translation (WMT23) and the evaluation of reward models (Reward Bench 2). Our findings indicate that our proposed method, which optimizes the allocation of votes, significantly outperforms standard techniques that depend on self-consistency. By meticulously analyzing the distribution of votes, our approach effectively transforms disparate individual model decisions into more reliable ratings, achieving results comparable to or exceeding those of human evaluators when benchmarked against a gold standard based on human agreement.  Our methodology involved several key steps. Initially, we identified the limitations inherent in conventional methods of aggregating outputs from large language model (LLM) judges during inference, underscoring the critical importance of employing a more robust aggregation technique. Subsequently, we developed a novel approach utilizing Expected Risk Minimization (ERM) in conjunction with a Bradley-Terry-Davidson model, which we validated on a limited set of calibration data. This approach consistently outperformed existing methods across diverse tasks, including reward benchmarks and machine translation.  Specifically for machine translation, we employed a consensus-based evaluation mechanism to establish more accurate ground truth labels amidst noisy data. This enabled a fair comparison with human raters and demonstrated that LLM judges have the potential to surpass human performance in certain instances. Recently, LLMs have been leveraged as evaluative "judges" for generated text, proving remarkably effective and offering a scalable means to assess quality beyond traditional metrics. These models have consistently demonstrated alignment with human judgments across various domains.  Several methodologies have emerged, ranging from the direct application of general-purpose LLMs such as G-Eval or JudgeLM to tailored models designed specifically for evaluation tasks, such as Prometheus or Auto-J. While these approaches exhibit considerable strength, they are not without challenges, including susceptibility to prompt design variations and inherent biases, such as preference for specific candidate orders or longer outputs. Furthermore, LLM judges exhibit variability in their evaluative strictness, particularly concerning minor discrepancies or tied outcomes. Our research endeavors to mitigate this variability and enhance decision consistency through an improved method of result aggregation.
