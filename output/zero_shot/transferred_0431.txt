Machine learning and generative techniques have become increasingly widespread; however, the application of artificial intelligence (AI) for decision-making purposes remains relatively uncommon. Decision-making inherently involves the allocation of resources, a process that is not typically the focus of academic research endeavors. In the field of mineral exploration, a notable application of AI for decision-making has been documented in the re-evaluation of the Mingomba deposit in Zambia, where advanced analytical tools were utilized to reinterpret legacy drilling data. This analysis led to the identification of a previously unrecognized high-grade zone within the deposit. The decision-making process was guided by AI configured as a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP), enabling a systematic, step-by-step approach to drill planning. The primary objective of this methodology was to use AI to address uncertainties surrounding geological interpretations of the mineral deposit, focusing particularly on identifying high-grade zones. This approach was demonstrably more efficient than traditional grid-based drilling, as it prioritized the falsification of geological hypotheses over a purely exploratory search for mineralization.  In the forthcoming section (Part III), I will explore how AI can accelerate the discovery and characterization of significant mineral deposits. This discussion will begin with an examination of data analysis and predictive models, followed by an exploration of AI-driven decision-making. Historically, the introduction of AI in mineral exploration has met with resistance from some experts, who expressed concerns about the potential for AI to undermine the role of human expertise. These apprehensions stemmed from a perception that AI might independently identify ore deposits or render certain expert roles obsolete. However, such fears are misplaced. AI should instead be regarded as a complementary tool designed to support experts by automating repetitive or less complex tasks. This, in turn, allows geologists and geochemists to focus on more creative aspects of their work, such as developing and refining geological models. Consequently, AI enhances, rather than diminishes, the value of human expertise in mineral exploration.
