In this study, we utilize forecasting questions from platforms such as Metaculus and Polymarket to examine the extent to which large language models (LLMs) exhibit belief entrenchment. Our selection of this domain is motivated by two primary considerations: firstly, forecasting questions provide definitive answers upon resolution, thereby serving as a clear benchmark for evaluation; secondly, proficiency in addressing these questions requires effective reasoning skills, as it involves the analysis of evidence and the adaptation of beliefs accordingly. Although forecasting diverges from the mere articulation of factual responses, success in this area necessitates the ability to update beliefs in a balanced and impartial manner, thus offering an appropriate metric for evaluating reasoning in contexts characterized by uncertainty.  Another method employed to assess belief entrenchment is the scientific peer review process. For this, we utilize the ICLR submission dataset available on OpenReview, which encompasses paper summaries, references, reviewer feedback, author responses, and final acceptance decisions. In our methodological framework, the model simulates the role of an area chair, tasked with determining the acceptability of a paper based on the summary and the evaluative arguments presented in reviews and responses. This approach enables us to scrutinize the model's reasoning process, especially when initial impressions (derived from the summary) might conflict with subsequent information (such as critiques and responses).  To evaluate belief entrenchment, we employ two distinct reasoning methodologies: Chain-of-Thought (CoT) and debate. The debate methodology involves two iterations of the same expert model advocating opposing viewpoints on a particular topic, thereby facilitating comprehension of complex issues for individuals with less expertise. Our experimental framework includes tests with models such as GPT-4o, DeepSeek R1, DeepSeek V3, Gemini 2.0 Flash, LLaMA 4 Scout, and LLaMA 4 Maverick. In order to assess the impact of initial beliefs on the reasoning process, we manipulate the models' starting conditions through system prompts. In addition to a baseline configuration devoid of any prompt, we employ two alternative setups: one wherein the prompt aligns with the initial belief and another that encourages a critical thinking approach.
