Online videos have become a prominent medium of communication, enabling the rapid dissemination of harmful and hateful content. The proliferation of such material exacerbates issues of discrimination, fosters social division, and may even incite real-world violence. Conventional approaches to detecting hateful content in videos primarily rely on extracting features from visual, auditory, and textual elements. However, these methods often fail to thoroughly capture the interplay between these modalities, leading to significant challenges in identifying and addressing hateful content effectively.   There are two principal challenges in detecting hateful videos. The first pertains to understanding contextual nuances. Such videos frequently rely on subtle and temporally distributed cues across multiple modalities, such as the alignment of specific images with particular words or the relationship between visual and auditory content. The second challenge involves the integration of multimodal information. While existing methodologies utilize features from video, audio, and textual data, they often struggle to meaningfully integrate these diverse modalities, resulting in limited analytical depth and effectiveness.   Addressing these challenges necessitates advanced approaches, as conventional systems demonstrate limited capacity for nuanced reasoning and multimodal understanding. Emerging techniques seek to enhance multimodal comprehension through the application of models that jointly process visual and linguistic inputs. While these methods incorporate reasoning mechanisms, they often provide simplistic interpretations that fail to capture the hateful intent underlying the content. Furthermore, some contemporary systems generate incorrect or unreliable explanations that inadequately reflect the model’s underlying reasoning processes, which poses significant concerns in the context of sensitive tasks such as hate detection.   Beyond reasoning, recent research underscores the complexity of hateful cues in videos. Such cues may manifest briefly within specific segments or be distributed throughout the video's entirety. These signals often convey intricate meanings that require integration with foundational multimodal features. These limitations highlight a critical gap in current systems, which frequently lack the capability to perform sophisticated reasoning or effectively synthesize diverse streams of information. Consequently, there is a pressing need for more robust methodologies to reliably detect hateful content in video-based media.
