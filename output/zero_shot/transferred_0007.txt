Model innovation continues to progress; however, practical implementation of these models often hinges on critical decisions that are underrepresented in academic literature. Such decisions include the operational definition of virality, the types of data available for collection (e.g., textual or numerical), and how models are evaluated within real-world constraints, such as data accessibility, time limitations, and class imbalance issues. This study prioritizes evaluation methodology when addressing these considerations. Using two recent datasets, we demonstrate that choices related to label design and data type (text versus text augmented with additional signals) can substantially impact performance, rivaling or exceeding the contributions of changes to model architecture. Through concrete examples, we highlight the trade-offs involved in these methodological decisions.  Although this work emphasizes evaluation design over the development of novel models, we show that simpler models can achieve performance comparable to, or exceeding, state-of-the-art multimodal and graph-based systems on established benchmarks, such as the FAKENewsNET dataset. Specifically, we focus on two key tasks: fake news detection and early virality prediction, aiming to identify the data and definitions most critical for ensuring robust model performance under real-world conditions. To this end, we compare text-based features, such as sentence-level transformer embeddings, with numeric features, including user statistics, early engagement metrics, and temporal information.  The evaluation is conducted using two datasets with distinct structures: one comprising standalone news articles (EvONS) and the other consisting of sequential social media posts (FAKENewsNET). We implement two supervised classification tasks across these datasets, resulting in four experimental setups: fake news detection and virality prediction on EvONS and FAKENewsNET, respectively. For the fake news detection task, we classify whether an item (either a news article or a sequence of tweets) is truthful or fabricated. The classification task is performed on EvONS articles and tweet sequences from FAKENewsNET, with performance reported using standard evaluation metrics.  For FAKENewsNET, the tweet sequences are constructed from the dataset’s repository. The data is anonymized using unique identifiers and requires API hydration. While we successfully obtained the Politifact dataset from prior academic sharing, the GossipCop component could not be recovered due to current accessibility restrictions. Each sample in FAKENewsNET represents a series of tweets associated with the dissemination of a specific news article, providing insights into its spread dynamics.  This study underscores the importance of evaluation design, dataset selection, and feature engineering in achieving robust performance, particularly when addressing real-world constraints. By comparing simpler models with advanced architectures, we challenge the prevailing emphasis on model complexity and highlight the often-overlooked significance of thoughtful methodological choices in applied machine learning research.
