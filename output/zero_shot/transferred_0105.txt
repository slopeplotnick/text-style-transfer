Psychometric scales and questionnaires constitute pivotal instruments in research across domains such as psychology, medicine, social sciences, education, and human-computer interaction. The introduction and examination of novel concepts or theories typically necessitate the development of measurement tools. However, the construction of these instruments demands substantial effort, time, financial investment, and resources (Stefana et al., 2025). This article explores the potential to streamline this process through the utilization of computer simulations and data generated by Large Language Models (LLMs) for the creation of psychometric scales. The article explicates the rationale underpinning this approach, delineates four empirical studies conducted to evaluate its efficacy, and concludes with a discussion of the findings and recommendations for future research.  Securing a participant sample that accurately represents the target population is often costly and time-consuming, prompting researchers to frequently rely on convenience samples, such as university students who participate voluntarily. This practice poses challenges for the generalizability of findings to broader populations (Hanel & Vione, 2016; Wild et al., 2022). Recently, researchers have turned to online platforms such as Prolific.co and Amazon MTurk to recruit participants (Buhrmester et al., 2018; Douglas et al., 2023; Huang et al., 2015). These platforms facilitate more rapid and cost-effective participant recruitment and, in some cases, enable the assembly of samples that better represent specific populations, such as those in the UK or US. Nonetheless, ethical concerns persist regarding the potential exploitation of participants from marginalized backgrounds (Huang et al., 2015; Huff & Tingley, 2015; Moss et al., 2023).  In instances where researchers investigate a new concept without an existing validated measurement scale, or when scale validation is impracticable, they often resort to developing ad hoc questionnaires grounded solely in theoretical constructs. Although this strategy economizes on resources and time, it may yield results with limited external validity beyond the sample in question (M. K. Lee et al., 2020).
