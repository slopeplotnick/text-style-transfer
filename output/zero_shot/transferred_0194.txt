This approach can be applied universally across disciplines. For instance, geologists incorporate models and diagrams to articulate concepts in their research, mathematicians employ them to establish rules for probability frameworks, and geophysicists utilize them for Bayesian inversion processes. A critical consideration in this context is the use of alternative datasets, as the initial hypothesis may prove inaccurate. This issue is part of a broader scientific challenge: how can researchers validate their assumptions? The Austrian philosopher Karl Popper dedicated his career to addressing such questions. Popper argued against the use of inductive reasoning in science—where theories are derived directly from observations—and instead posited that scientific theories are conjectures formulated to address shortcomings in existing explanations. Once a theory is proposed, it must be rigorously tested against empirical observations. If a theory fails to align with the data, it should be rejected. Conversely, theories that withstand such tests are considered the most reliable within the current scientific framework.  In the context of uncertainty quantification, models can be regarded as informed guesses about reality. The number of potential models reflects the number of these guesses. While the formulation of models may be constrained by prior knowledge, empirical data should not be utilized to confirm a model’s validity; rather, it should be employed to discredit a model if it fails to align with the observed data. Popper’s philosophy provides a means to address the inherent subjectivity in Bayesian reasoning, a form of inductive inference, as opposed to the deductive method of falsifying hypotheses. Specifically, if a Bayesian prior distribution is incapable of predicting the observed data, it is invalidated. In other words, none of the models generated from the prior distribution would adequately account for the empirical evidence. While Popper’s framework may reduce the subjectivity associated with priors through empirical testing, he does not prescribe a method for revising the prior, leaving this task to human judgment.
