The primary question addressed in this study is whether contemporary large language models (LLMs), which are trained solely to predict subsequent words without explicit instruction in processing perceptual information, independently develop mechanisms for handling information in ways analogous to human perception. Understanding how LLMs manage uncertain information could provide valuable insights for constructing more robust systems capable of processing inputs of variable quality. To explore this, methods from psychophysics—a discipline focused on the relationship between stimuli and perception—were employed to investigate these models’ behavior. Treating the LLMs as "black boxes" without examining their internal architectures, the study instead analyzed their responses to controlled inputs to infer their information-processing mechanisms.  By systematically varying the uncertainty in input data and examining evidence of Bayesian processing—a probabilistic framework for decision-making under uncertainty—it was possible to assess whether LLMs exhibit perceptual behaviors comparable to those observed in humans, despite their lack of training in such tasks. The findings demonstrate that certain classical psychophysical tasks are effective for probing the behavior of LLMs and reveal notable patterns. These tasks facilitate the evaluation of LLMs’ capacity to handle diverse input qualities in a controlled experimental setting, enabling precise measurement of their responses. Unlike real-world tasks, which are often confounded by excessive noise, this controlled framework enables targeted assessments of specific strategies and uncovers potential limitations that may not be apparent in standard benchmarks.  This study makes three principal contributions. First, it introduces a psychophysics-inspired framework for evaluating LLMs, which comprises four tasks: length discrimination, location estimation, distance comparison, and temporal duration assessment. This framework allows for manipulation of factors such as noise and context to assess changes in model behavior. Second, it proposes a novel benchmarking tool, termed BayesBench, designed to evaluate models’ performance, efficiency in integrating information, and alignment with Bayesian reasoning, utilizing a newly developed scoring system. Lastly, the findings reveal that advanced LLMs exhibit behavior consistent with Bayesian reasoning in certain contexts. However, a critical limitation was identified, whereby even highly accurate models fail to adopt reliable strategies in specific scenarios.  By leveraging psychophysical methodologies, this study corroborates the value of classical perceptual tasks for probing LLM behavior. Psychophysics has historically demonstrated consistent relationships between physical stimuli and human perception, often encapsulated by foundational principles such as Weber-Fechner’s law. These findings highlight both the potential and the limitations of LLMs in tasks requiring probabilistic reasoning and perceptual-like behavior.
