In the realm of predictive modeling, machine learning techniques, such as neural networks and generative artificial intelligence, offer significant potential. For strategic planning under conditions of uncertainty, Monte Carlo Tree search algorithms are particularly beneficial. The field of artificial intelligence fundamentally consists of two major components: prediction and decision-making. This paper elucidates the application of these components within the context of exploration.   The process of prediction necessitates the utilization of datasets, which poses the most challenging aspect of mineral exploration—not the selection of a specific machine learning method. These datasets are characterized by their complexity, featuring numerous variables, three-dimensional structures, or extreme values. Such complexities are largely unprecedented in other disciplines. Two pervasive issues in the organization of data are often overlooked: the inconsistency in scaling across datasets and the inappropriateness of amalgamating disparate datasets. A methodology effective in one domain may be unsuitable in another. For instance, ground truth data, such as core samples, is frequently scaled to match geophysical data. Additionally, geophysical and geochemical datasets are sometimes combined in geologically unsound manners.   Machine learning models developed for one domain are frequently applied to another without adequate validation, resulting in increased false positives and undermining the perceived efficacy of artificial intelligence. However, the fundamental problem lies in data handling. A well-established pitfall in the mining industry is the variance differential across data measured at different scales. For example, interpolated geophysical data exhibits reduced variance compared to the original measurements, due to the interpolation process. Furthermore, drilling data captured at a granular scale is often expanded or adjusted to align with geophysical data, artificially inducing correlations between labels and predictors as a consequence of data manipulation. These issues contribute to the prevalence of false positives in current mineral potential or prospecting maps.
