Concerns regarding the legal and ethical implications of generative models are intensifying, particularly in relation to the use of copyrighted materials for model training. Central to this debate is the issue of attribution and the moral rights associated with creative works. Legal actions have been initiated, alleging that the unauthorized use of copyrighted content infringes upon property rights. Notably, the New York Times has filed a lawsuit against OpenAI for utilizing its news archives without consent, while Getty Images is pursuing legal action against Stability AI for generating content derived from its database. Beyond these legal proceedings, there is also growing dissent among artists. Prominent figures such as Elton John, Dua Lipa, along with 200 other artists, have collectively authored a letter urging technology companies to safeguard human creativity from AI systems capable of mimicking their voices and artistic styles without authorization.  In response to these challenges, various organizations have proposed regulatory frameworks. The European Union's AI Act emphasizes the necessity for transparency in AI systems, whereas the United Kingdom and UNESCO have developed guidelines advocating for responsible AI use that ensures the protection of privacy and intellectual property. Denmark has recently proposed a pioneering amendment to its copyright law, granting individuals rights over their voice, facial features, and bodily likenesses. This initiative, unprecedented in Europe, aims to address the legal complexities arising from AI that replicates personal characteristics. The Danish Culture Minister emphasized the importance of safeguarding individual rights to one's body, voice, and face, underscoring the imperative of identity protection in the face of generative AI advancements.  Despite these regulatory advances, most generative models remain opaque, rendering it challenging to discern the influence of training data on their outputs. Even open-source models are of such complexity and scale that exact replication is impractical. Current methodologies, such as influence functions or retraining-based attribution, require access to the internal mechanisms of these systems.
