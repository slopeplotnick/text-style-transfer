Recent advancements in the development of large language models (LLMs) have significantly improved their capacity to process information and generate responses, fostering the perception that such models are increasingly capable of providing accurate and reliable outputs. However, emerging evidence suggests that iterative reasoning processes within these models can lead to the reinforcement of existing beliefs rather than the discovery of objective truths. In this study, we propose a novel methodology to evaluate the extent to which LLMs exhibit belief entrenchment—a phenomenon wherein models persist in maintaining prior beliefs despite opportunities for revision. Our approach leverages the mathematical concept of the Martingale property, a principle in probability theory which posits that if beliefs are updated rationally, future beliefs should align with present ones, and it should be impossible to predict future belief changes based solely on current beliefs.  To operationalize this concept, we introduce the *Martingale Score*, a metric designed to identify instances where belief updates deviate from this rationality criterion, thereby signaling potential failures in belief updating. Through application across diverse domains, including event prediction, opinion-based question answering, and academic paper review, we observe frequent instances of belief entrenchment in LLMs. Our empirical analysis further examines the relationship between belief entrenchment and factors such as model architecture, reasoning strategies, and subject domains.   Importantly, we demonstrate that the Martingale Score correlates with model accuracy in tasks where ground-truth answers are available. This indicates that while the score is formulated to function independently of explicit correctness benchmarks, it nonetheless serves as a valuable proxy for assessing model performance and epistemic reliability. Analogous to human cognitive biases, such as confirmation bias or susceptibility to social conformity, our findings highlight how LLMs may exhibit tendencies to anchor to existing beliefs, potentially limiting their capacity to synthesize novel or divergent information. These insights offer critical implications for the development and evaluation of LLMs, particularly in contexts where unbiased and adaptive reasoning is essential.
