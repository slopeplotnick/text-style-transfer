Our proposed method enhances the precision of actions, as demonstrated in simulation experiments. Furthermore, it addresses issues related to policy failures resulting from suboptimal teleoperation data. During the Pen Grasping phase, variations in the operator’s grasping strategies and timing result in significant state disparities across grasping modes. This creates a sparse distribution of observation-action pairs within the expert dataset, which frequently leads to instability in the base policy. Such instability manifests in behaviors such as improper closure of the gripper, reopening the gripper post-grasp, or oscillating between grasping modes. By applying our scaling method during testing, these issues are largely mitigated: the gripper closes correctly, and the robot successfully avoids undesired modes of operation.   This improvement can be attributed to the ability of our scaling method to filter out suboptimal trajectories introduced by noise in the teleoperated dataset. Attempting to model the mapping from features to action errors increases the complexity of the learning process, as the model is required to account for both optimal and suboptimal features. However, our test-time scaling approach focuses solely on selecting the optimal action, which simplifies this process. Empirical results indicate that performance declines are primarily caused by overfitting to the training dataset, which subsequently hampers the generalization ability of the model. Moreover, when separate encoders are employed in place of Internal Features and are trained alongside the Contrastive Feature Network (CFN), the resulting feature representations tend to converge. This lack of feature diversity impairs accurate pseudo-counting, leading to a significant reduction in performance. Additionally, this approach is less efficient as it fails to leverage the inherent scene-action understanding provided by Vision-Language Architectures (VLAs).   In this study, we introduce TACO—a test-time scaling framework that incorporates a straightforward pseudo-count estimator to serve as a robust validator for action segments. The TACO framework is compatible with various VLA models and consistently enhances base policy performance across both real-world and simulated tasks. While TACO has certain limitations, such as its inability to evaluate novel actions generated exclusively through perturbations and its reliance on the representational capacity of the underlying VLA, our findings demonstrate that simple test-time scaling effectively mitigates exploration challenges and reduces instability and performance degradation caused by distribution shifts in action prediction.
