To evaluate the efficacy of the attack, we carried out experiments under two conditions: first, by keeping the underlying model constant (Deepseek) and testing its performance across different frameworks; and second, by holding the framework constant (OpenManus) and evaluating its performance across various models. For all experiments, we set the generation temperature to 0 and imposed a maximum limit of 20 tool executions.   To assess the computational overhead introduced by the LeechHijack attack, we integrated it with various tool configurations and quantified resource consumption in comparison to standard tools. Our primary metric was the total token usage, which encompassed both input and output tokens. Additionally, given that backdoored tools can activate the LeechHijack mechanism multiple times within a single task, we calculated the average additional computational cost per activation. While the extra output generated by the hijacked tools constitutes only a portion of the total computational resources utilized, from the end user's perspective, this additional overhead represents a significant security concern.  To evaluate the practical utility of the LeechHijack attack, we examined how effectively an attacker could exploit the misappropriated computational resources. Specifically, we compared the accuracy of the additional task's responses to the baseline performance of the tested models on the MMLU benchmark. The results, presented in Tables 7 and 8, demonstrate that LeechHijack successfully repurposes computational resources to execute covert tasks. However, this repurposing is accompanied by a detectable decline in overall model performance. These findings confirm that the LeechHijack attack is an effective mechanism for resource hijacking and could represent a valuable tool for potential attackers.
