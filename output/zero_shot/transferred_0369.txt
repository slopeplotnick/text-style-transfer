Empirical analysis indicates that the sequence in which input articles are presented to a summarization model significantly influences the generated summaries. Specifically, these models exhibit a bias toward the first article in the sequence, which affects their framing of information and the degree to which the summaries align with the original content. Prior research has documented that such models tend to allocate greater attention to earlier sections of lengthy inputs. Our findings extend this observation to scenarios involving multiple input documents, demonstrating a consistent prioritization of the first article when the model ranks and integrates information.   This phenomenon is particularly evident when evaluated using BERTScore, a metric designed to assess semantic similarity. The analysis reveals that models disproportionately incorporate words and arguments from the first article compared to subsequent ones. Consequently, summaries show a higher similarity to the first article, as reflected by elevated BERTScore metrics. In contrast, evaluation methods such as ROUGE-L, which primarily measure lexical overlap, and SummaC, which assesses factual consistency, are less sensitive to this framing bias. Although all three evaluation metrics consistently highlight the primacy of the first article over the second and third, they reveal minimal variation between the second and third articles, suggesting that the model's bias is localized to the first input rather than a gradual degradation of attention toward later documents.  This prioritization of the first article has practical implications, particularly in real-world applications of multi-document summarization systems. For instance, AI tools such as Google Search’s summary generation, which reportedly employs Gemini models, may disproportionately emphasize information from the first article processed while de-emphasizing or neglecting information in subsequent articles. Such biases could introduce skewed viewpoints into the summaries, potentially influencing users' understanding of a topic, especially given the high degree of trust users place in AI-generated content. Addressing these biases is essential to ensure fair, comprehensive, and representative outputs in multi-document summarization systems.
