The present study demonstrates that employing prompt-engineered large language models (LLMs) integrated with Isolation Forest scores can outperform conventional Joint Entropy Trees (JETs) and machine learning (ML) methodologies in anomaly detection, while concurrently providing explanations in straightforward language. Despite the challenges associated with false positives, implementation costs, and practical application, the findings underscore the potential of artificial intelligence to enhance auditing processes by increasing accuracy and comprehensibility. This investigation represents a novel contribution by establishing the feasibility of LLMs in processing tax ledger data, a domain that demands clarity and reliability. The results indicate that the integration of LLMs with established anomaly detection methodologies offers a viable approach to optimizing accuracy, recall, and interpretability.  Future research directions will involve the examination of larger and more diverse datasets to assess the efficacy of varying prompts and model modifications. It is noted that the current experiment employed a singular anonymous tax ledger alongside a synthetic benchmark; real-world ledgers exhibit variability across industries, systems, and geographic regions, complicating the acquisition of genuine labels. Simulated labels derived from JETs may introduce biases, while simulated anomalies are established through simulators. Hence, further validation with expansive and heterogeneous datasets and authentic audit labels remains necessary.  The stability and clarity of the model's outputs are susceptible to the nuances of prompt phrasing and the selection of models, as minor adjustments can significantly impact results, and disparate models may yield divergent outcomes. While the AuditCopilot framework mitigates discrepancies, it cannot wholly safeguard against variations due to changes in phrasing, complex inputs, or model updates. Moreover, although auditors may find plain language explanations beneficial, such explanations may not always align with formal decision rules.  The experimental application of LLMs in financial audits appears promising, yet it is accompanied by potential risks such as errors, biases, prompt-related issues, and fluctuations in model parameters. It is emphasized that while the proposed methodology holds promise for aiding decision-making, it necessitates rigorous human oversight, controls, and regulatory compliance prior to practical implementation.
