The effective management of multi-agent systems necessitates a paradigm shift from adherence to predefined rules toward holistic system-level oversight. This involves examining the propagation of influence among agents, the impact of oversight signals on collective behavior, and the conditions under which feedback loops become unmanageable. The central premise is that while foundational safety measures remain essential, they are insufficient when models operate within interaction networks. Even with optimal performance at the local level, systemic failures may occur due to emergent dynamics involving feedback, interconnections, and collective incentives.  Recent research has investigated risks inherent to systems composed of multiple agents, proposing frameworks to categorize and address these challenges. Two notable contributions include the Multi-Agent System Failure Taxonomy (MAST) and the Multi-Agent Risks from Advanced AI (MARAI). MAST categorizes failures into three primary domains: risks arising from system design, agent coordination challenges, and limitations in system verification. In contrast, MARAI classifies three types of failures based on agent interactions: miscoordination, conflict, and collusion, all of which can drive misaligned behaviors.   In light of these insights, this paper proposes a novel framework that integrates and extends the aforementioned approaches. Specifically, it: (1) synthesizes MAST and MARAI into a unified framework that prioritizes system-level risks over isolated agent-level failures, and (2) formalizes this perspective through the introduction of the Emergent Systemic Risk Horizon (ESRH). The ESRH delineates the threshold at which localized reliability gives way to systemic instability, offering a conceptual and methodological foundation for analyzing large language model (LLM) ecosystems as complex adaptive systems rather than as discrete agents.   This framework underpins the organization of the paper. Section 2 discusses the transition to multi-agent systems and the regulatory implications of distributed causality. Section 3 elaborates on the ESRH taxonomy and associated metrics for evaluating systemic risks. Finally, Section 4 introduces the concept of Institutional AI architecture, a design proposal aimed at embedding governance mechanisms directly into the operation of interacting model groups.
