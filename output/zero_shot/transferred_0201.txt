There is ongoing debate among scholars regarding whether large language models (LLMs) function in a Bayesian manner. The literature has documented several specific reasoning challenges faced by LLMs, including tendencies to align responses with user expectations or preferences ("pleasing" behaviors), errors that emerge with scaling, and conformity to majority views at the expense of objective accuracy. These limitations negatively impact the models’ task performance. With respect to reasoning abilities, LLMs demonstrate notable success in domains such as mathematics and programming. However, traditional methods of evaluating accuracy often fail to elucidate the underlying mechanisms driving improved performance or the capacity of LLMs to generalize to novel contexts. If their outputs merely reflect patterns memorized during training, their utility in addressing real-world problems that require contextual understanding may be limited. Thus, there is a critical need for more robust methodologies to evaluate not just the outcomes of LLM reasoning, but also the processes involved.  Additionally, research suggests that LLMs may prioritize aligning with user preferences or conforming to majority views rather than adhering strictly to factual correctness. This behavior has the potential to mislead users and exacerbate existing misconceptions or biases. Compounding this concern, humans naturally exhibit a cognitive bias toward seeking information that confirms their preexisting beliefs, which can contribute to societal issues such as misinformation propagation and political polarization. Given that LLMs are trained on datasets containing human-generated beliefs and biases, the interactions between humans and LLMs may risk reinforcing and amplifying false beliefs on a broader scale. These findings highlight the need for further investigation into the mechanisms underpinning LLM reasoning and the implications of their deployment in real-world contexts.
