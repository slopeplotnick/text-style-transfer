The origins of the studied methods are not a primary consideration in this analysis. Hallucination Detection (HD) methods rely on internal model states, which Fact Verification (FV) approaches cannot access. Efforts to address this limitation, such as the development of static benchmarks for assessing outputs of large language models (LLMs) (e.g., HELM), have proven inadequate. These benchmarks struggle to evaluate novel or atypical models and fail to monitor changes in the generative patterns of LLMs. Consequently, this misalignment has resulted in the emergence of two distinct research trajectories, which has led to inefficiencies and left critical questions unresolved. Specifically, it remains unclear whether one approach outperforms the other, how their respective strengths diverge, or whether they can be integrated effectively. Additionally, the separation of these approaches limits their potential synergies: HD’s internal signals could enhance FV’s evidence-based reasoning, while FV’s contextual grounding could refine HD’s uncertainty assessments.  In response to this fragmentation, we have undertaken the first large-scale study to directly compare Hallucination Detection and Fact Verification within a unified framework. This investigation extends beyond the creation of benchmarks to conduct an extensive examination of the intrinsic connections between these two methodologies. By integrating them, this study seeks to address three critical research questions: (1) How do HD and FV perform in detecting factual inaccuracies in the same LLM outputs? (2) Do HD and FV identify distinct types of errors, reflecting complementary strengths, or do they encounter similar limitations? (3) Can signals from both methods be combined to construct a hybrid system that surpasses each individual approach in performance?  Historically, such a comparison has been deemed infeasible due to the fundamental differences between the two methodologies. However, we introduced a novel framework, *UniFact*, which resolves the incompatibilities that have divided the field. Unlike prior work that relies on static LLM outputs, UniFact employs a dynamic approach. It prompts LLMs to generate responses to factual questions in real-time and evaluates the accuracy of these responses using an automated process. This evaluation compares the generated outputs against both ground truth answers and pre-labeled reference knowledge. This dynamic setup is critical, as it provides the external evidence needed for FV while simultaneously enabling access to the internal signals required by HD. By aligning both methods on a shared dataset, UniFact facilitates a direct comparison and paves the way for a more unified and robust approach to assessing factual accuracy in LLMs.
