This paper examines the influence of initial noise patterns in flow-matching and diffusion-based setups, highlighting the impact of random noise on action generation. Specifically, we address the issue of managing shifts in data distribution without excessive exploration and propose a novel framework termed *Test-time Anti-exploration via pseudo-Counts* (TACO). The concept of anti-exploration originates in offline reinforcement learning (RL), where the objective is to constrain the policy from engaging in behaviors that are absent from the training dataset. In a similar vein, during Visual-Language Action (VLA) inference, the goal is to restrict actions to align with the successful patterns observed in the Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) dataset while minimizing the incorporation of irrelevant or non-optimal actions that may stem from pretraining or suboptimal fine-tuning data.  TACO introduces a unique approach by focusing on modifying the action-sampling process during test time, rather than altering the underlying policy. This strategy addresses the inherent challenges of applying standard RL techniques to flow-matching or diffusion-based VLA models, which often involve complex sampling mechanisms that are incompatible with traditional gradient-based updates. Instead, TACO circumvents this limitation by modifying the sampling process directly, eliminating the need for gradient computations.  To estimate pseudo-counts for each observation-instruction-action triplet from the SFT dataset, we employ a classical Coin-Flipping Network (CFN) with a specialized internal structure. These pseudo-counts serve as a measure of alignment with the frequent and successful actions in the SFT dataset, with higher pseudo-counts indicating greater conformity. During decision-making, TACO leverages these pseudo-counts to select the optimal action piece from a set of candidates, prioritizing the option with the highest pseudo-count.  To enhance computational efficiency, particularly when processing multiple test instances, we integrate a shared observation key-value cache. This design choice facilitates the reuse of visual-language information across samples, significantly reducing processing time. Collectively, TACO offers a systematic method for mitigating unnecessary exploration during test time while maintaining alignment with high-quality, dataset-consistent actions.
