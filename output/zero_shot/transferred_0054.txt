Udio possesses the capability to transform natural-language prompts into complete musical compositions in a variety of styles, incorporating lyrics in multiple languages. The perception of AI-generated music has been the subject of investigation within the fields of computational creativity and machine learning, where studies have explored the creative potential of these models through listener evaluations. Participants are tasked with assessing music produced by these models in comparison to music generated by other models or by human composers. Evaluations frequently focus on parameters such as overall quality, creativity, novelty, and specific musical elements, including melody, harmony, and rhythm. A critical aspect of these assessments is the perceived naturalness or human-like quality of the music, often measured using rating scales or a musical Turing Test. This test involves participants discerning whether a piece was composed by a human or AI, sometimes with varying levels of information about the actual composer. In tasks such as the creation of Bach chorales, early models like Deepbach have achieved results that are indistinguishable from authentic compositions. To evaluate the emotional dimensions of AI-generated music, listeners typically attempt to identify the intended emotional cues, categorizing them as "happy" or "sad," or employing models that assess emotional dimensions. As the sophistication of AI music increases, research has examined whether listeners' judgments are influenced by their perceptions or beliefs regarding the music's origin. However, findings in this area are not uniform: initial studies that manipulated participants' knowledge about the composer did not reveal significant biases when evaluating live recordings of string-quartet pieces produced by humans and computers. Similarly, research conducted by Zlatkov and colleagues demonstrated no bias against AI-generated music using pop music samples. Further studies focusing on consumer marketing, such as those by Moura and Maw, also concluded that there is no overt preference for music based on the identity of the composer, whether in classical or pop genres.
