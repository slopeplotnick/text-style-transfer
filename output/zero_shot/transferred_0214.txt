The phenomenon whereby adversarial training facilitates the transferability of attacks is not attributed to specific model architectures but rather to the way adversarial training alters the information processing mechanisms within models. This effect is observed across a wide range of model architectures, underscoring its relevance to improving the overall robustness of machine learning systems. To investigate the underlying reasons for increased transferability induced by adversarial training, researchers employed the method of integrated gradients to analyze how models explain their decision-making processes. For instance, a comparative study was conducted on two versions of the DenseNet121 architecture: one trained using adversarial training (AT) and the other trained using standard training (ST). The AT-trained model demonstrated an ability to focus on salient features of an image, such as the head, body, and legs of an ostrich, whereas the ST-trained model tended to emphasize finer details, such as feather textures or background elements. This distinction is significant, as AT models prioritize generalizable features that are prevalent across multiple images, whereas ST models concentrate on specific, localized details that are less transferable across different models.  The enhanced transferability associated with adversarial examples generated by AT-trained models can thus be attributed to their reliance on features that are commonly utilized across diverse model architectures. For example, within the DenseNet family, DenseNet121 and DenseNet201 share similarities in their image processing methodologies, while MobileNetV3 exhibits vulnerabilities due to its adoption of design principles inspired by EfficientNet. Consequently, adversarial attacks originating from EfficientNet-based models are more effective against MobileNetV3 due to the shared structural characteristics.
