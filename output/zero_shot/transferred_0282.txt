**Fact Verification:** The performance of fact verification (FV) methods is strongly influenced by the effectiveness of the retrieval component and the availability of external evidentiary information. As shown in Table 3, failures in the retrieval process—particularly when relevant supporting evidence is not retrieved—pose significant challenges. Specifically, when the retrieval mechanism, such as in a large language model question-answering setup (LLM-Q), fails to locate pertinent information due to mismatched keywords, the verification component is forced to make decisions in the absence of sufficient evidence. This lack of corroborating information results in the model exhibiting erratic behavior: either overestimating uncertainty and producing false positives (e.g., Case 1) or overlooking inaccuracies and producing false negatives (e.g., Case 2). Conversely, when the retrieval process incorporates the generated answer (LLM-QA), the system is more likely to retrieve relevant documents, thereby enabling the FV model to make more accurate predictions.  **Hallucination Detection:** Hallucination detection (HD) methods are often challenged by the inherent variability and creativity of language generation. As evidenced in Table 4, HD methods perform adequately when dealing with straightforward responses (Group A) but struggle with more nuanced or creative outputs (Group B). In such cases, the model may correctly identify factual information (e.g., the formation date of a music band) but encounter difficulty in determining the appropriate phrasing. Current HD methodologies are limited in their ability to differentiate between legitimate linguistic variation and actual fabrications. Consequently, when a statement permits multiple valid phrasings, it is frequently misclassified as erroneous, even when the underlying information is accurate.  **Implications for Hybrid Design:** The findings of this study underscore the distinct limitations of HD and FV methods, highlighting the necessity of integrating the two approaches. FV operates as a precise mechanism, excelling when appropriate evidence is available but struggling in its absence. In contrast, HD functions as a broader safety measure, capable of identifying potential errors without relying on external evidence but prone to misinterpretation of linguistic variability. A well-designed hybrid framework should account for these differences, assigning greater weight to FV predictions when sufficient evidence supports verification, while relying on HD to detect inconsistencies in scenarios where retrieval fails. This balanced approach informed the development of the hybrid framework proposed in our RQ3.
