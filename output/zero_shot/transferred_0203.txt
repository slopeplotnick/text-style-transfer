Video language models are increasingly utilized in applications such as AI-powered glasses and domestic robots. These models are required to process extensive video content, often encompassing several hours or even days of footage. Recent advancements employ memory systems to identify and extract salient portions of video data, enabling the models to concentrate on essential information without the computational burden of analyzing every individual frame. This selective approach is both more cost-effective and computationally efficient compared to methods that process video content in its entirety. Despite these advancements, most contemporary techniques heavily rely on textual data, often converting video content into captions or summaries to facilitate information retrieval and analysis. While some approaches incorporate visual information, their use is typically limited to object recognition, with little emphasis on leveraging broader visual context.  Additionally, existing models frequently process a predetermined number of video clips—for instance, three 30-second segments—when analyzing extensive video datasets. However, this methodology presents two primary limitations. First, it inadequately integrates visual data with textual memory for tasks requiring detailed feature detection, spatial reasoning, or scene understanding—capabilities that textual information alone cannot sufficiently capture. Second, relying on both captions and visual frames to generate responses can overwhelm the model with excessive visual input, potentially impairing its performance. Therefore, there is a critical need to develop methods for dynamically balancing textual and visual information in response to specific queries. Despite the significance of such optimization, this area remains underexplored in current research.
