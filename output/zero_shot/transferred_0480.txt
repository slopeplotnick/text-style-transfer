Software product lines represent a prevalent methodology for developing customizable software solutions. Conceptually, they function analogously to a supply chain within the software domain, comprising various component parts. Feature models delineate the potential attributes of a software product and specify the ways in which these features may be combined. Furthermore, a repository of software components enables the implementation of these features. Within a software product line, specialized tools facilitate the selection of features to configure a particular setup and subsequently assemble the final product by integrating components from the repository. This approach is advantageous in scenarios requiring the creation of multiple distinct configurations. However, companies may encounter challenges when initiating this strategy for novel product types. As a result, many organizations initially develop a series of similar software systems before transitioning their development process to a product-line architecture. Despite documented instances of successful implementation in the literature, the transformation of an extant collection of software systems into a unified product line platform remains complex, often engendering reluctance among firms. Concerns primarily arise from the lack of explicitly defined implementation pathways and uncertainties regarding the cost-benefit ratio. Consequently, even with substantial well-documented code bases, companies frequently resort to manually crafting custom applications directly from the base code to address client-specific requirements, often employing a strategy known as clone-and-own.  Our collaborator, ITK - Predict and Decide, constitutes a small to medium-sized enterprise comprising 120 personnel, which includes 19 agronomists, 29 developers, 2 UI/UX designers, and 4 system administrators. Approximately one-third of the workforce is concentrated in technology-oriented roles. The organization adopts an agile methodology for system development, ensuring effective operational processes for development and delivery. ITK specializes in applications that assist agricultural stakeholders by enabling the prediction of plant and animal diseases and crop outcomes. Central to these applications is a suite of simulators developed by the agronomy team, to which the IT team appends additional code for data acquisition from IoT devices or online services, thereby populating the simulators and visualizing results on dashboards. The back-end infrastructure of the software is implemented using Kotlin, while the front-end is constructed utilizing Angular, with extensive use of TypeScript.
