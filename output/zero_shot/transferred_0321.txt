Each article was divided into smaller segments to ensure that both the summary and each segment remained within a 512-token limit. The similarity between the summary and each segment was assessed, and the final SummaC score was obtained by averaging the scores across all segments. This score ranges between 0 and 1. To examine the potential influence of input order on summary content, heatmaps and a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) were employed. The findings reveal that summaries exhibit greater similarity to the first article presented in the input sequence. Figure 3 presents the average scores for three evaluation metrics across six input orders and three stance categories. The average score for each condition was calculated by aggregating scores across all article groups with identical input orders and stances. Variations in shading and scores highlight that the position of the input significantly impacts similarity patterns in summaries generated by large language models (LLMs). Specifically, higher ROUGE-L and BERTScore values were observed when comparing summaries to the first document in the sequence. Furthermore, BERTScore values for the first position exceeded those for the second and third positions, suggesting that documents presented earlier contribute more prominently to the generated summaries.  The results for Research Question 1 (RQ1) consistently demonstrate that summaries are more similar to the first article in an input sequence. However, the observed metric values deviate from those typically reported in summarization research. This discrepancy arises because, in this study, the generated summaries were directly compared to the full original articles rather than to human-written reference summaries, as is customary in prior research. The generated summaries contain approximately 220 words, whereas the source articles range from 300 to 1,600 words, which naturally reduces token overlap and lowers similarity scores. Consequently, this study focuses on relative score differences across input conditions rather than absolute metric values.  An additional challenge lies in the lack of evaluation metrics designed to assess the similarity between summaries and their source documents. Most existing evaluation frameworks rely on reference-based approaches, leaving limited methodologies to measure source alignment directly. SummaC, which evaluates sentence-level entailment, often produces low scores with minimal variation across positional conditions. Abstract summaries inherently omit specific details from the source material, and entailment-based scoring penalizes unsupported or implied statements. Furthermore, although the model may prioritize content from the first article, it is unlikely to replicate its exact wording. Therefore, SummaC scores fail to capture subtle content biases unless explicit supporting sentences from the original are preserved.
