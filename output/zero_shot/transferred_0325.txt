Agentic systems can be conceptualized as contemporary software architectures comprising various integrated components that interact through interfaces and are configured within software processes. Unlike traditional programs, these systems operate in a stochastic and adaptive manner to address complex problems. Evaluation of these systems typically focuses on their ultimate success or failure, which overlooks the intricate processes of reasoning, planning, execution, and adaptation that occur over time. To address this gap, we developed GRAPHECTORY, a framework designed to map the temporal and semantic relationships within these systems, thereby facilitating the development of metrics for performance assessment that extend beyond mere success rates.   Utilizing GRAPHECTORY, we conducted an analysis of 4000 pathways generated by two predominant agentic programming methodologies, SWE-agent and OpenHands, employing four prominent Large Language Models (LLMs) in an effort to resolve SWE-bench Verified issues. Our automated evaluations yielded several insights: (1) systems equipped with more effective prompts or more robust LLMs exhibit more intricate GRAPHECTORY structures, indicative of enhanced cognitive processes, comprehensive context assimilation, and meticulous validation prior to implementing solutions; (2) the strategies employed by these systems in problem-solving vary based on problem difficulty and the specific LLM utilized—for successfully resolved issues, methodologies typically adhere to a structured sequence of error identification, resolution, and verification, whereas unresolved problems exhibit disordered, iterative, or regressive patterns; (3) notwithstanding their efficacy, agentic programming systems frequently exhibit protracted operational cycles, resulting in unnecessarily elongated pathways.  Agentic systems leveraging large language models (LLMs) represent an innovative approach to tackling challenging, multi-faceted tasks across diverse domains, including software engineering, web navigation, virtual assistance, scientific research, and robotic navigation. Analogous to conventional software systems, agentic systems are composed of elements such as LLMs, tools, APIs, and memory, and encompass input, internal state, operational mechanisms, and output. However, rather than following a predetermined sequence, these systems generate dynamic pathways—consisting of sequential cognitive, behavioral, and observational steps—that collectively formulate the problem-solving logic. Historically, the evaluation of agentic software systems has predominantly emphasized their ability to resolve a problem, focusing on the outcome rather than the process.
