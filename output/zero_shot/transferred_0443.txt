In this study, we utilize two primary metrics to assess performance: the Toxic Score and the Attack Success Rate (ASR). Consistent with prior research, we employ GPT-4o to evaluate the harmfulness of a response. When presented with a harmful query and a model-generated response, an evaluator assigns a Toxic Score ranging from 1 to 5. A higher score indicates a greater degree of harmfulness, with a score of 5 signifying that the response fully aligns with the harmful intent of the query. An attack is deemed successful if it receives a score of 5. The evaluation prompt is detailed in Appendix B.3. Our study comprises four distinct components, each optimized for different contexts and models. An attack is considered successful if either the original target image or any of the four enhanced target images achieves success. To determine the Toxic Score, we select the highest score from five evaluations for each example in a given scenario. Results are predominantly presented for the Demonstrative Framework; however, additional results for other strategies are available in Appendices C and D. Extensive testing was conducted using the MMSafetyBench-tiny and SafeBench-tiny datasets, with comprehensive results provided in Tables 1 and 2. The approach involves multiple rounds and images during interactions, as well as robust red-teaming models to generate attack prompts, resulting in higher computational and financial costs compared to CIA. Conversely, CIA requires the generation of only a single image per query and employs a more straightforward attack method, making it more suitable for scalability and repetition. Furthermore, CIA demonstrates efficacy in challenging domains such as illegal advice, fraud, and hate crime, indicating that it exploits a general vulnerability in the alignment of vision and language in current models, rather than relying on specific category templates.
