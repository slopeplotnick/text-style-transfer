In recent years, brain-computer interfaces (BCIs) have garnered significant attention as mechanisms facilitating direct communication between the human brain and electronic devices or digital systems. These interfaces are categorized as either invasive, requiring surgical intervention, or non-invasive, which do not necessitate surgical procedures. This emerging technology has piqued the interest of scientists, medical professionals, and media representatives alike. BCIs hold promise in assisting individuals with severe motor or speech impairments, such as those resulting from locked-in syndrome, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), spinal cord injuries, or cerebrovascular accidents, facilitating communication and enabling movement in situations where such abilities would otherwise be compromised.  Consequently, there is a pressing need to enhance non-invasive BCIs through the comprehensive analysis of electroencephalogram (EEG) data. The extraction of features from EEG signals plays a crucial role, utilizing methodologies that range from basic temporal and spectral analyses to more sophisticated techniques incorporating multidimensional approaches. Transforming raw EEG signals into actionable information—such as time-dependent statistics, spectral power density, or integrated time-frequency methods like wavelet transforms—is essential for accurate signal classification and optimal system functionality. Furthermore, it is imperative that extracted features exhibit robustness across individuals, given the substantial variability in EEG signals among different subjects.  Challenges such as temporal fluctuations and inter-dataset variability necessitate the adoption of methods like transfer learning and domain generalization to ensure feature reliability. A pivotal issue within this domain is identifying optimal combinations of feature-extraction and classification methodologies, particularly in light of the inherent diversity of individual EEG signals. Addressing this issue requires rigorous evaluation practices designed to prevent overly optimistic results. These include cross-validation techniques that exclude data from subjects involved in model training (e.g., leave-one-subject-out cross-validation), utilization of metrics adept at managing data imbalances (such as balanced accuracy and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve), employment of statistical tests with minimal assumptions regarding data distribution, and stringent prevention of data leakage. For instance, operations such as data filtering or normalization must be restricted to training datasets to maintain the integrity of test datasets.
