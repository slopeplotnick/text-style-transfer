Self-consistency is a methodological approach that involves analyzing a set of results and adopting the conclusion indicated by the majority. In contrast, soft self-consistency involves selecting the lowest, average, or composite confidence scores within each group. Generative self-aggregation involves employing a language model to synthesize a new response based on a variety of sample inputs. Universal self-consistency is characterized by utilizing the language model to select the most suitable answer from among several options.  This study examines several computational models designed for advanced reasoning, specifically gemini-2.5-flash, qwen3 next-80b, and gpt-oss-120b. The investigation focuses on two machine translation tasks and six tasks derived from the Reward Bench 2 benchmark, which evaluates reward models across six domains: factual accuracy, precise adherence to instructions, mathematical problem-solving, safety considerations, attention to focus, and handling of tie situations.  To conduct this evaluation, preference pairs were constructed by pairing all possible combinations from each task's source data, which comprises both accepted and rejected responses. These pairs are categorized into 'non-tie' pairs (comprising one accepted and one rejected response) and 'tie' pairs (consisting of either two accepted or two rejected responses). From this comprehensive dataset, 1,000 examples are selected for each of the six tasks, ultimately constructing the final benchmark. Detailed explanations of the voting distributions for each task are provided in Appendix B.  The results observed across the different reasoning models, namely gemini-2.5-flash, gpt-oss-120b, and qwen3 next-80b, demonstrate a consistent pattern, suggesting that the employed methodology is effective across these models. The study elucidates the structural dynamics of the problem, including the frequency of ambiguous cases prompted by the task, the clarity versus ambiguity in the language model's outputs, and the alignment of these characteristics with the Mean Absolute Error (MAE)-aligned Davidson model.  Certain tasks exhibit smooth, readily applicable calibration landscapes, whereas others present more abrupt landscapes that are less adaptable. Future research could explore the development of robust assessments to predict cross-task applicability and evaluate resilience under significant changes in distribution or external targets.
