The Area Under the Curve (AUC) is commonly utilized to evaluate a model's ability to rank predictions effectively, independent of specific decision thresholds. This metric is particularly advantageous when dealing with imbalanced datasets. In this paper, we introduce a novel evaluation framework that integrates Hallucination Detection and Fact Verification, two areas that have traditionally been studied in isolation. We begin by examining the reasons for this historical separation and identifying the limitations in existing evaluation methods that hinder the simultaneous assessment of these two tasks. Subsequently, we present the core principles behind our framework and elaborate on its methodology.  The separation between Hallucination Detection and Fact Verification is rooted in the distinct design and objectives of traditional datasets and evaluation approaches associated with each task. Fact Verification datasets, such as FEVER, typically consist of fixed pairs of claims and corresponding labels without consideration of the source or generation process of the claims. These datasets are designed to benchmark text-based verification tasks. Conversely, Hallucination Detection methods focus on examining model generation processes, such as analyzing internal states or ensuring consistency across outputs. The absence of generation-related signals in fixed Fact Verification datasets renders them unsuitable for evaluating Hallucination Detection techniques.   One proposed solution to this gap involves leveraging pre-generated outputs from large language models (e.g., HELM) to create new evaluation benchmarks. While this approach offers utility, it is accompanied by two significant challenges within the rapidly evolving landscape of artificial intelligence. First, models become obsolete quickly. For instance, constructing a dataset using outputs from state-of-the-art models such as LLaMA-3 may result in a resource that becomes merely a historical artifact as newer models are released. Second, fixed datasets are unable to accommodate the evaluation of novel models. Hallucination Detection methods are often tailored to the properties of specific models, and a static evaluation framework limits the ability to test these methods' adaptability and effectiveness against emerging architectures. As a result, the development of an evaluation system capable of addressing these limitations is essential for fostering progress in both Hallucination Detection and Fact Verification.
