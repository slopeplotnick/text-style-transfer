In order to effectively interpret user queries and comprehend the characteristics of items, a system must initially possess foundational knowledge regarding the subject matter. This foundational knowledge enables the system to accurately discern user intentions, identify pertinent item details, and align them with user requirements. Visual representations of items often convey attributes that are challenging to articulate verbally, such as aesthetic or tactile qualities. Consequently, the system examines images of items and integrates the information derived from both textual and visual sources, thereby enhancing its understanding and providing a more comprehensive depiction of the item. Subsequent to identifying user needs and extracting item details, the system must adhere to predefined rules to verify and correlate these details. This involves utilizing these rules to ensure logical coherence and functional accuracy.  Step 1: Knowledge and Reasoning. Section 2.3 delineates knowledge into two categories: general knowledge pertaining to the world and specialized knowledge relevant to e-commerce. General knowledge is largely acquired by large language models through extensive training, enabling them to comprehend common concepts and relationships. However, e-commerce presents unique challenges with numerous phrases and terms that cannot be accurately interpreted based solely on their literal meanings. These challenges predominantly manifest in two areas: (1) Rare Queries: These involve highly specific inquiries about atypical items, where models may lack the requisite background knowledge to effectively correlate terminology with appropriate meanings. For instance, a search query such as "strange things are also loved by someone" related to "novel and interesting toys" actually references a book title. Absent prior knowledge, the system may erroneously associate the query with unrelated items. Additional examples encompass brand-specific terminology, product nicknames, and marketing language that holds unique significance within the e-commerce domain. (2) Confusing Items: Certain items are so uncommon that the system lacks sufficient information to accurately interpret them. For example, an item referred to as "lying gate" cannot be deciphered or classified solely based on its name without further contextual information. To effectively address these scenarios, the system necessitates augmented e-commerce-specific knowledge for accurate interpretation.
