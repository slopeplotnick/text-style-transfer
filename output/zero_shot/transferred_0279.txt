The study utilized the AI2-THOR dataset in place of the previously employed TEACH dataset. This modification was implemented to ensure reproducibility and facilitate fair algorithm comparisons. Additionally, this change enabled indirect comparisons of performance across similar tasks. However, when evaluating the Long-Term Task Allocation Algorithm (LTAA) against traditional Multi-Robot Task Allocation (MRTA) methods, the researchers maintained the use of the same dataset for consistency. The authors also initiated tests of the SMART-LLM framework with the TEACH dataset to achieve a more balanced comparison.   The study was conducted on a small scale, which allowed a focused examination of fundamental principles and capabilities. Nevertheless, real-world construction scenarios typically involve a significantly larger array of tasks, characterized by intricate temporal and spatial complexities, which were not comprehensively addressed in this research. To maintain generalizability, the authors categorized construction tasks using broad descriptors such as "elemental/compound" and "heavy/dexterous" rather than utilizing highly specific attributes. This approach was necessitated by the absence of an extensive database cataloging real-world construction operations. The authors suggest that the development of such a database would enable testing of the LTAA framework across diverse scenarios with dynamic plans and resource constraints, thereby providing a robust evaluation of the framework's efficacy.  The variability in outputs generated by large language models (LLMs) emerged as a potential concern for safety in construction contexts. Despite repeated validations to mitigate failures, the inherent stochasticity of LLMs, while fostering nuanced reasoning, may lead to inconsistencies in task allocations. Such variability poses challenges for construction sites where precision is paramount, likely necessitating more comprehensive verification mechanisms than those currently in place.  Regarding computational resource requirements, the study did not isolate the cost of SMART-LLM across individual stages, as a unified prompt was utilized for all stages. To enable comparison with LTAA, which focuses solely on task allocation, the researchers estimated SMART-LLM's allocation costs by apportioning the total number of tokens and computation time evenly across three stages. While this estimation provides a baseline, it may lack precision in capturing the specific allocation costs. Future studies incorporating detailed cost analyses are recommended to achieve a more accurate assessment of the computational demands associated with SMART-LLM.
