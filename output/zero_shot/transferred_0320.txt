Enhanced taxonomic frameworks have highlighted the distinct challenges faced by construction sites, particularly when deploying robots equipped with specialized skills for intricate tasks. These challenges distinguish construction from controlled environments where traditional methodologies have been more effective. Construction sites are inherently complex, characterized by fluctuating material flows, unpredictable weather conditions, and intricate interactions among various trades. They necessitate systems capable of navigating spatial constraints, temporal dependencies, and operations where safety is paramount. Furthermore, the oversight required in construction cannot be predetermined or easily programmed. Given these complexities, there is a need for adaptive coordination methods that can accommodate dynamic conditions and disordered environments.  Traditional optimization techniques have endeavored to address these challenges through diverse algorithmic strategies. Established multi-robot coordination approaches are grounded in classical optimization methods, which provide mathematical precision and performance assurances via various computational techniques. The foundational conceptualization for optimal assignment problems was introduced by Kuhn (1955) through the Hungarian method, demonstrating that resource and task matching could be resolved efficiently using linear programming. However, the Hungarian method is restricted to straightforward one-to-one assignments and is unable to accommodate diverse robot skills or task dependencies. In the construction sector, robots possess specialized capabilities—such as heavy lifting or precision assembly—and workflows necessitate coordinated sequences of tasks with explicit dependencies. These limitations led to the development of more sophisticated optimization techniques. Atay and Bayazit (2006) advanced multi-robot task allocation by employing mixed-integer linear programming (MILP), which factors in constraints related to robot skills, task order relationships, and communication limitations. Enhancements in MILP-based coordination were also realized in military and aerospace sectors through advanced techniques that address varying skills and timing constraints. MILP confers significant advantages in modeling complex multi-robot scenarios with explicit satisfaction of constraints. Nonetheless, MILP approaches rely on centralized optimization systems, which encounter scalability issues when managing large teams of robots. The extensive and disordered nature of construction sites, coupled with limited communication capabilities, renders centralized coordination infeasible. Consequently, these scalability challenges have necessitated the adoption of distributed coordination methods.
