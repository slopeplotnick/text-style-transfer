Different models exhibit varying levels of reliability. This review examines how factors such as model size, architecture, and training data influence the occurrence of hallucinations in language models. A study identifies patterns of hallucination across different models and offers potential solutions, such as improved training techniques and output constraints. Furthermore, another study emphasizes the pervasive issue of hallucinations in large language models (LLMs), regardless of their size or training approaches. It posits that judicious selection of models and training regimens can mitigate hallucinations; however, the authors contend that complete elimination of hallucinations remains improbable. This understanding is critical for establishing realistic objectives and evaluating a model's suitability for specific tasks. When selecting an LLM to minimize hallucinations, considerations should include the model's architecture, the quality and diversity of its training data, and strategies such as fine-tuning or the incorporation of supplementary information. At present, numerous models are available, including GPT, BERT, and T5. GPT is known for generating coherent and fluent text, but it is also susceptible to producing fabricated content, or hallucinations. BERT demonstrates a reduced tendency for hallucinations due to its bidirectional training for specific tasks, though it may still invent information. Other models, such as PaLM and LLaMa, have been developed with the aim of minimizing hallucinations, yet their effectiveness is contingent upon their design and training methodologies. Understanding the capabilities and limitations of these models is paramount when selecting a tool for tasks where accuracy is of utmost importance and the reduction of hallucinations is a priority.
