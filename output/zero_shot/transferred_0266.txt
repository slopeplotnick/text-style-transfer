Advanced reinforcement learning (RL) techniques, such as domain randomization and reward shaping, have been employed to address discrepancies between simulated environments and real-world conditions. Tobin et al. (2017) demonstrated that domain randomization enables robots to acquire skills in simulation that can subsequently be applied in real-world settings. Similarly, Grześ and Kudenko (2010) explored reward shaping as a mechanism to accelerate learning processes in multi-agent systems. These approaches have proven effective in enhancing robots’ capacity to adapt to dynamic environmental changes, particularly in construction-related applications. However, both methods require substantial computational resources and careful parameter optimization, presenting challenges in dynamic and resource-constrained environments such as construction sites. Furthermore, a notable limitation of these techniques is the inherent difficulty in interpreting and validating the decision-making processes of the resulting learned policies.  This challenge has driven interest in incorporating game-theoretic approaches, which provide a structured framework for modeling interactions between autonomous agents. For instance, Martin et al. (2023) utilized game theory to optimize task allocation among multiple robots, employing Shapley values to quantify the contributions of individual robots. Their method demonstrated efficacy and provided a detailed analysis of the stability and equity of the task allocation solutions. Nonetheless, the application of game theory in such contexts is limited by its complexity and reliance on the assumption that agents act rationally—a condition that may not hold true in the unpredictable and dynamic environments characteristic of construction. Additionally, game-theoretic models necessitate the definition of precise utility functions, a requirement that is particularly challenging in the context of multifaceted construction tasks.  Despite these advancements, significant limitations persist in applying artificial intelligence (AI) and learning-based methods for construction coordination, particularly with respect to the explainability of decision-making processes. The inherent complexity of deep learning and game-theoretic models poses substantial barriers to understanding how coordination decisions are derived. Such interpretability is crucial in construction management, where decision-makers must ensure the safety and reliability of outcomes. This concern has spurred investigations into the potential application of Large Language Models (LLMs) in robotic coordination. LLMs, capable of reasoning in natural language, offer an unprecedented ability to articulate the rationale behind their decisions, thereby enhancing transparency.  The introduction of LLMs has the potential to transform multi-robot coordination by enabling contextual reasoning, language understanding, and the ability to adapt decisions based on evolving information. Unlike traditional AI methods, which require the explicit definition of all problem parameters, LLMs facilitate more natural and intuitive interactions between humans and robots. Moreover, their capacity to generate human-readable explanations fosters greater clarity in decision-making processes. As such, LLMs represent a promising advancement in addressing the challenges of explainability and adaptability in construction robot coordination.
