In constructing narratives, individuals may begin with an event and subsequently determine the characteristics and development of the entities—be they people or objects—associated with that event. Alternatively, they may start with a particular person or object and create a sequence of events around this focal point. E2, a doctoral candidate in literature, posits that narratives can be deconstructed into discrete components, including outlines, sequences, ideas, and fundamental linguistic elements. By assembling these elements in reverse order, one can generate a cohesive narrative. Specifically, basic ideas may consist of elements such as individuals, actions, or attributes. Several ideas can be organized into a sequence, and sequences can be combined to form a complete narrative. However, merely assembling these components does not inherently result in a well-constructed story. The connective elements, referred to as "relationships," serve a critical function in integrating these components into a coherent and engaging narrative.  Interviews with two subject-matter experts revealed four primary components of storytelling: entities (e.g., people or objects), events, relationships, and outlines. Through discussions with these experts, it became evident that each component can possess varying interpretations depending on the context. Among these components, events are central to narrative construction; however, the specific details of events may not always be explicitly articulated within the narrative structure. While events can exist independently within a story, their interplay establishes the tension and meaning essential to storytelling. Furthermore, the entities within the story, along with the relationships between them, exert a significant influence on the nature and progression of events. The attributes and relational dynamics associated with these entities can also be analyzed independently to further elucidate their role within the narrative framework.
