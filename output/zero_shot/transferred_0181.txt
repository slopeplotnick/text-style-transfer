In this study, we aim to demonstrate the capability of Large Language Models (LLMs) in addressing complex optimization problems. Specifically, we investigate the efficacy of LLMs in resource allocation by employing dynamic prompts. Furthermore, we conduct a comparative analysis of LLM performance against established algorithms such as Deep Q-Network (DQN) and Q-learning. The proposed framework accomplishes task decomposition and optimizes resource allocation through the strategies elucidated by the SMART-LLM framework (Kannan et al., 2024). The framework employs two main strategies: the first focuses on organizing tasks based on the involved objects and the necessary actions, while the second emphasizes required skills, such as precision handling or exerting force. These subdivided tasks are subsequently delegated to various robots according to their capabilities and task demands.   The LTAA framework utilizes LangGraph for organizational purposes, prioritizing task completion by assessing the likelihood of success for each robot assigned to specific tasks. Initially, an imbalance was observed, with certain robots being overburdened and others underutilized, prompting the development of novel strategies to dynamically adjust priorities as the project progresses. These strategies initially prioritized success rates and subsequently ensured equitable task distribution.  The developmental process encompassed three phases: initially, the SMART-LLM framework was tested for Multi-Robot Task Allocation (MRTA), revealing issues related to validation and reliability. Addressing these challenges, a Self-Corrective Agent Architecture was implemented, incorporating multi-stage validation and retry mechanisms. Ultimately, the LTAA framework integrated these concepts with novel allocation strategies to facilitate comparison with traditional optimization methods.  The authors delineate the establishment of a conventional LLM-based MRTA framework, namely SMART-LLM (Kannan et al., 2024), and the principal challenges encountered, such as validation and coordination issues. These obstacles catalyzed the development of a phase-adaptive, self-corrective architecture employing LangGraph, characterized by multi-stage validation and retry mechanisms. These innovations culminated in the LTAA framework. Additionally, the paper outlines performance evaluation against traditional optimization techniques, utilizing the TEACH dataset for direct comparison with algorithms such as Q-learning, DQN, and Dynamic Programming (DP) under consistent task conditions. Performance metrics include task balancing, feature-based task execution, and reasoning quality, assessing both allocation efficiency and task performance outcomes.
