This study addresses the issue of quantifying the extent to which a reasoning model adheres to its initial beliefs by developing a novel metric termed the "Martingale Score." A reasoning model is considered overly fixed in its beliefs if it fails to comply with the Martingale property, a fundamental concept in Bayesian reasoning. According to this principle, it should not be possible to predict changes in beliefs prior to their occurrence. If future beliefs of a model can be anticipated solely based on its past beliefs, this indicates a deviation from the Martingale property, suggesting an excessive adherence to initial beliefs.  Our research involved experimental evaluations across three domains: event prediction, subjective question answering, and academic paper review. We examined various models and reasoning methodologies, discovering that models frequently exhibit a tendency to adhere excessively to their initial beliefs. Our experiments demonstrated that future beliefs were often predictable from past beliefs, contrary to the expectations set by the Martingale property. Furthermore, we established that deviations from this property were correlated with decreased accuracy in model predictions.  The Martingale Score effectively quantifies the predictability of a model's belief updates based on its prior beliefs. Utilizing this metric, we evaluated several prevalent models, finding a substantial prevalence of excessive adherence to initial beliefs across diverse domains, prompts, and model architectures.
