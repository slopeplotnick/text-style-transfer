A journal entry in accounting constitutes a fundamental record of a financial transaction, typically comprising both debit and credit entries across one or more accounts. Each journal entry is detailed with specific attributes, including the transaction date, identification number, currency denomination, author of the entry, applicable tax rates, amounts involved, and occasionally, a descriptive account of the transaction. These attributes, which encompass numerical, categorical, and textual data, render journal entries an effective medium for evaluating systems designed to detect anomalous patterns in heterogeneous datasets. Notably, both synthetic and anonymized datasets retain these core characteristics, thereby facilitating their use in the consistent development and testing of analytical models.  In recent advancements, researchers have increasingly employed machine learning (ML) techniques to enhance the detection of atypical or suspicious entries within accounting data. Unsupervised ML methodologies, such as clustering algorithms and the Isolation Forest technique, have demonstrated efficacy in scrutinizing journal entries to identify deviations from normative patterns (Herreros-Martinez et al., 2025). Empirical investigations utilizing synthetic general ledger datasets indicate that these techniques may surpass traditional rule-based systems in efficacy (Gronewald et al., 2024). Nevertheless, significant challenges persist: conventional ML models typically operate on structured data and may lack contextual understanding necessary to discern genuine risk from mere anomalies.  Conversely, Large Language Models (LLMs), exemplified by GPT-4, have introduced novel possibilities within the auditing domain. These models excel in comprehending and reasoning with textual data, and preliminary research suggests their potential utility as AI assistants in the analysis of financial data (Gu et al., 2023). Recent studies indicate that LLMs can also assist in detecting anomalous patterns, as evidenced by the application of models such as SigLLM for time-series data (Alnegheimish et al., 2024) and AnoLLM for tabular data (Tsai et al., 2025). These models demonstrate robust performance without necessitating extensive data preprocessing. However, the deployment of LLMs in critical tasks like auditing must be approached with caution. LLMs are susceptible to generating inconsistent responses, exhibiting biases, or fabricating information. Consequently, efforts have been initiated to develop tools for verifying the outputs of these models, such as AuditLLM (Amirizaniani et al., 2024a) and LLMAuditor (Amirizaniani et al., 2024b). These verification tools underscore the substantial utility of LLMs while emphasizing the necessity for stringent regulatory frameworks and oversight when employing them in decision-making processes within auditing and related fields.
