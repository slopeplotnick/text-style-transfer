Training effective reward models presents significant challenges due to their inherent subjectivity and situational dependence, as well as the susceptibility of learned proxies to manipulation. The scarcity of annotations further complicates the model's capacity to address intricate behaviors. In response to these challenges, various methodologies have been explored to reduce reliance on annotations. Direct Preference Optimization, for instance, does not utilize explicit reward models but still requires preference data. Verifiable rewards depend on actual outcomes but are effective only when these outcomes can be automatically verified. Self-evaluation approaches face issues related to prompt sensitivity and biases. Despite these efforts, these methods still depend on external signals for quality assessment, often neglecting the information contained within the model's internal representations.  The authenticity of a generated output should be reflected in its internal processing. For example, when a model generates the statement "The capital of France is Paris," the hidden states reveal factual knowledge, whereas fabrications exhibit different patterns. This observation prompts the question: Can text quality be evaluated using internal signals, thereby reducing dependence on external assistance for aligning large language models (LLMs)?  Our findings demonstrate that a simple geometric feature of LLM hidden states, known as effective dimensionality, serves as a reliable indicator of LLM response quality. We propose the use of stable rank, a measure derived from matrix theory, as an unsupervised means of assessing generation quality. Stable rank evaluates the number of independent semantic directions employed in a response by examining the ratio of total variance to the primary direction variance within the hidden-state representation. This measure strikes a balance between retaining rich information and maintaining coherence; a high stable rank indicates that information is distributed across numerous dimensions rather than concentrated in a few.  The theoretical foundation for this approach is rooted in two areas of study. First, the softmax bottleneck analysis suggests that accurately modeling language necessitates engagement with a high-dimensional semantic space, given the inherent complexity of the probability distribution of natural language.
