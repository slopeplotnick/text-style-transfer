Previous studies have demonstrated that the performance of smaller language models does not always degrade linearly as model size decreases, a finding that is corroborated by the present research. In this study, we observed that a smaller model, MentalQLM (0.5 billion parameters), exhibited strong predictive capabilities in identifying mental health conditions such as depression, stress, and suicidal ideation. These findings suggest that smaller, fine-tuned models can effectively balance computational efficiency with predictive accuracy in the context of mental health assessments. Consistent with prior research, it was further observed that fine-tuned models retain reasoning and question-answering capacities, enabling them to perform well across a range of tasks, including mental health prediction.   Our empirical evaluations indicate that models with 3–7 billion parameters achieve an optimal balance between efficiency and accuracy when fine-tuned. In contrast, smaller models frequently exhibit limitations in task adaptability, whereas models within this parameter range demonstrate robust performance in classifying psychological signals under both zero-shot and few-shot conditions. Fine-tuning methods applied to our model, referred to as Menta, demonstrate the establishment of a lower bound for model size that ensures reliable performance in complex mental health prediction tasks while maintaining computational efficiency. Moreover, a comparison between smaller fine-tuned models and larger counterparts revealed that the smaller models performed comparably, or even superiorly, in predicting depression and stress. However, their performance in predicting suicidal ideation was comparatively weaker. Nonetheless, fine-tuning significantly enhances the performance of smaller models compared to their non-fine-tuned equivalents, achieving greater task adaptability and balanced performance across various prediction tasks.   These findings highlight the potential of well-optimized smaller models to retain the capacity for reasoning and contextual understanding required for accurate mental health predictions. Mental health tasks often rely on specific linguistic markers, such as self-referential language, emotional expression, and temporal references, which smaller models can sufficiently capture without requiring the extensive general knowledge characteristic of larger models. Previous research has demonstrated that fine-tuning models on task-specific datasets effectively reduces the performance disparity between smaller and larger models. The present study builds upon this body of work by showing that efficient fine-tuning techniques, such as LoRA, paired with specialized loss functions, enable smaller models to acquire task-specific knowledge for mental health predictions while minimizing computational resource requirements.
