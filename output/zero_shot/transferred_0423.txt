The model utilized in this study was trained on both single-speaker inputs and multi-speaker conversations. In the given context, inputs often combined content from multiple individuals without explicit demarcation, thereby introducing ambiguity and increasing the complexity of interpretation. This scenario deviates significantly from the model’s standard training distribution. Such discrepancies, along with the extended input length, can lead to the model disproportionately prioritizing early salient segments of the text, subsequently compromising its ability to comprehend the broader context due to challenges associated with maintaining stability over long sequences.   Nevertheless, through the application of domain-specific optimization and carefully tailored adjustments, our fine-tuned *Menta* model demonstrated robust performance in a variety of mental health tasks, including stress detection, depression severity estimation, and assessments of suicidal ideation and risk. Notably, *Menta* exhibited effectiveness in addressing diverse mental health outcomes. Despite its relatively smaller size, the model performed competitively against larger models, particularly excelling in depression and stress-related tasks, sometimes surpassing these larger counterparts. These findings underscore the potential of well-optimized, compact models to serve as an efficient solution for continuous mental health monitoring, offering a cost-effective yet high-performing approach for early detection within digital mental health initiatives.  This research addresses three primary questions regarding the efficacy of smaller language models, including our fine-tuned *Menta* model, in predicting mental health outcomes. It also examines the inherent limitations of current approaches and proposes potential avenues for future research. One critical question is whether smaller models can effectively predict mental health conditions. The results indicate that such models can indeed produce accurate predictions of mental health outcomes when trained on social media text data. Although these smaller models have fewer parameters, the optimized *Menta* model demonstrated proficiency in handling complex tasks, highlighting their utility in resource-constrained settings.   While prior research, such as Jia et al. (2025), explored the capabilities of smaller language models for mental health applications with a focus on those in the 2–7 billion parameter range, our study concentrated on even smaller models, specifically those below the 7-billion-parameter threshold. The results revealed that models with fewer than approximately 3.8 billion parameters (e.g., those in the range of 1 billion parameters) underperformed, often producing blank or erratic outputs in zero- or few-shot learning scenarios. This suggests that reducing model size below a certain threshold leads to a substantial drop in performance, as opposed to a gradual degradation. Our findings identify this lower performance boundary specifically within the domain of mental health prediction, contributing to a more nuanced understanding of the limitations and potential of small-scale language models in this context.
