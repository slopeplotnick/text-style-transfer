When data features lack clearly defined conceptual boundaries, researchers commonly employ probing techniques to identify neurons or groups of neurons that most effectively represent target concepts. These probes are typically linear but may adopt nonlinear forms when the target concepts are not linearly separable. Probes can be trained using two distinct approaches: supervised learning, which relies on labeled data corresponding to the desired concept, and unsupervised learning, which identifies concepts relevant to the model, albeit not necessarily interpretable to humans.   This study advances beyond observational methods by incorporating structural modeling through equations. It explores varying levels of modeling fidelity, ranging from Fully Specified Causal Models (FSCMs), which are designed to align with domain-specific knowledge for interpretability, to Partially Specified Causal Models (PSCMs), which encompass all FSCMs compatible with the given data. This underscores the significance of selecting appropriate modeling frameworks and highlights the utility of partial information when fully specified causal models are unavailable.   Galhotra et al. (2021) introduced an explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) methodology leveraging FSCMs to compute probabilistic measures of sufficiency and necessity. Their approach primarily targets tabular data and integrates causal modeling into data features by presuming these features represent high-level concepts, thereby facilitating the inclusion of additional concepts outside the model’s input specification. Notably, this technique is contingent on the assumption that the features correspond to meaningful concepts and operates independently of a causal data generator. Furthermore, the method posits a preference for explanatory models that illustrate how specific input values can modify predictions to achieve preferred outcomes or maintain favorable predictions when stability in the output is desired.
