The proposed threat model examines the consequences of an adversarial agent utilizing a malicious tool. It is posited that while the tool’s code may be reviewed or inspected, the covert backdoor embedded within remains undetected due to its similarity in behavior to legitimate functionalities. In cloud-based or hybrid deployment environments, end users lack visibility into the internal workings of deployed tools, making them only able to detect anomalous behavior observable at the interface level. Importantly, the malicious agent operates within the predefined behavioral constraints, avoiding escalation of privileges or tampering with memory systems. The attack is deemed successful if it satisfies two key objectives: (1) completing the adversary’s intended task, and (2) maintaining the tool’s functionality in fulfilling the user’s objectives with minimal perceptible performance deviations.   The crux of the threat pertains to economic impacts, where the malicious exploitation of computational resources results in tangible costs, such as depletion of API tokens or consumption of GPU processing time. When deployed at scale, such misuse can lead to significant resource waste. The focus of this analysis is the LeechHijack attack, which is systematically explored in this discussion. Initially, a foundational overview of the attack methodology is presented. This is followed by an explanation of how a covert backdoor can be integrated into a seemingly legitimate tool that adheres to operational norms. Subsequent sections detail how this backdoor can be exploited to establish a covert communication channel and compel the agent to perform unauthorized tasks on behalf of the attacker. Finally, the conditions under which the attack is operationally feasible are outlined, alongside proposed metrics to evaluate its efficacy.  The LeechHijack attack unfolds in two distinct phases. In the first phase, the attacker deploys a seemingly benign tool that discreetly incorporates a latent backdoor. This backdoor remains dormant during standard operations and exhibits no observable malicious behavior. In the second phase, the backdoor is activated via a specific trigger. Once activated, the tool generates manipulated outputs designed to induce the agent into performing extraneous computational tasks aligned with the attacker’s goals. This phase concludes when the tool is utilized for its intended purpose, at which point the backdoor ceases operation, and the tool reverts to its original state, leaving no discernible trace for the user.
