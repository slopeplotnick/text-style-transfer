The challenges presented by climate change and the transition to new energy sources are multifaceted and extend beyond the capacity of any single scientific discipline. However, there remains a notable gap in higher education programs, such as the absence of specialized degrees in Mineral Exploration. Concurrently, the rapid proliferation of information, facilitated by advanced algorithms that can synthesize and analyze vast amounts of data almost instantly, has transformed the ways in which knowledge is accessed and applied. Despite these advancements, there is a decline in the number of individuals in Western countries pursuing studies in economic geology and mining engineering. This trend underscores the critical importance of preserving traditional geological knowledge—particularly regarding the formation of ore deposits—which is often archived in older geological reports scattered globally.  The need for centralized, high-quality geological databases is evident. Countries such as Canada and Australia have developed exemplary models for such repositories; however, this infrastructure remains largely absent in many other regions. For instance, in Africa, only a single country—Zambia—has established an accessible geological database (gsd.gov.zm). Developing and maintaining online, publicly accessible geological information is essential to attract investment and support more sustainable mineral exploration efforts.  This discussion advocates for enhanced collaboration between scientists and industry stakeholders to develop innovative approaches to mineral exploration. As exploration increasingly targets deposits hidden beneath surface cover, traditional methods that begin with surface outcrop observations or rely heavily on deterministic geophysical inversion have become less effective, as evidenced by waning discovery rates. To address these limitations, greater emphasis should be placed on making risk-informed decisions that explicitly account for uncertainty. Reducing the frequency of erroneous drilling decisions necessitates the acknowledgement that current understanding of orebody formations is incomplete, as quantified through epistemic uncertainty.  Furthermore, there is a pressing need to improve the integration and application of geostatistical methods to analyze data at appropriate spatial and temporal scales. The lack of such methodologies currently contributes to the prevalence of false-positive results in mineral exploration. By addressing these challenges, the field can advance toward more efficient, reliable, and sustainable exploration practices.
