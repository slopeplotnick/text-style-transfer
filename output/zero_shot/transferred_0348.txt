To assess the preservation of the individual's identity in the edited video, we compute the average similarity between facial features from each frame and the initial edited frame using a pre-trained ArcFace model. This metric indicates the extent to which the individual's appearance and facial characteristics are preserved during the editing process, with higher values signifying superior identity consistency. A user study further demonstrated a preference for our method in terms of visual quality and temporal alignment. Detailed information about the study and metric calculations is provided in the supplementary materials.  Analysis of the results in Table 1 reveals that text-based methods such as LucyEdit excel in certain synchronization metrics (e.g., Speech Correlation of 0.80 compared to our 0.72), yet they fall short in implementing the intended edits, as evidenced by their low Directional CLIP score of 0.11 versus our 0.21. Our approach offers a more balanced performance, achieving effective synchronization while accurately applying visual edits. In contrast, VACE employs additional conditioning factors like pose, canny, and depth to enhance CLIP alignment, but it struggles with maintaining temporal consistency and identity preservation. Sync-LoRA, which is specifically trained for synchronized editing, achieves robust temporal alignment while executing precise edits that preserve identity, demonstrating our method's ability to balance motion consistency with edit accuracy.  Figure 6 presents ablation tests where we systematically omit one synchronization factor at a time. Models are trained on datasets excluding one factor—such as speech, gaze, blinking, or pose—by setting its weight to zero in our filtering score. For instance, in the initial test (top two rows), omitting pose results in noticeable errors in head orientation. In the subsequent test (bottom two rows), omitting any factor leads to issues such as spatial drift or misalignment in gaze or blinking. The exclusion of any factor significantly deteriorates synchronization, particularly affecting the male subject's mouth movements and the female subject's eye motions, underscoring the necessity of all four factors in delivering comprehensive motion information.
