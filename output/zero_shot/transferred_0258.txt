In conventional practice, the process typically begins with performing a deterministic inversion followed by a corresponding deterministic interpretation, as illustrated in Figure 2. Subsequently, a knowledgeable geologist examines the deterministic inversion to identify relevant geological structures, such as intrusions, that are significant for exploration purposes. This interpretation is facilitated by software that emphasizes a single deterministic model. Within this framework, geophysical inversion precedes geological interpretation. Although some may argue that regularization terms incorporate geological insights, this is ambiguous and minimally effective. It appears as though geological knowledge is redundantly utilized: initially in the regularization term and subsequently during interpretation, resulting in potentially circular reasoning.  In contrast, Bayesian approaches integrate inversion and interpretation into a singular problem. Bayesian methodology necessitates consideration of potential solutions, or geometries, prior to any inversion activities. Bayesian inversion provides relatively straightforward, easily implementable solutions that have general applicability. Revisiting Figure 2, we explore the establishment of a prior probability model. The initial inquiry pertains to the minimal knowledge available about the subsurface before acquiring any data. Notably, past observations have established that petrophysical properties fall within certain ranges; familiarity with rock types can further refine these ranges. Additionally, it is known that these properties do not exhibit random variation nor remain constant over extensive areas; rather, their distribution is influenced by specific depositional processes. Essentially, the foundational information comprises constraints on histograms and spatial correlation patterns. Mathematically, this information can be conceptualized into a spatial covariance model (or variogram in geostatistics), although there is uncertainty regarding parameters such as the mean, ranges, and anisotropy, which are not arbitrary but rather variable. This conceptualization promotes a layered approach to understanding these phenomena.
