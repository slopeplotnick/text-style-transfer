Engineers have increasingly implemented systems wherein supervisory entities act as intermediaries between human operators and autonomous worker agents. These supervisors are tasked with evaluating the agents' outputs, identifying errors, and intervening when operational thresholds are exceeded. This concept falls within the broader framework of "Institutional AI," which will be elaborated upon subsequently. However, the mere presence of supervisors does not suffice to ensure robust oversight. Supervisors often utilize systems analogous to those employed by the agents they monitor, exposing them to comparable vulnerabilities or recursive failure patterns. Excessive consensus among supervisors can further exacerbate the situation by fostering collective errors or even deliberate malfeasance.   To enhance system governance, it is necessary to establish additional layers of supervision, employ diverse verification mechanisms, adopt redundant observation methods, and ensure a clear division of authority between supervisory entities. As systems comprising multiple autonomous agents evolve into more complex, self-regulating networks, ensuring safety becomes an increasingly intricate challenge. Reliance on centralized control structures or static operational rules is no longer sufficient. Instead, decentralized networks must be developed to autonomously detect, address, and rectify operational anomalies. Failure within one model has the potential to propagate across interconnected components, underscoring the importance of understanding and mitigating collective vulnerabilities.  To safeguard AI systems, it is imperative to investigate the origins of group-level errors and develop metrics for their assessment. The interaction between language models demonstrates the limitations of prevailing approaches for addressing risks, including bias, misuse, and system weaknesses. While individual models can be tested in isolation, their interactions give rise to emergent behaviors that may result in failure modes not yet fully understood. In response to this challenge, the concept of the Emergent Systemic Risk Horizon (ESRH) has been proposed. The ESRH delineates the critical threshold at which the collective behavior of interconnected models begins to deteriorate, despite the individual reliability of each constituent model.
