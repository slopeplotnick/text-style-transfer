In the analysis of the three models, it is evident that the impact of youth on both imagery and predictive outcomes remains consistent, thereby substantiating its efficacy as a reliable measure across varying levels of detail. However, it is crucial to note that explanation models, which compartmentalize variables without accounting for their interdependencies, primarily illustrate the direct effect. This direct effect may correspond to the total effect in the initial model but may be negligible in the final model. Consequently, reliance on direct influence as a metric can be misleading. Section 5.3.1 addresses the challenges posed by incorrect model assumptions, advocating for judicious model selection. When assumptions introduce bias in explanations, it is imperative to either modify the model or equip the explainer with an understanding of the biased context in which these explanations are applicable. This does not imply that complex models inherently surpass simpler ones in effectiveness. To ensure that explanations resonate with the intended comprehension, the causal model should align with the individual's understanding of the world. Simplification is vital; hence, condensing detailed causal mechanisms into comprehensible formats is preferred to enhance clarity. Additionally, a notable advantage of the causal explanation model discussed in Section 5.3.1 is its utilization of expert knowledge to identify and eliminate spurious correlations more effectively than data-centric models. This capacity enables the explanation model to detect false relationships assimilated by the predictor, even when the same dataset is employed in developing the explanation model. With the increasing accessibility of expert knowledge through large language models (LLMs), as demonstrated within the example provided, this approach is particularly beneficial for refining models by identifying unintended behaviors.
