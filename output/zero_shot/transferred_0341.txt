With the exception of Study 1, the synthetic datasets replicated the factor structure of their corresponding scales. Moreover, M-CFA analyses indicated that both synthetic and genuine participants exhibited comparable patterns of responses to the scales. These findings align with prior research examining group-level synthetic data. Notably, Studies 3 and 4 demonstrated higher levels of measurement consistency between datasets compared to Studies 1 and 2, suggesting that scales derived from synthetic samples may function more effectively with real-world data than vice versa. One interpretation of this observation is that large language models (LLMs) may process survey items in a more standardized manner, less influenced by random variability or biases associated with study conditions or characteristics of the sample population. Consequently, factor structures generated from synthetic data may generalize more effectively across diverse samples than those derived from real-world datasets.   Future research is needed to investigate the factors influencing the ways in which LLMs and human participants interpret and respond to survey items. Although confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) yields promising results, synthetic datasets fail to replicate the pairwise correlations, distribution properties, and variance observed in genuine samples. These findings highlight that, despite the equivalence in factor structures, the score distributions of synthetic and real-world datasets differ significantly across all examined domains. Therefore, while group-level data generated synthetically can provide reliable structures for preliminary or pilot testing, individual-level synthetic data are inadequate for such purposes. These limitations are consistent with findings reported by Petrov et al. Importantly, while Studies 2, 3, and 4 exhibit similar results, Study 1 presents notable discrepancies. This divergence may be attributable to several factors: first, the factor structure for this scale was derived using a distinct extraction method, specifically minres, which may have influenced participant responses; second, this scale employed a 5-point Likert response format, whereas the other scales utilized 6-point or 7-point Likert formats.
