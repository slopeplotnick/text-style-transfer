To address the significant challenge of ensuring factual accuracy in information, researchers have developed two primary methodologies: Fact Verification (FV) and Hallucination Detection (HD). Fact Verification represents an established field of study that aims to evaluate the truthfulness of statements by corroborating them with external sources, such as web pages or structured databases. This process is agnostic to the origin of the text, whether authored by humans or generated by machines, and seeks to determine its factual correctness based on available evidence.  In contrast, Hallucination Detection is a more nascent area of research that has emerged alongside the development and proliferation of large language models (LLMs). HD is specifically concerned with identifying fabrications or inaccuracies within content generated by these models. Unlike FV, which focuses primarily on validating statements as independent textual units, HD incorporates an analysis of the generative processes underlying the text. While HD may utilize external information sources similarly to FV, its primary emphasis lies in leveraging internal model signals, such as token-level confidence scores, activation patterns, and consistency across multiple model outputs. Consequently, HD offers the potential to detect hallucinated content even in the absence of external evidence.  Although both FV and HD share the overarching goal of identifying factual inaccuracies, their research trajectories have largely diverged, utilizing distinct experimental frameworks, evaluation metrics, and dissemination outlets. This divergence stems from fundamental differences in their evaluative approaches. FV, for instance, focuses exclusively on textual content and often employs benchmarks such as the FEVER dataset, where claims are assessed as standalone textual entities. HD, on the other hand, prioritizes the analysis of the generative characteristics and internal mechanics of LLMs.
