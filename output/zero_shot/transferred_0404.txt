The identification of hate speech in low-resource languages presents significant challenges due to the limited availability of datasets and the inherent linguistic variability. Bangla, spoken by over 230 million individuals in Bangladesh and India, particularly in West Bengal, exemplifies such a language. Despite the expanding requirement for automated moderation on social media platforms, computational resources for Bangla remain insufficient. This study examines Subtask 1A and Subtask 1B of the BLP 2025 Shared Task focused on hate speech detection. We replicated official baselines, such as Majority Class, Random, and Support Vector Machine, and explored Logistic Regression, Random Forest, and Decision Tree as additional baseline methods. Additionally, we employed transformer-based models, including DistilBERT, BanglaBERT, m-BERT, and XLM-RoBERTa, for hate speech classification. Our findings indicate that all transformer models, except DistilBERT, outperformed the baseline methods across the subtasks, with BanglaBERT demonstrating superior performance in both. Despite its smaller size, BanglaBERT surpassed m-BERT and XLM-RoBERTa, underscoring the importance of language-specific training. These results highlight the potential and necessity for pretrained language models tailored to Bangla, a language with limited computational resources.  The proliferation of internet access and the consequent rise in social media usage have become pervasive globally over the past few decades. Enhanced connectivity facilitates the exchange of opinions and information online, with social media increasingly integral to public discourse. However, this increased usage also provides a platform for the dissemination of hate speech. The rapid surge of toxic and harmful content, such as hate speech, poses significant safety concerns on these digital platforms. Hate speech encompasses language that is harmful, offensive, or derogatory toward individuals or groups. Consequently, the automatic detection of hate speech is a critical task in Natural Language Processing (NLP). While substantial advances have been achieved in detecting hate speech in resource-rich languages like English, progress remains constrained in resource-poor languages such as Bangla, primarily due to the scarcity of high-quality annotated datasets and the complexity of the language itself.
