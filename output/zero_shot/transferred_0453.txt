The findings demonstrate that the application of reasoning to enhance safety alignment significantly improves the efficacy of omnimodal guardrails across a diverse array of input types. This approach facilitates a more profound comprehension and generalization in complex safety scenarios where reliance solely on labels proves insufficient. To ascertain whether safety knowledge acquired from one form of input is transferable to another, a series of experiments were conducted. In each experiment, the OMNIGUARD system was trained using data from three distinct input types, with subsequent testing performed on the same types, excluding one type to evaluate performance on unfamiliar data. The results, comprising averaged F1 scores and accuracy metrics, are presented in Table 5.   Two primary insights emerged from this analysis. Firstly, OMNIGUARD exhibits robust capability in transferring acquired knowledge from one type of input to another. The model’s performance on novel input types closely approximates that on the originally trained types, with an average F1 score of 79.4 compared to 81.8. This indicates OMNIGUARD’s proficiency in discerning harmful patterns across text, images, videos, and audio, thereby formulating universally applicable safety protocols.  Secondly, an exception was noted concerning audio input. The F1 score for audio on which the model was trained was marginally lower (64.6%) compared to audio on which it was not trained (65.1%). This discrepancy arose due to the model's underperformance during the WildGuard-TTS test—a text-to-speech-derived audio version—when trained without text data. The absence of text data during training impeded the model’s ability to effectively process audio, suggesting that the interplay between various forms of content, such as text and speech, significantly influences the learning and application of safety rules. Moreover, it underscores the potential for knowledge derived from one modality to be applied to another modality conveying equivalent information.
