This study explores the extraction of meaningful information from images using advanced multimodal language models. These models integrate various data types, aligning them with specific knowledge systems to construct interpretable knowledge graphs. Additionally, we propose a method to evaluate the correspondence between images generated by models and the original training images, thereby facilitating an understanding of their similarities. This approach is particularly beneficial for determining ownership, enhancing interpretability, and ensuring data provenance, highlighting its potential to improve traceability in generative models.  Our methodology has been tested on both locally trained models and large-scale models through a specialized experiment focusing on popular Ghibli-style images. This experiment aims to assess the method's effectiveness in identifying stylistic influences, even when model internals are inaccessible. The structure of this paper is as follows: Section 2 reviews related work on the understanding of generative models and the construction of knowledge graphs using advanced language models. Section 3 details our proposed methodology. Section 4 presents the experiments and results obtained from both local and large-scale models. Finally, Section 5 concludes with a summary of key findings and directions for future research.  As generative models continue to advance, understanding them is increasingly important in technical, ethical, and policy contexts. While general methods such as t-SNE, LIME, and attention visualization are useful for illustrating model operations, they do not directly address the provenance of training data, a critical issue in fields like authorship and copyright. Various techniques have been proposed to examine the impact of training data on model outputs. Initial approaches, including influence functions, retraining-based attribution, and Shapley value approximations, have proven useful in well-defined areas such as classification tasks. However, these methods are often impractical for large-scale models due to their reliance on gradients, retraining access, or extensive computational resources. Consequently, they are not widely adopted in contemporary deep learning frameworks, particularly for generative models where internal inspection is not feasible.
