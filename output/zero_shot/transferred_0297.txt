In the context of Experiment 2, it was observed that the utilization of a singular system prompt enhanced performance across all tested languages, indicating a systematic alteration in the model's behavior. To elucidate the changes in the model's outputs, an analysis of reasoning steps was conducted, examining the correlation between modifications in reasoning and performance across various languages. An inquiry was made into whether improved prompts altered the model's language preference. Initial investigations focused on the languages employed during the model's reasoning processes. Figure 7 illustrates that even with identical random prompts, the model exhibited differential language preferences contingent upon the language of the query. These preferences correspond with the availability of training data for each language. For instance, owing to the substantial amount of Chinese data in model families such as Qwen, the model responds to Chinese inquiries in Chinese nearly as frequently as it does in English. Upon enhancement of the prompts, these language preferences became more pronounced; the model predominantly responds in the language of the query rather than defaulting to English, as was the case with the system prompt. However, for languages with comparatively less training data, such as Hindi, the proportion of responses in English remains relatively unchanged despite improved prompts. Furthermore, it was determined that languages apart from the task language and English are largely unhelpful to the model, as they nearly vanish following prompt optimization. Additional information regarding language identification can be found in Appendix A.9, with further data provided in Tables 6 and 7. Figure 8 provides an analysis of reasoning behavior types across different languages. Although overarching trends are predominantly consistent, certain languages demonstrate specific preferences: English tends towards reframing, Chinese towards logical reasoning, and Spanish towards calculation. Conversely, languages with limited training data, such as Hindi, show a higher prevalence of the 'Other' category, suggesting a deficiency in structured reasoning practice during training. While these trends are consistent, they appear to be less influenced by prompt design and more by language-specific patterns inherent in model pretraining.
