This paper addresses a significant issue and proposes a novel conceptual framework for understanding safety within interconnected systems. Rather than viewing safety as an attribute confined to individual models, it should be understood as an emergent property resulting from the interactions between multiple systems. The paper introduces the concept of the Emergent Systemic Risk Horizon (ESRH) to elucidate the conditions under which systems may become destabilized. The discussion progresses methodically: beginning with the interaction of dyadic entities, advancing to the collaborative dynamics of multiple agents, examining various forms of risk, and ultimately proposing a design paradigm termed Institutional AI to manage these risks effectively.  Currently, the safety of language models is predominantly ensured by concentrating on human-model interactions, employing mechanisms such as prompts and filters to obstruct harmful content and ensure adherence to guidelines. This approach operates under the assumption that a model's behavior is transparent and relatively stable. However, language models are inherently complex, influenced by factors such as their internal state, historical interactions, and contextual deployment. Minor contextual variations can precipitate divergent outcomes, despite apparent compliance with established norms. This complexity is exacerbated in scenarios where models interact with one another, creating chains wherein the output of one model serves as the input for another, potentially generating feedback loops that amplify minor discrepancies.  Empirical assessments, such as JAILJUDGE, illustrate that the outcomes of interacting models can significantly deviate from those observed in isolated model evaluations. Further experiments, including those conducted in simulated market environments, demonstrate that individual models designed to operate equitably can collectively produce inequitable outcomes. This underscores the fallacy of assuming systemic safety based solely on the satisfactory performance of individual components. A paradigmatic shift is required, moving the focus from isolated prompts or actions to a holistic consideration of systemic communication and interaction.
