Language models present significant advantages over traditional algorithmic models due to their superior predictive capabilities and user-friendly nature, as well as their ability to simultaneously manage multiple tasks. Small Language Models (SLMs) emerge as a pragmatic alternative to Large Language Models (LLMs), characterized by their efficient operation through techniques such as quantization, knowledge distillation, and lightweight architectural designs. SLMs are adept at performing functions such as text classification, information summarization, and question answering on resource-constrained devices, including smartphones and embedded systems. They are a viable option compared to LLMs, offering reduced computational demands while maintaining robust performance in specific tasks.   Empirical research indicates that in data-scarce environments, smaller models can match or surpass their larger counterparts in specialized domains such as text classification and healthcare. This capability renders SLMs particularly suitable for identifying mental health issues on social media platforms. However, previous studies predominantly focused on single tasks or specific domains and did not address the integration of various mental health issues such as depression, stress, and suicidality. In contrast to earlier LLMs, which are burdened with excessive parameters unsuitable for resource-constrained or privacy-sensitive environments, SLMs prioritize usability, with rapid response times, minimal memory consumption, and cost-effectiveness.  Prior research has conducted comparative analyses of several SLMs and LLMs on diverse mental health tasks utilizing social media data, demonstrating that few-shot prompting significantly enhances SLM performance. Kim et al. developed an SLM named mhGPT, which outperforms larger models such as MentaLLaMA and Gemma in mental health-related tasks, despite having fewer parameters and utilizing less data. Further studies propose that SLMs can be implemented in clinical settings for structured tasks beyond mere issue detection. Nevertheless, these studies often exhibit imbalanced task allocation and lack optimization through efficient fine-tuning, typically employing either minimal prompting or comprehensive fine-tuning approaches.
