This study examines the capacity of a computational model designed to simulate human language acquisition to independently discern grammatical structures and lexical categories through interaction with a pre-existing language model, referred to as Delilah, which provides exemplars of adult language use. Upon internalizing these grammatical rules, the model, termed the "daughter model," is capable of engaging in dialogue with another computational entity known as the "mother model." The daughter model possesses the ability to generate novel sentences based on the acquired grammar and communicate these constructs to the mother model within the context of their interaction. Subject to predefined parameters, the mother model may deliver evaluative feedback regarding the grammatical accuracy, which the daughter model can then utilize for further refinement of its linguistic competence. Additionally, the daughter model applies its acquired knowledge to comprehend new sentences from the mother model, including the identification of lexical categories utilized by the latter.  Existing literature suggests that function words, which serve grammatical purposes, appear with higher frequency than content words, which convey specific meanings and may occur infrequently within a corpus. This observation aligns with Zipf’s law, a principle establishing that the frequency of word occurrence in natural languages follows a predictable distribution pattern, as extensively analyzed by scholars such as Mandelbrot and Zipf. Furthermore, a correlation exists between the frequency distribution of words in languages and the grammatical categories defined by linguists. This paper investigates whether a similar correlation is manifest in language generated by computational models.
