The initial portion of this discourse will focus on the foundational research surrounding language models that employ Bayesian methods and n-grams. These approaches were pivotal in developing speech recognition systems by managing sequential word patterns and addressing uncertainties inherent in spoken language. Such models laid the groundwork for current systems. Subsequently, the discussion will transition to contemporary end-to-end speech models, which integrate contextual information directly into the processing sequence. These advanced models extend beyond mere word recognition to encompass aspects such as tone, emotion, and speaker identity, thereby enhancing the comprehension and generation of speech.  Further examination will be directed towards the role of large language models (LLMs) in rectifying inaccuracies within automatic speech recognition (ASR) systems. Particular emphasis will be placed on issues related to fairness and bias, notably the impact of diverse accents, dialects, and speech patterns on speech processing. The conversation will explore recent advancements addressing these challenges and the ethical implications of ensuring equitable system performance across varied user demographics.  The analysis will incorporate theoretical perspectives on the adaptation and integration of LLMs with speech input, including discussions on risk assessment across different groups and the potential for transferring models from speech to LLM adaptation. This will inform varied design approaches for spoken agents. In light of these considerations, the exploration will delve into methods for training models using both text and speech inputs, which have enhanced understanding by aligning learning objectives across these modalities. Additionally, the potential for LLMs to assess voice quality and adapt to diverse speakers in real-time will be addressed.
