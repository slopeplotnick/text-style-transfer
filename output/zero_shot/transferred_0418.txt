In their 2021 study, Galhotra et al. address the challenge of estimating probabilities in systems with incomplete information. They investigate the use of specific models to derive probabilistic ranges for counterfactual inquiries. This approach allows for the refinement of model components in scenarios of uncertainty, potentially yielding narrower probabilistic ranges than conventional computational methods. Moreover, Galhotra et al. consider methodologies for evaluating AI interpretability by examining the notions of necessity and sufficiency in explanatory contexts.  Concurrently, Mothilal et al. (2021) propose that optimal model interpretability can be achieved by identifying the minimal set of critical features that are both necessary and sufficient to produce an outcome in a given context. Their framework assumes that explanations are confined to input features, which are considered to be independent of each other. They conduct a comparative analysis of various types of AI explanations. Within this framework, the concept of necessity pertains to changes requisite for producing the original outcome, whereas sufficiency relates to identifying features that consistently lead to the outcome irrespective of other variables. Their findings indicate a substantial practical variability among different interpretative methods.  Further expanding on these ideas, Pawar et al. (2024) also operate under the assumption of feature independence, utilizing the principles of necessity and sufficiency to evaluate AI methodologies and explore diverse strategies for generating explanatory instances. Beckers (2022) examines scenarios in which models predict outcomes based on actions, assuming model infallibility. In this context, they delineate explanations that are deemed sufficient and those that consider alternative hypothetical scenarios while presuming consistent predictive accuracy. Additionally, Beckers decomposes explanations by assessing the causal influence to identify the underlying reasons for the observed outcomes.
