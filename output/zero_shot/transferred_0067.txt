Large language models (LLMs) generally demonstrate superior performance across a variety of tasks, primarily due to their size and ability to effectively follow instructions. However, smaller language models (SLMs) have been shown to match or even surpass LLMs in specific domains, such as predicting mental-health indicators like depression and stress. While SLMs have improved significantly for other tasks, they often fall short in performance when compared to LLMs (Xu et al., 2024). These findings suggest that SLMs are highly effective when their linguistic processing capabilities align with the requirements of a given task. Nevertheless, a critical limitation of SLMs lies in their inability to effectively process lengthy inputs. Their constrained token capacities and difficulty in maintaining coherence over extended or complex contexts reduce their utility in tasks requiring the comprehension of longer narratives—an area in which LLMs excel (Kumar, 2025; Lu et al., 2024).   This evidence highlights the importance of moving beyond the simplistic notion of increasing model size to enhance performance. Instead, future advancements in mental-health-focused artificial intelligence (AI) should prioritize fine-tuning models to meet specific application demands. As concerns related to efficiency, transparency, safety, and privacy in clinical machine learning become increasingly relevant (Zhang et al., 2021), the trade-off between model performance and practical deployment will play a pivotal role in shaping the development of digital mental-health tools. In this context, SLMs have already proven capable of addressing the need for real-time, privacy-preserving mental-health detection tools (Harari et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2018), aligning with the broader push for AI systems to prioritize transparency and accountability in healthcare (Wies et al., 2021; Smith et al., 2023).  This study demonstrates that fine-tuned SLMs, when trained using strategies that integrate diverse data sources, can achieve performance comparable to or better than LLMs in predicting mental-health outcomes. These findings indicate that smaller, task-specific models may represent a more sustainable and ethical approach for mental-health monitoring in real-world contexts. Specifically, SLMs trained on diverse datasets are especially effective in predicting depression, stress, and suicidal ideation when combined with optimized loss functions. These results corroborate earlier findings by Benton et al. (2017), which indicate that leveraging shared patterns across related mental-health tasks can improve outcomes. However, unlike prior approaches that relied on multi-task learning setups, the current study achieves similar benefits through an efficient fine-tuning method, namely Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA), which reduces computational and memory demands. This underscores the potential of small, resource-efficient models to effectively leverage heterogeneous data, provided that training objectives are appropriately designed.
