Sentiment analysis is a crucial component of contemporary Natural Language Processing (NLP) applications, playing a significant role in domains such as digital governance, public health monitoring, inclusive financial services, and online content moderation. Despite advancements in deep learning and language modeling techniques globally, sentiment analysis research has inadequately addressed under-resourced South African languages. This neglect results in a scarcity of annotated datasets, insufficient lexical resources, and limited methodologies for cross-linguistic knowledge transfer. Consequently, models that perform effectively in well-resourced languages such as English and French exhibit diminished accuracy in African contexts, thereby impeding equitable access to NLP technologies.  The majority of multilingual sentiment analysis research has concentrated on language translation, cross-lingual embeddings, and the development of word lists for individual languages. While these approaches have demonstrated some success, they are fraught with challenges. Machine-translated lexicons often contain inaccuracies and fail to capture cultural nuances, particularly in African languages with complex morphological structures. Furthermore, word extraction methods reliant on extensive datasets are hampered by the lack of available resources for under-resourced languages. Although recent advancements in African language models, such as AfroXLMR and AfriBERTa, have been made, these models still encounter limitations due to the paucity of robust sentiment lexicons necessary for effective classification across diverse languages.  To address these challenges, this paper introduces TriLex, an innovative three-step framework designed to augment sentiment word lists for under-resourced African languages. The TriLex framework entails: (1) identifying potential sentiment-expressing words from existing datasets; (2) ensuring cross-linguistic equivalence by mapping these words across English, French, and African languages; and (3) employing Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) to refine, verify, and expand the word list using large language models enhanced with multilingual retrieval signals.  This study contributes to the field by: (1) presenting TriLex as a novel framework for the development of sentiment lexicons for South African languages with limited resources; (2) generating and disseminating a comprehensive multilingual sentiment word list for several South African languages to facilitate further research; and (3) conducting the inaugural detailed evaluation of AfroXLMR and AfriBERTa models utilizing an expanded resource of sentiment lexicons for isiXhosa and isiZulu.
