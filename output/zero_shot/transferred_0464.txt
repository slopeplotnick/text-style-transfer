The comprehension of fundamental scientific principles is crucial for accurately predicting physical phenomena across various contexts. However, contemporary video model evaluations are predominantly grounded in rudimentary physical knowledge and fail to assess the extent to which these models exhibit scientific reasoning. To address this limitation, we developed **VideoScience-Bench**, an evaluation framework designed to assess the capacity of video models to understand and reason about scientific concepts at a collegiate level. Each test item within this benchmark integrates multiple scientific concepts, requiring not only knowledge but also critical reasoning to achieve correct outcomes. The benchmark comprises 200 questions encompassing 14 topics and 103 core concepts from physics and chemistry.  We conducted expert assessments of seven state-of-the-art video models under Text-to-Video (T2V) and Image-to-Video (I2V) conditions, evaluating their performance across four dimensions: continuity, appropriate transformation of states, spatiotemporal coherence, and alignment with expected outcomes. A Vision-Language Model (VLM)-based evaluation framework was employed to judge the generated videos, yielding results that closely align with human evaluators’ assessments. To the best of our knowledge, **VideoScience-Bench** represents the first benchmark explicitly designed not only to evaluate the video-generating capabilities of these models but also to measure their ability to simulate and reason about physical and chemical phenomena accurately. The dataset and associated evaluation code are publicly available at [github.com/hao-ai-lab/VideoScience](https://github.com/hao-ai-lab/VideoScience).  Recent advancements, as evidenced by the releases of **Sora-2** and **Veo-3.1**, signify a paradigm shift in video generation. These models not only produce cinematic-quality videos but also exhibit enhanced comprehension of real-world dynamics. As the capabilities of video models evolve, the criteria for assessing video generation quality are expanding beyond traditional metrics such as Fréchet Video Distance (FVD) and Fréchet Video Motion Distance (FVMD). Increasingly, emphasis is being placed on the adherence of generated videos to fundamental physical laws and their ability to demonstrate a sophisticated understanding of the real world. Emerging models are now capable of zero-shot reasoning, tackling complex problems that require scientific, mathematical, and spatial reasoning.  Human experts rely on scientific principles to understand the world and predict outcomes, such as determining whether citric acid can remove iron stains or analyzing the behavior of an LED circuit with or without a resistor. This raises a critical question: beyond aesthetic quality and basic adherence to physical laws, can video models accurately simulate scientific phenomena and engage in meaningful scientific reasoning? Achieving scientific understanding, unlike general world comprehension, necessitates the integration of interconnected concepts—for example, applying principles of specific heat and heat transfer to predict the thermal absorption properties of water. A robust understanding of such principles is pivotal for leveraging these models in real-world discovery applications, where accurate predictions of complex phenomena are required.
