The evaluation of citation quality is assessed through metrics such as Recall, Precision, and F1 score, which collectively measure the comprehensiveness, accuracy, and overall balance of the citations, respectively. Recall quantifies the proportion of relevant references from a human-generated survey that appear in the machine-generated survey, indicating the extent of literature coverage. Precision, on the other hand, assesses the accuracy of the machine-generated citations by determining the proportion of references that align with those in the human-generated survey. Together, these metrics enable an evaluation of the degree to which machine-generated citations resemble those curated by human researchers, thereby providing an indication of the alignment between automated and human literature analysis. This methodology facilitates a comparative analysis of the effectiveness of machine and human approaches to literature surveys.  A commonly employed method for evaluating machine-generated text is the "LLM-as-a-Judge" approach, which is favored due to its scalability, consistency, and cost-efficiency. This approach typically involves providing a model with evaluation guidelines and scoring criteria to assess the quality of generated text. However, this method can be overly lenient, particularly for complex tasks such as the generation of comprehensive surveys. To address this limitation, two variants of the LLM-as-a-Judge framework are utilized: one incorporating a human-written survey as a reference to evaluate overall content, and another employing principle-based guidelines for structural validation.   For assessing survey quality, a human-generated survey on the same topic is provided alongside the machine-generated one. This explicit comparison enables the model to make more informed evaluations, addressing several limitations of guideline-based assessments. First, the inclusion of a human-authored survey serves as a high-quality reference, reducing the likelihood of the model assigning inflated scores to vague or superficial content. Second, the direct comparison enables the identification of critical elements such as robust arguments and supporting evidence, which may be overlooked when relying solely on generalized guidelines. Third, the use of an expert-level example ensures that evaluation scores more accurately reflect the relative strengths and weaknesses of different systems, avoiding uniform scoring and allowing for a clearer differentiation between systems. This approach provides a more nuanced understanding of system performance, highlighting areas of strength and opportunities for improvement in the generation of machine-based surveys.
