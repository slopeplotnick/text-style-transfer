The Adversarial Motion Prior (AMP) demonstrates competence in imitating motions; however, it requires access to reference motion data during training. In contrast, the Score-Matching Prior (SMP) is capable of accurately reproducing a wide range of motions, including complex ones, without relying on reference data for training. Moreover, SMP outperforms both SMILING and AMP-Frozen across all evaluated skills. While AMP-Frozen attempts to address AMP's dependency on reference data by employing a pre-trained discriminator, this approach fails to achieve effective motion imitation and often results in suboptimal performance.   DeepMimic employs phase synchronization as a mechanism to reduce tracking errors for certain motion tasks; nonetheless, SMP remains competitive. However, DeepMimic is less effective at handling more challenging motions, such as spin kicks and cartwheels, particularly when pose termination is disabled, leading to suboptimal solutions in some trials. Notably, this work represents one of the first to leverage score-matching techniques to construct a reusable motion model that exhibits comparable performance to leading adversarial imitation learning methods.   This section examines the stabilizing effects and improved performance resulting from the use of ensemble score-matching during training. Specifically, we compare the proposed ensemble score-matching approach—which averages multiple evaluations of the score-difference sampling (SDS) function over predetermined diffusion timesteps—to the conventional single-timestep SDS method employed in prior studies. This comparison evaluates single-clip imitation tasks, including "Run," "Cartwheel," and "Backflip." The results, detailed in Table 5, indicate that single-timestep SDS performs adequately for simple motions but struggles with complex tasks. For instance, policies trained using single-timestep SDS often fail to replicate a backflip accurately, instead producing suboptimal behaviors such as standing still or falling backward. By contrast, the proposed ensemble SDS approach generates a more robust and reliable reward signal, significantly enhancing the training of policies for intricate motions, including acrobatic tasks.
