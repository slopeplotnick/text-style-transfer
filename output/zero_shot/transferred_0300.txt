This study investigates the parallels between neural processes and artificial intelligence (AI) mechanisms, emphasizing the concept of "world models," which serve as systems to facilitate prediction and comprehension. Through a comparative analysis of information processing in the brain and AI, particularly with respect to large language models, it is evident that both domains have evolved comparable methodologies. Consequently, this research proposes a novel perspective on large-scale information processing within the brain. Specifically, structures such as the neocortex and cerebellum appear to construct world models by anticipating future events, learning from predictive errors, and utilizing these models across diverse tasks. These tasks include sensory interpretation and motor control.  This hypothesis integrates classical theories of brain function, such as the internal model theory and the mirror neuron system, positing that such a unified framework is crucial for a wide range of cognitive functions using a singular foundational architecture. This conceptual framework holds potential to bridge the gap between neuroscience and AI research, thereby enhancing our understanding of human-like intelligence.  Notably, the cerebellum is posited to function as a predictive agent, processing external information, forecasting subsequent occurrences, and refining its predictions based on inaccuracies encountered.
