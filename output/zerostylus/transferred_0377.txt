The anticipated improvement in solver performance when utilizing a verifier to assist in selecting optimal solutions forms the primary focus of this study. To test the validity of this hypothesis, we analyze various combinations of solvers and verifiers selected from a set of 12 models, specifically concentrating on the three smallest models within each of four predefined groups. In this process, the solver iteratively generates solutions until the verifier identifies one as correct, allowing for a maximum of nine attempts. If no correct solution is confirmed within this limit, we retain the final attempt for evaluation. The results of these experiments, along with the projected improvements, are depicted in Figure 4. However, while these improvements are incremental and constrained by the nine-attempt limit, the observed outcomes generally align with theoretical projections. Notably, the analysis reveals that self-review by solvers provides limited benefits, whereas evaluation by an external verifier—particularly one from a distinct model group—results in more substantial performance improvements. This observation underscores the effectiveness of our approach for estimating enhancements, which does not necessitate significant additional computational effort. A key insight is that individuals, and by analogy solvers, are susceptible to cognitive biases, such as perceiving solutions resembling their own as more likely to be correct. This phenomenon, akin to the self-enhancement bias, is reflected in solver-verifier interactions. Specifically, our findings indicate that models with strong reasoning capabilities gain minimal advantage from self-verification. Conversely, their performance improves significantly when assessed by verifiers from distinct model families, mirroring the aforementioned bias. Such biases diminish the efficacy of verification, particularly in tasks requiring precise differentiation between correct and incorrect solutions. Verifiers with solution distributions closely aligned to those of the solver are more likely to accept erroneous outputs, thereby limiting potential improvements. Therefore, employing a verifier with a distinct perspective mitigates this issue and enhances overall performance.
