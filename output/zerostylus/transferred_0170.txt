The methods analyzed exhibit substantial challenges in two primary areas. Firstly, regarding data types, most studies predominantly utilize numerical and temporal datasets in their analyses. Although textual information is incorporated, it remains superficial, thereby impeding the synthesis of diverse data sources such as market sentiment and policy explanations. Consequently, this limitation reveals intrinsic problems at the model level. Neural networks, while proficient in identifying and learning patterns, fundamentally lack the capability to comprehend the underlying semantic and contextual nuances inherent in complex situations. This deficiency impairs performance in intricate market conditions, where discerning cause-and-effect relationships between events and price fluctuations is essential. However, the advent of large language models (LLMs) has revolutionized the analytical landscape by transitioning from solely analyzing historical patterns to incorporating mechanisms for memory utilization. For instance, FinMem developed a financial memory framework designed to retain concise representations of news and events, while MagicNet constructed a hierarchical memory architecture to temporally organize market data. Nevertheless, the structural design of their memory systems continues to reveal inherent deficiencies. Current approaches predominantly focus on storing discrete textual or data fragments in isolation, without systematically integrating them into a cohesive framework that captures the progression of events. This reliance on identifying analogous meanings impedes performance in complex scenarios, where effectively managing intricate situations is essential. Although these methods are transitioning from an exclusive focus on historical patterns to incorporating memory, substantial efforts remain necessary to comprehend and apply the appropriate information effectively. Therefore, this paper directs attention to these specific challenges. The event-driven approach to stock prediction seeks to identify the underlying causal factors driving fluctuations in stock prices. Prior to the advent of Large Language Models (LLMs), research in this domain evolved from analyzing the impact of events on stock returns to the automatic extraction of event signals from text, utilizing rudimentary models such as Bag-of-Words and topic models, eventually progressing to sophisticated deep learning frameworks like BERT. However, these methodologies persistently encounter a fundamental issue: they remain predominantly concentrated on numerical data. Event labels and sentiment scores are often extracted and subsequently converted into features for integration into temporal models such as Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks or Transformer architectures, thereby enhancing their analytical capabilities.

The models, in addressing these challenges, internalize data patterns rather than comprehending the underlying logic of events. Consequently, this limitation impairs their capacity for comprehensive analysis, resulting in the omission of significant details. Moreover, by not fully grasping the intricacies involved, the models are restricted in their analytical abilities. Therefore, enhancing the models' understanding of event logic is essential for improving their analytical performance and ensuring the inclusion of critical details.
