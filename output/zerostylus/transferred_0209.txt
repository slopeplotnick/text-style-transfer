In all three models, the influence exerted by the young on both image and prediction remains consistent, demonstrating that it is a dependable measure irrespective of the level of detail. However, an explanation model that treats variables independently and disregards their interrelations only reveals the direct effect, which may be equivalent to the total effect in the initial model but null in the final one. Therefore, direct influence does not consistently serve as a dependable metric. Section 5.3.1 addresses issues arising from violations of model assumptions, highlighting the necessity of careful model selection. Consequently, addressing the influence of assumptions that introduce bias into explanations necessitates either modifying the model or ensuring the explainer is cognizant of the biased context in which the explanations are applicable. Nevertheless, this observation does not imply that complex models are always superior to simpler ones in every situation. For instance, ensuring that explanations align with intended understanding requires the causal model to correspond to the granularity of detail comprehended by the individual. Moreover, simplifying complex information by omitting certain detailed causal mechanisms facilitates comprehension by presenting essential data in a more accessible form. Additionally, leveraging expert knowledge in the causal explanation model presented in Section 5.3.1 aids in effectively discerning and eliminating spurious correlations, surpassing the capabilities of data-dependent models. Consequently, the explanation model is capable of identifying spurious correlations established by the predictor, even when the same dataset is employed to derive the explanation model. With expert knowledge becoming more accessible through LLMs, as demonstrated with the example presented, this capability proves particularly beneficial for enhancing models by identifying unintended behaviors.
