The study employed a different dataset, AI2-THOR, instead of the previously used TEACH dataset during the benchmarking phase. This methodological transition was necessary to enable direct replication for feasibility assessment and provide a common ground for fair algorithmic comparison. Consequently, it also allowed for indirect performance evaluation with tasks of a similar nature. However, for benchmarking LTAA against traditional MRTA approaches, the same dataset was used. Furthermore, the authors are actively testing SMART-LLM with the TEACH dataset to establish a more balanced benchmark system. The study was conducted on a relatively small evaluation scale, which facilitated systematic baseline comparison and thorough capability analysis. Nonetheless, real-world construction involves a substantially greater number of tasks with complex spatial and temporal constraints, which were not fully captured within the evaluation dataset. The study employed abstract descriptors such as elemental/compound and heavy/dexterous to represent construction tasks, enhancing generalization. This abstraction was imperative given the absence of a large-scale real-world construction operation database. The authors posit that with such a database, evaluating LTAA in scenarios involving dynamic replanning and resource conflicts would bolster confidence in the framework's robustness. Moreover, the inherent variability in LLM outputs raises concerns about reliability in safety-critical construction operations. Despite multi-stage validation eliminating execution failures, the controlled stochasticity allowed for nuanced reasoning but could potentially lead to allocation variations. Therefore, construction environments requiring absolute determinism may necessitate additional verification mechanisms beyond current protocols. Additionally, when assessing the computational resources required, challenges arose in quantifying the costs per stage of SMART-LLM due to the use of a single concatenated prompt for all stages. To facilitate comparison with LTAA, which exclusively focuses on allocation, the authors approximated SMART-LLM's stage-level costs by dividing its total tokens and runtime by three. Although this approximation provides a preliminary estimation, it may not fully capture the true allocation-specific overhead. Future work with stage-level instrumentation would allow for more precise analysis.
