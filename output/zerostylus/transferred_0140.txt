The Area Under the Curve (AUC) is notably employed to evaluate a model's ranking efficacy independently of specific decision thresholds, thereby addressing potential class imbalance within evaluation datasets. In this study, we introduce our innovative evaluation framework, which integrates Hallucination Detection with Fact Verification, two domains that have rarely been examined in conjunction (Author et al., 2024). To elucidate the rationale for their historical separation, we initially explore the reasons these two closely related paradigms have evolved in isolation, and subsequently, we analyze the challenges inherent in current testing methodologies that hinder comprehensive evaluation across both areas (§3.1). Subsequently, we outline the fundamental concepts underlying our system and detail its operational mechanisms (§3.2). Traditional Fact Verification research primarily emphasizes text-based datasets, whereas Hallucination Detection approaches predominantly focus on model behavior and mechanisms. Traditional fact verification assessments, such as FEVER, are inherently structured around predetermined claim-label pairs, without accounting for the provenance of these claims. However, this structure is unsuitable for Hallucination Detection methods, which require an analysis of generative processes, such as examining internal model states or assessing consistency across outputs. Consequently, the absence of these signals within fixed Fact Verification datasets complicates the concurrent evaluation of both components. A potential solution to address this issue involves the utilization of pre-generated outputs from extensive language models, such as HELM, which aim to enhance testing processes. While this approach demonstrates utility, it presents two significant challenges within the rapidly evolving domain of AI: the swift obsolescence of models and their inability to remain consistently up-to-date. Thus, constructing a dataset utilizing contemporary models such as LLaMA-3 may soon serve merely as a historical archive, given the continuous emergence of more advanced models. Furthermore, the evaluation of novel models cannot be conducted, as their testing is precluded. When a fixed test is employed, it fails to evaluate hallucination detection methods on novel or divergent models, revealing limitations in adaptability and generalization. Therefore, dependence on specific model characteristics may constrain the capacity to evaluate novel architectures, thereby impeding advancements and failing to demonstrate genuine adaptability.
