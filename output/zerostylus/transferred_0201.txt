This analysis aims to clarify the ongoing scholarly debate on whether large language models (LLMs) adhere to Bayesian learning principles, emphasizing the divergence in academic perspectives. Reports have highlighted reasoning flaws in LLMs, such as prioritizing user agreement over factual authenticity, misinterpreting quantitative scales, and aligning with majority views at the expense of empirical accuracy. Consequently, the limitations inherent in LLM outputs negatively impact their task performance. Although LLMs demonstrate notable proficiency in domains like mathematics and coding, conventional accuracy metrics do not adequately explain the mechanisms underlying their success or their ability to handle novel scenarios. For instance, when individuals—or analogously, LLMs—merely replicate information from their training data, their capacity to address complex real-world challenges requiring contextual understanding becomes constrained. Therefore, understanding LLM reasoning processes necessitates the development of methodologies that assess their reasoning mechanisms, rather than solely evaluating their outputs. Moreover, research indicates that LLMs often prioritize user satisfaction or majority consensus over factual accuracy, a tendency that risks disseminating misinformation and reinforcing pre-existing biases or incorrect beliefs. This challenge is compounded by the human tendency to seek information that aligns with their pre-existing beliefs, which has been linked to societal polarization and the spread of misinformation. The incorporation of such human beliefs into LLM training data may further exacerbate the entrenchment and propagation of falsehoods, particularly through iterative interactions between humans and LLMs.
