From an evolutionary perspective, research has been conducted on emergent communication utilizing multi-agent systems, focusing on the potential evolutionary pathways of language. A significant example is the Talking Heads framework, developed by Steels (2000, 2015). This project provides a model of language evolution based on interactions among agents, including references to non-linguistic contexts. Through interactive processes such as language games, these agents develop an artificial language. The naming game, in particular, is aimed at defining names for objects in the surroundings (cf. Steels 2001; Steels and Kaplan 2001 for studies on word naming; Steels and Loetzch 2012). Currently, the research also addresses the emergence and acquisition of syntactic phenomena (cf. Steels and Beuls 2017; Steels et al. 2018; van Trijp 2016). A key distinction from the MODOMA project is that, while the MODOMA framework emphasizes the acquisition of a fragment of a natural language by a single agent, the Talking Heads project involves both agents collaboratively engaging in the development and learning of an artificial language. Alishahi has conducted research simulating language acquisition by applying algorithms to datasets, which are either human-curated or artificially generated. Notably, studies by Alishahi and Chrupala (2009, 2012) and Alishahi and Stevenson (2008) explore the acquisition of linguistic categories, including lexical items, semantic interpretations, and fundamental syntactic structures. Additionally, Matusevych et al. (2013) have enhanced artificially generated input to emulate child-adult interactions, akin to data from the CHILDES database (MacWhinney 2014). However, unlike the MODOMA framework, the language learning algorithm does not participate in conversational exchanges. Beekhuizen et al. (2014) offer a different perspective by modeling the acquisition of grammatical constructions using a single agent tasked with processing generated representations of utterances and meanings. Furthermore, Conner et al.'s Baby SRL project models the acquisition of semantic role labeling by inputting annotated data from the CHILDES corpus or constructed sentences into their algorithm.

The Baby SRL project (2008, 2009) builds upon data sourced from the CHILDES database and synthetically generated sentences to explore how algorithms can effectively acquire the ability to label the semantic roles of words within a sentence. This project represents a significant advancement in computational linguistics, as it diverges from traditional methods, which typically involve inputting corpora into language acquisition procedures (e.g., Alishahi and Stevenson 2008, Alishahi and Chrupala 2012, Conner et al. 2009, Matusevych et al. 2013). In contrast, the Baby SRL project employs a broader definition of "language model" that encompasses rule-based approaches, statistical methods, and artificial neural networks, rather than limiting it to the neural models trained on a next-word prediction objective, such as transformer-based models like GPT (cf. Brown et al. 2020, Radford et al. 2018) and BERT (cf. Devlin et al. 2019). This distinction is crucial, as it allows for a more comprehensive understanding of how algorithms can deduce linguistic roles from data. Moreover, by integrating these varied methodologies, the project seeks to determine whether algorithms can identify and utilize statistical patterns comparable to those found in human language. Specifically, the research aims to assess the extent to which these patterns can be translated into grammatical rules and to evaluate the system's effectiveness in acquiring structured linguistic knowledge in an unsupervised manner. Ultimately, the findings contribute to a deeper understanding of language acquisition processes and the potential for developing more sophisticated computational models.
