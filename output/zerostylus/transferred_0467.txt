A significant challenge for OLLMs arises from the diversity and complexity of input modalities, which fundamentally reshape the nature of safety risks and necessitate more sophisticated detection strategies. The heterogeneity of input types introduces distinct risk patterns that vary across modalities, such as implicit bias in textual data, misinformation in visual content, and privacy breaches or harmful noise in auditory inputs. Moreover, the integration of multiple modalities adds another layer of complexity, as cross-modal interactions can obscure latent risks that are not evident within single modalities. For instance, while the text "I want to fly" and an image of an individual standing on a rooftop may appear innocuous when analyzed separately, their combination could imply a potentially harmful intent, such as suicidal ideation. Similarly, a video of police patrols paired with a textual query like "How to overcome obstacles" could convey an implicit criminal motive, which would remain undetectable if the modalities were assessed in isolation. Consequently, addressing safety risks in OLLMs requires both a nuanced understanding of individual modalities and enhanced integration of cross-modal relationships. However, applying safety alignment techniques directly to base OLLMs proves to be an inefficient and resource-intensive approach. While alignment strategies, such as those proposed by Ouyang et al. (2022) and Bai et al. (2022), aim to mitigate risks, they often demand extensive development time, additional computational resources, and can inadvertently degrade the core reasoning capabilities of the models. Furthermore, even after significant investment, aligned models frequently require costly retraining to address emergent safety issues and remain susceptible to low-cost adversarial attacks, such as jailbreak attempts. In this context, guardrail models offer a more flexible and decentralized alternative for ensuring safety. However, current research on guardrail systems is primarily focused on unimodal scenarios or basic combinations, such as text-image pairs, leaving omni-modal settings largely underexplored. This limitation is particularly critical given that unsafe inputs may emerge not only from individual modalities but also from their diverse and mixed combinations. As a result, unimodal guardrails and simple image-text filters are insufficient to address the broader complexities of omni-modal moderation. Moreover, many existing guardrails simplify safeguarding by framing it as a binary classification task. While this approach reduces the complexity of safety evaluations, it inherently limits the ability to differentiate between varying degrees of risk, thereby constraining the overall effectiveness of these systems.
