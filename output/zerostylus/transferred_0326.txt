A journal entry, within the realm of accounting, constitutes the foundational record of a financial transaction, typically involving a debit and a credit entry across one or more accounts. Each entry is supplemented with attributes such as the transaction date, posting ID, currency, the individual responsible for the entry, applicable tax rates, monetary amounts, and occasionally a descriptive note. These heterogeneous features, encompassing numerical, categorical, and textual data, render journal entries an ideal testbed for systems designed to detect anomalies in multi-type data environments. Both synthetic and anonymized datasets share a common feature space comprising these core attributes, thereby facilitating consistent modeling and evaluation across different domains. In response, researchers have explored the application of machine learning (ML) to enhance the detection of anomalies within accounting data. Unsupervised algorithms, such as clustering and Isolation Forest, are proficient at analyzing journal entries to identify those that deviate from established patterns, as demonstrated by Herreros-Martinez et al. [2025]. Moreover, recent research utilizing synthetic general ledger data demonstrates that these approaches can surpass the performance of traditional rule-based systems, as noted by Gronewald et al. [2024]. Nonetheless, certain limitations persist: conventional ML models primarily engage with structured datasets, which may impede their ability to discern the contextual nuances necessary to differentiate genuinely risky anomalies from benign outliers. Meanwhile, the emergence of Large Language Models (LLMs) introduces new possibilities for auditing processes. These foundational models, exemplified by GPT-4 and its counterparts, exhibit powerful natural language understanding and reasoning capabilities. Early studies, such as those by Gu et al. [2023], suggest that LLMs could serve as AI auditors or co-pilots, collaborating with humans in the analysis of financial data. Furthermore, recent advancements indicate that LLMs can be effectively leveraged for anomaly detection, including SigLLM for time-series data as highlighted by Alnegheimish et al. [2024] and AnoLLM for tabular records as detailed by Tsai et al. [2025], both demonstrating competitive performance without requiring extensive feature engineering. However, deploying LLMs in critical audit applications necessitates caution. By their nature, LLMs may occasionally produce inconsistent responses, exhibit bias, or generate fabricated information. Recognizing this, the community has initiated efforts to audit the auditors themselves with methodologies such as AuditLLM by Amirizaniani et al. [2024a] and LLMAuditor by Amirizaniani et al. [2024b]. These initiatives underscore that, while LLMs are undeniably powerful, governance and oversight remain essential when employing them as decision-support tools in domains like auditing.

Section 2 underscores the significance of establishing guidelines and oversight when utilizing large language models (LLMs) for decision-making in fields such as auditing. This is particularly crucial because it has catalyzed the development of evaluation tools, notably AuditLLM by Amirizaniani et al. (2024), designed to assess the outputs of these models. Moreover, the process entails several key considerations that must be acknowledged. While LLMs provide substantial utility, it is imperative to balance their capabilities with rules that ensure responsible application. As demonstrated by Amirizaniani et al. (2024), integrating structured evaluation metrics into the deployment of LLMs can enhance their practical utility in auditing contexts, thereby fostering both accuracy and interpretability in high-stakes domains.
