Except for Study 1, all simulated datasets successfully replicated the factor structure of their corresponding scale (H1). Moreover, M-CFA demonstrates that simulated participants and real-world participants respond to the scales in comparable ways (H2). These findings align with prior research on group-level simulated data (Argyle et al., 2023; Hewitt et al., 2024). Notably, Studies 3 and 4 exhibit higher levels of measurement invariance between datasets compared to Studies 1 and 2. This suggests that scales developed using simulated subjects may demonstrate greater generalizability to human participants than the reverse. One plausible explanation for this is that, when responding, large language models (LLMs) appear to interpret scale items through a standardized “common semantic denominator.” This approach reduces susceptibility to randomness and biases arising from study conditions or population-specific characteristics. Therefore, factor structures derived from simulated samples may exhibit greater semantic generalizability to external datasets than those derived from empirical data. However, additional research is necessary to investigate the factors influencing differences in how LLMs and human participants interpret and respond to survey items. While CFA produces consistently positive results, simulated samples fail to replicate certain properties of real-world data, including pairwise correlations (H3), distribution (H4), and variance (H5). These findings indicate that, although the two datasets share the same factor structure, their score distributions differ significantly across all measured dimensions. Consequently, group-level simulated data and their corresponding dimensional structures are sufficiently robust for preliminary pilot testing, whereas individual-level synthetic data lack the reliability necessary for this purpose. These limitations align with observations reported by Petrov and colleagues (2024). It is also important to note that, while Studies 2, 3, and 4 yield comparable outcomes, Study 1 exhibits markedly divergent results. This divergence may stem from several factors. First, the factor structure of this scale was determined using a different rotation method (minres) compared to other scales, which may not have led to substantively different responses (Sass & Schmitt, 2010). Second, this scale employs a 5-point Likert format, contrasting with the 6-point scale used in Study 2 and the 7-point scale used in other studies.
