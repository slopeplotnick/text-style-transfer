The language acquisition experiments discussed in this study focus on integrating explicit grammatical knowledge into a lexicon. Subsequently, the daughter agent utilizes this acquired knowledge to participate in communication with the mother agent. To achieve this, the system employs statistical machine learning techniques in conjunction with a clearly defined grammatical representation. This approach contributes to existing systems, which rely predominantly on statistical methods and neural networks, by introducing a more interpretable, rule-based framework for language modeling. Notably, the study leverages Zipf's law (Mandelbrot, 1954; Zipf, 1949) to provide a robust framework for evaluating the feasibility of implementing a multi-agent simulation for first language acquisition. However, the study's primary aim extends beyond the binary classification of function and content words; instead, this classification is employed as a methodological tool to assess the validity of the proposed approach to modeling first language acquisition. The application of Zipf's law is advantageous due to its solid and reliable foundation for assessing system performance and components, a practice widely recognized in the field. For instance, previous studies, such as those by Chaabouni et al. (2019) and Rita et al. (2020), have successfully utilized Zipf's law to develop multi-agent frameworks for modeling language evolution. Similarly, recent contributions from Dębowski (2021), Takahashi and Tanaka-Ishii (2019), and Takamichi et al. (2024) further underscore its utility. Importantly, the experiments conducted in the present study validate the essential components required to enable a multi-agent framework for first language acquisition. Specifically, the acquisition of function and content words is facilitated by a hybrid language learning strategy, which leverages statistical tendencies to support the acquisition of discrete grammatical rules. Consequently, these findings substantiate the modeling of more complex grammatical phenomena and affirm the feasibility of the MODOMA research agenda. In line with this, recent work by Shakouri et al. (2025) demonstrates that core grammatical categories, such as nouns, verbs, and prepositions, can be effectively acquired through unsupervised learning techniques. This paper presents two experiments that exemplify the proposed approach. The initial simulation, detailed in Sections 6 and 7, utilizes a training dataset generated by the mother agent and serves to determine the optimal parameter settings for acquiring this linguistic phenomenon. Subsequently, a second experiment, employing a novel test dataset produced by the adult language model, validates these configurations (see Section 8). Taken together, the results indicate that the MODOMA framework successfully acquires and represents grammatical categories within the daughter agent's grammar formalism.

The daughter language model, constructed as part of the MOD-OMA framework, exhibits notable characteristics in its design and functionality. Similar to Delilah, the daughter agent functions as both a parser and a generator; however, unlike the adult language model, the grammatical representations within the daughter agent are initially underspecified and become increasingly refined through language acquisition procedures. A significant similarity between the adult and child systems lies in their shared use of graph structures to represent grammatical knowledge. Both systems utilize parsers and generators that execute their respective grammars by unifying lexical structures, enabling the generation of well-formed utterances, grammaticality judgments, and parses based on input utterances relative to the current grammar specification. Importantly, the child agent’s parser and generator possess the unique capability to operate on an incomplete grammar containing underspecified structures. This aligns with the process of human first-language acquisition, as the daughter language model must engage in conversational exchanges with the mother agent prior to the completion of its acquisition process. The daughter agent is composed of three interconnected modules: (1) the parser and generator—collectively referred to as the automaton—which execute (2) the grammar, specified through acquisition facilitated by (3) the language acquisition device (cf. Shakouri et al., 2018). Crucially, while the grammar evolves during acquisition, the parser and generator themselves remain unaltered by the learning procedures. The language acquisition process results in explicit grammatical knowledge represented by newly acquired lexical entries and/or grammatical properties of existing lexical entries, thereby constraining their combinatory possibilities. For instance, lexical entries, such as the minimal example in Figure 2, serve as comprehensive data structures representing words and constructions. These structures are specified for multiple grammatical properties, including phonological form (phonform), semantic content representation (semiform), and an expandable list of additional acquired grammatical properties. Grammatical properties are formalized as feature-value pairs, such as [CONTENT OR FUNCTION WORD: content word] and [CONTENT OR FUNCTION WORD: function word], the acquisition of which constitutes the focal objective of this study. Since the learning procedures are unsupervised, the daughter language model operates without direct access to the labels utilized by the mother language model or its grammar descriptions. Accordingly, acquired grammatical properties and semantic representations are encoded using unique alpha-numeric identifiers, as demonstrated by property type 'A' and property value 'b' in Figure 2 (see also Figure 3). These representations further encompass grammatical properties not directly investigated in this study, such as terminal features that distinguish terminal words from abstract constructions, and semiform indices that facilitate the representation of anaphora, including reflexives. Additionally, technical features—such as session IDs and lexical entry numbers—are embedded to uniquely identify each rule within the grammar. As language acquisition progresses, the grammar evolves to include increasingly complex structures, with the HEAD and ARGUMENT components capable of incorporating data structures analogous to lexical items.
