The tutorial will commence with an examination of the pioneering research involving Bayesian methods and n-grams in language models, which were crucial in establishing frameworks for speech recognition by addressing sequences of words and the inherent uncertainties in human speech. These early models played a pivotal role in shaping the structures underlying contemporary systems. Subsequently, we will explore the rise of end-to-end speech models that inherently incorporate contextual information throughout the processing framework. These advanced models aim not only to analyze lexical elements but also to incorporate aspects such as tone, emotion, and speaker identity, thereby enhancing the comprehension and synthesis of speech. Moreover, this discussion will address the role of large language models (LLMs) in correcting errors within automatic speech recognition (ASR) systems. Special attention will be devoted to issues of fairness and bias in speech processing, with a particular focus on how variations in accent, dialect, and speaking styles may influence system performance. In this context, Section 2 will examine recent advancements addressing these challenges and investigate the ethical considerations necessary to ensure systems function equitably for all users. This section will also delve into theoretical frameworks regarding how LLMs adapt to and integrate speech input, focusing on assessing risk across diverse groups and analyzing mechanisms for transferring models from speech processing to LLM adaptation. This analysis ultimately informs distinct design considerations for spoken agents. Furthermore, we will examine approaches for training that integrate text and speech modalities, leveraging combined learning objectives to enhance comprehension across both domains. In our subsequent discussion, we shall systematically explore how large language models can estimate voice quality and adapt to diverse speakers in real-time.
