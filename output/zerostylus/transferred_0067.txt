Larger language models (LLMs) generally outperform across a broader range of tasks due to their larger capacity and enhanced ability to follow instructions. However, smaller language models (SLMs) demonstrate competitive and even superior performance on specific tasks, particularly those related to depression and stress prediction. Despite notable improvements in other areas, SLMs still underperform compared to LLMs (Xu et al., 2024). This finding suggests that SLMs can be highly effective when the task structure and linguistic signals align well with their capabilities. Nonetheless, a critical limitation is that SLMs struggle with long input sequences due to both token-length constraints and a reduced ability to manage extended contextual dependencies (Kumar, 2025). Consequently, this restricts their applicability in scenarios requiring nuanced understanding of lengthy or complex narratives, where LLMs maintain a distinct advantage (Lu et al., 2024). Therefore, our findings support a paradigm shift from size-centric scaling to purpose-centric optimization in mental-health AI. As factors such as efficiency, transparency, safety, and user privacy (Zhang et al., 2021) gain increasing priority in clinical machine learning, balancing performance with practicality will define the next generation of digital mental-health tools. Moreover, SLMs address emerging demands in digital mental health for in-situ, low-latency, and privacy-preserving early detection (Harari et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2018), while aligning with calls for transparent and accountable AI in healthcare settings (Wies et al., 2021; Smith et al., 2023). By demonstrating that fine-tuned SLMs, when employing adaptive, cross-dataset strategies, can match or surpass LLMs in mental-health outcome prediction, this study provides empirical evidence that smaller, well-aligned models may offer a more sustainable and ethically sound path forward for real-world mental-health monitoring. Specifically, our work shows that cross-dataset training is not only feasible but beneficial for predicting depression, stress, and suicidal ideation, particularly when combined with an adaptive loss design. This finding resonates with observations from Yao et al. (Benton et al., 2017), who reported that shared latent representations across related affective tasks can promote positive transfer. However, unlike their multi-task architecture, our approach achieves comparable cross-task gains within a parameter-efficient fine-tuning paradigm (LoRA), thus reducing computational cost and memory footprint. This distinction highlights that even lightweight fine-tuning frameworks can effectively leverage dataset diversity, provided the optimization objective is properly calibrated.

Our results underscore the efficacy of leveraging diverse data sources for training models aimed at predicting depression, stress, and suicidal ideation, particularly when integrated with a strategically designed loss function. This approach aligns with the findings of Yao et al., demonstrating the advantages of diverse data integration. Moreover, Benton et al. (2017) highlight that leveraging shared patterns across related mental-health tasks can enhance performance outcomes, a notion that further supports our approach. In contrast to their multi-task framework, our methodology achieves comparable advantages through a more streamlined fine-tuning process, specifically utilizing LoRA, which effectively reduces computational power and memory requirements. Recent investigations corroborate this, indicating that small and lightweight models can effectively leverage diverse datasets when training objectives are appropriately configured (Xu et al., 2023; Ding et al., 2023; Lialin et al., 2023; Han et al., 2024). Therefore, our findings suggest that strategic goal-setting plays a crucial role in optimizing model performance. In summary, our experiments demonstrate that leveraging parameter-efficient techniques, such as LoRA, combined with adaptive, weighted loss functions, enables models to learn discriminative representations for mental-health prediction with reduced computational demands.
