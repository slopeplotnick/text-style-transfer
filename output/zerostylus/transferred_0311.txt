The study examines whether a computational program designed to simulate human language acquisition can autonomously identify grammatical structures and lexical categories through interaction with a linguistic model known as Delilah, which provides examples of adult language usage. Subsequently, the daughter model algorithm utilizes the acquired grammar to engage in communication with the mother model through a structured computational methodology. As demonstrated in the interaction process, the daughter model is capable of generating new sentences utilizing the grammar it has learned and subsequently shares these constructions with the mother model during their discourse. Furthermore, as illustrated in the settings configuration, the mother model provides evaluative feedback on the quality of grammar, which the daughter model then employs to enhance its performance. Notably, in the daughter's learning process, it employs the acquired knowledge from the mother model to interpret novel sentences, thereby enabling it to discern the linguistic patterns and word choices utilized by the mother model. Empirical studies suggest that terms fulfilling grammatical functions appear frequently, while those conveying specific meanings occur less often. Certain content words might occur only once or a limited number of times within a text. This distribution is consistent with Zipf’s law, positing that the most frequently used words in a language occur much more often than those used less frequently; this phenomenon has been analyzed by scholars such as Mandelbrot and Zipf. Consequently, there exists a correlation between the distribution of words within languages and the grammatical categories examined by linguists, with this interrelation influencing the structure and categorization processes in linguistic studies. Therefore, this study explores the possibility of identifying a similar kind of linkage within language generated by computational means.
