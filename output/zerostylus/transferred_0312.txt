Models demonstrate variability in performance across topics, as evidenced by the data presented in Table 2. Specifically, performance tends to be slightly lower in domains such as Religion and Sport, where scores typically range from 7.8 to 8.2. In contrast, the scores for Game and Lifestyle topics are noticeably higher, often exceeding 8.4. This disparity underscores the tendency of models to approach culturally or ethically sensitive subjects, like religion and sports, with greater caution and restraint. Conversely, topics such as gaming and lifestyle are more neutral and widely covered within training datasets, which facilitates smoother and more proficient mediation. Moreover, steering and judgment performances exhibit a strong correlation, as illustrated in Fig. 3. A positive relationship between average scores for these tasks suggests that models excelling in judgment tend to perform equally well in steering. Therefore, both tasks appear to rely on shared cognitive and alignment capabilities. This consistency further implies that enhancing fundamental mediation skills, such as contextual comprehension and balanced decision-making, could yield improvements across a broader range of evaluative tasks. However, while our findings indicate the potential for more unified skill integration to improve interpretability and control in language models, the exact methodologies for achieving such integration remain an open area for exploration. Furthermore, as shown in Table 3, the mediation process exhibits varying degrees of effectiveness across distinct linguistic dimensions. For instance, reductions in the use of toxic language and exclamatory expressions highlight the process's ability to promote neutral and composed responses. Consequently, this contributes to conversational exchanges that appear more balanced and less emotionally charged. However, argumentativeness scores show minimal change, suggesting that mediation has a limited impact on managing disagreements or discourse-level dynamics. Interestingly, differences in model behavior emerge between simulated user interactions and real-world experiments. Specifically, models exhibit distinct tendencies when generating both sides of a conversation compared to responding to actual user input. Therefore, it becomes evident that evaluating mediation requires consideration not only of procedural aspects—how the process is implemented—but also of its tangible communicative effects within realistic contexts.
