To enhance data quality, the survey incorporated an attention check similar to that employed by Shank et al. (2023). This check, which asked participants "Who was the composer of the music you just heard?", was administered prior to the music evaluation. Subsequently, participants were required to self-report the honesty and care of their responses on a five-point scale and to indicate whether they used headphones or speakers during the listening session. Importantly, participants were informed that their responses would neither influence the feedback provided nor affect their compensation. Figure 1 provides an overview of the study design. All participants were asked to assess their familiarity with the music excerpt and their enjoyment level on a scale from 1 (not at all) to 5 (very much). If a participant indicated a familiarity rating of 4 or higher, they were further asked whether the music evoked any specific memories of a person or experience. Moreover, participants in the "AI or human" condition were tasked with determining the origin of the music, indicating whether it was composed by AI or a human, and expressing their confidence on a 6-point scale, ranging from 1 (definitely AI) to 6 (definitely human). The emotional responses elicited by the music were evaluated using the Geneva Emotion Music Scale (GEMS; Zentner et al., 2008), which classifies emotions into three overarching categories: Sublimity (including Wonder, Tenderness, and Nostalgia), Vitality (such as Joy and Power), and Unease (such as Sadness and Tension). In this study, a modified version of the GEMS framework was employed, where each emotion was represented by a single label accompanied by several related terms. Participants rated each emotion on a scale ranging from 0 to 100, starting at 0, ensuring that the default value remained 0 if no selection was made. Furthermore, participants were given a field to input up to three additional emotions not covered by the GEMS framework and to specify the intensity of these emotions.
