In engineering practice, systems are increasingly designed with hierarchical topologies where supervisory agents mediate between human operators and worker agents. These supervisors evaluate outputs, identify discrepancies, and possess the authority to halt operations if thresholds are exceeded. This framework anticipates the "Institutional AI" paradigm, which is elaborated upon in subsequent sections. However, the mere presence of supervisors is insufficient. Supervisory agents operate using the same system framework as the worker agents they oversee, making them susceptible to similar challenges or feedback loops. Moreover, excessive consensus among supervisors can compromise decision-making, potentially resulting in collective errors or deliberate suboptimal outcomes. Therefore, effective management necessitates the implementation of hierarchical oversight structures, incorporating multiple supervisory layers, diverse verification mechanisms, redundancy in observational protocols, and a distinct delineation of authority among supervisory roles. With the advent of autonomous multi-agent systems, the singular focus on safety becomes increasingly inadequate. The reliance on a singular centralized framework or rigidly defined protocols is no longer sufficient; instead, there is a growing need for distributed networks capable of autonomously identifying, addressing, and resolving emergent challenges. When a single model encounters failure, its consequences are not isolated but rather propagate, impacting interconnected systems and agents. As the deployment of artificial intelligence systems in collaborative environments becomes more prevalent, it is imperative to ascertain the genesis of collective challenges and establish metrics for their assessment. The interaction of language models reveals the limitations of current frameworks in addressing risks such as bias, misuse, or weaknesses. While evaluating a single model in isolation renders potential errors more comprehensible, the complexity of interactions among models introduces failure modes that remain beyond our current understanding. We propose the Emergent Systemic Risk Horizon (ESRH) as a conceptual framework designed to address this challenge, offering a structured approach to understanding and mitigating systemic risks within complex interaction architectures. At this juncture, the collective dynamics of models begin to deteriorate, despite the individual validity of each model's behavior remaining intact.
