Galhotra et al. [2021] discuss the analytical determination of probabilities in situations where a fully specified system is unavailable. This approach holds particular significance as it leverages specific models to derive probabilistic bounds for addressing questions regarding alternative outcomes. Notably, this method facilitates a focus on critical components of models in the absence of complete knowledge, which can potentially result in narrower intervals compared to relying solely on mathematical calculations. Furthermore, methodologies that assess the necessity and sufficiency of certain features are considered as a means to enhance the interpretability of AI models. Assuming that the explanatory variables consist solely of independent input features, Mothilal et al. [2021] propose that the optimal explanation entails identifying the minimal subset of features that is both necessary and sufficient to achieve a specific outcome in a given context. They further argue that necessity is closely tied to contrastive explanations, as it involves identifying features whose changes are essential for altering the model's original output. Conversely, sufficiency pertains to feature attribution explanations, emphasizing features that consistently determine the outcome regardless of variations in other variables. Indeed, the comparison of different types of AI explanations reveals substantial variability in the practical performance of these methods. Similarly, Pawar et al. [2024], under the assumption of independent input features, explore necessity and sufficiency as evaluative criteria for existing explainable AI (XAI) methods. They extend this framework by investigating diverse approaches to neighborhood generation aimed at improving interpretability. In a related vein, Beckers [2022] examines scenarios in which predictive models generate outcomes based on observed actions, with the assumption that these models are causally meaningful and consistently accurate in their predictions. For such cases, sufficient explanations and counterfactual explanations are defined, with the latter focusing on alternative scenarios under the presumption of infallible model accuracy. Moreover, Beckers delineates the degrees of causal influence to identify the underlying factors that serve as valid explanations. Thus, analyzing both necessity and sufficiency, as well as causal influence, emerges as critical for advancing the interpretability and reliability of AI explanations.
