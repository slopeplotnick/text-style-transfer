Individuals frequently discussed their perceptions of AI-generated music, even in cases where explicit composer attribution was absent. These discussions often reflected concerns that align with debates within the music information retrieval and generative technology research communities, including the potential for negative economic consequences, ethical complexities surrounding authorship and copyright, and broader cultural implications (Holzapfel et al., 2018; Barnett, 2023). Moreover, a considerable interest was observed in the underlying mechanisms and functionalities of these emerging technologies, demonstrating how technological curiosity shapes public engagement. Taken together, these varied sentiments may help explain the mixed findings in the literature regarding composer bias in AI-music. Notably, perceptions of AI are not static; rather, they evolve alongside advancements in technology. As generative systems become more sophisticated, societal attitudes toward them may shift—from a fascination with innovation to perceiving these systems as potential challenges or threats to stability and trust. Therefore, perceptions of AI-composed music should be understood as dynamic and subject to change over time. This study, however, encountered several limitations. Firstly, the sample consisted primarily of university students and individuals recruited via Prolific, with participants engaging in the listening task through uncontrolled modalities such as headphones or speakers at self-determined volume levels. This sampling design complicates the generalizability of findings to broader populations. Additionally, participants recruited from Prolific may have possessed higher levels of technological literacy and familiarity with artificial intelligence, as noted in prior research (Shank et al., 2023). Secondly, framing the musical compositions as resembling "movie soundtracks" for one experimental cohort may have introduced unintentional bias, given the generally favorable reception of cinematic scores. Furthermore, the study exclusively utilized instrumental music, limiting the applicability of findings to compositions with lyrical content. Existing research has shown that lyrics can amplify the emotional intensity of sad songs, enhancing their perceived sadness, while exerting minimal or even diminishing effects on the emotional perception of happy songs (Ali and Peynicioglu, 2006; Barradas and Sakka, 2021; Brattico et al., 2011; Warmbrodt et al., 2022). Given the predominance of positively valenced music in this study, it is likely that the inclusion of lyrics would have had negligible or diminishing effects on listeners' emotional responses, assuming the lyrics were congruent and of comparable quality to human-composed material. Therefore, the decision to exclude lyrics was made to minimize potential confounding effects arising from lyrical semantics and prosody. This choice also reflected the limited availability of high-quality AI-generated vocal compositions at the time of stimulus creation.
