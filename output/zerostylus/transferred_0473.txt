Utilizing scikit-learn, we trained several classifiers, including Majority, Random, n-gram SVM, Logistic Regression, Random Forest, and Decision Tree, to establish baseline models. Features were extracted using TF-IDF and count-vector representations to enhance model input. While baseline models were trained on a CPU, evaluations of transformer models were conducted in a GPU-enabled environment, specifically using Google Colab T4, to leverage computational efficiency. The primary evaluation metric employed was the micro-averaged F1-score; however, accuracy, precision, and recall were also calculated to ensure a fair comparison across models. In our comprehensive analysis, we evaluated a diverse array of models, such as m-BERT cased, XLM-RoBERTa base, BanglaBERT, DistilBERT cased, in addition to the baseline models, to classify hate speech within the BLP2025 dataset. Table 2 provides a detailed classification report for subtask 1A. Notably, BanglaBERT achieved the highest accuracy of 0.71, thereby surpassing all other models in our evaluation. This model demonstrated superior performance with precision and recall values of 0.70 and 0.71, respectively. Thus, BanglaBERT excelled in terms of the official scoring metric for shared task 1a, achieving a micro-F1 score of 0.71. In comparison, the BERT multilingual base (m-BERT) achieved an F1 score of 0.66, while XLM-RoBERTa base achieved an F1 score of 0.67, both outperforming the baseline models. Conversely, DistilBERT attained an F1 score of 0.57, which was lower than the baseline methods, SVM and Logistic Regression. Among the baseline models, SVM exhibited the best performance with an F1 score of 0.61, whereas the random baseline demonstrated the lowest performance with an F1 score of 0.16.
