This study investigates the limitations of safety mechanisms designed for human-model interaction when applied to environments involving communication between large language models (LLMs). Current regulations emphasize safeguarding a singular model through the implementation of prompts, fine-tuning, and moderation. However, this approach may falter when addressing multi-model interactions due to its focus on individual model behavior. Although these configurations presume a single model engages with a user under consistent oversight, the assumptions and implications of such interactions remain unexamined. Meanwhile, research and industrial sectors are rapidly advancing toward ecosystems where models communicate with one another, utilizing each other's outputs as inputs within interconnected chains. Consequently, a critical discrepancy arises between the reliability of individual models and the robustness of the overall system. Therefore, this work proposes a shift from prioritizing individual model safety to addressing system-level safety considerations. We introduce the Emergent Systemic Risk Horizon (ESRH) as a conceptual framework to demonstrate that systemic issues arise from interactions between models, rather than isolated behavior of individual models. This paper elucidates (i) a theoretical framework concerning the risks associated with model interactions, (ii) a methodology to integrate various tiers of failure, and (iii) a framework for Institutional AI, which serves as a foundation for enhanced oversight in environments populated by numerous agents. Current safety methods rely on the oversight of a single model by humans, where inappropriate behavior can be mitigated through timely interventions, learning from feedback, or output regulation. Nevertheless, this approach becomes inadequate in complex settings involving multiple interacting models. This is significant, as it renders models more reliable and polite, albeit their effectiveness is limited to individual instances. While individual response mechanisms govern the behavior of a single model in relation to a specific input, they do not extend to the coordination or collective functionality of multiple models operating in tandem. As expansive models are increasingly integrated into tool-utilizing, agent-driven, and multi-agent environments, the concept of ensuring safety through a solitary model framework begins to deteriorate. In addressing this issue, we consider how individually effective models may inadvertently generate complications when interacting due to feedback loops, imitation, or unforeseen collaborative dynamics. Ultimately, this results in a critical divergence between ensuring individual model performance and maintaining systemic safety, where the adequacy of each component does not guarantee control over the entire network.
