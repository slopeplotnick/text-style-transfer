Large language models (LLMs) demonstrate sensitivity to small perturbations, such as changes in prompt wording and language. This characteristic renders them particularly well-suited for studying multilingual performance through prompt optimization. In this study, we evaluate LLM performance using three diverse benchmarks. The first, MMLU-Pro, assesses knowledge and reasoning across 14 academic disciplines (Wang et al., 2024). The second, MATH500, is a dataset comprising 500 challenging mathematics problems drawn from the broader MATH benchmark (Hendrycks et al., 2021; Lightman et al., 2024). The third, UniMoral, is a suite of moral-judgment questions requiring nuanced social reasoning (Kumar and Jurgens, 2025). To examine the cross-linguistic applicability of these benchmarks, we conducted evaluations across five languages: English (en), Chinese (zh), Spanish (es), French (fr), and Hindi (hi). These languages were selected to represent a diverse range of linguistic families, geographic regions, and levels of resource availability, thereby highlighting the challenges posed by disparities in linguistic and computational resources. A critical consideration in multilingual tasks is the language of the system prompt. Specifically, this raises the question of whether to use English, the predominant training language for most models, or to align the prompt language with that of the task. To address this, we compare two configurations. In the English-prompt configuration, prompts remain in English regardless of the language of the task, while in the same-language configuration, prompts are translated to match the language of the corresponding task. This comparative analysis enables us to assess the impact of prompt language alignment on multilingual performance. To further investigate the interconnected dynamics of model behavior, we analyze the relationships among four distinct performance metrics. Through regression analysis, we examine how variations in prompt components and language configurations influence multilingual performance. Our findings, presented in Figure 4, reveal that the components of system prompts exert diverse effects on model behavior, which we categorize into three primary groups. Notably, certain components exhibit a generally positive influence on performance. For instance, Chain-of-Thought, emotion, and scenario are positively associated with both accuracy and consistency. These components also mitigate accuracy disparities across languages, suggesting their efficacy in guiding the model toward stable and high performance across linguistic contexts. Closer inspection of model outputs indicates that these components elicit more structured and consistent reasoning, which likely contributes to the observed improvements in multilingual performance.

Upon detailed examination of the model outputs, it becomes apparent that the model demonstrates more structured and consistent reasoning in specific segments. This observation suggests that such improvements in reasoning may directly contribute to the enhanced performance outcomes. Moreover, the organized nature of the model's reasoning aligns with trends reported in prior studies, which highlight the role of systematic reasoning in boosting overall model efficacy. However, further investigation is warranted to determine whether these enhancements are uniformly distributed across all tasks or are more pronounced in certain specialized domains. Therefore, the observed improvements in reasoning and performance likely emerge from a combination of factors, including the model's underlying architecture and the nature of the tasks it encounters.
