The traditional approach to geophysical analysis involves initially performing a deterministic inversion, which subsequently informs the construction of a deterministic interpretation, as depicted in Figure 2. Following this, an expert geologist interprets the deterministic inversion by identifying shapes, such as intrusions, that are significant for exploration purposes. This deterministic methodology ensures that software consistently targets a singular model to facilitate these interpretations. Consequently, geophysical inversion precedes geological interpretation in this process. However, it can be argued that the role of regularization terms in imposing geological knowledge is both vague and limited in scope. Indeed, this approach may lead to a redundant application of the same geological knowledge: initially through the regularization term and then once more during interpretation. As a result, this process risks becoming a self-fulfilling prophecy fraught with circular reasoning. In contrast, the Bayesian methodology addresses both inversion and interpretation concurrently, treating them as a unified problem. Prior to any inversion, Bayesian analysis necessitates the consideration of plausible solutions, such as relevant geometries. The Bayesian inversion consequently offers relatively straightforward solutions that are both easily implemented and broadly applicable. Revisiting Figure 2, we can explore how to formulate a prior probability model. A critical initial question is: what baseline information about the subsurface is available prior to data acquisition? First, it is observed that petrophysical properties tend to fall within specific ranges, and knowledge of the lithologies involved can facilitate more precise delineation of these ranges. Second, it is recognized that the spatial variance of these properties is neither random nor constant over extensive areas. Understanding the formation of deposits is crucial, as it results in specific variations of these properties. Conceptually, the fundamental parameters include constraints on histograms and spatial correlations. Mathematically, this can be represented as a spatial covariance model, known in geostatistics as a variogram. However, uncertainty remains regarding the parameters of this variogram, such as the mean, ranges, and anisotropy, which could assume various values, provided they are not entirely random.
