The emergence of a distant target induces the character to swiftly transition into a running motion, thereby facilitating a more rapid approach. These behaviors emerge from the integration of a stationary motion setup with a straightforward task objective focused on target proximity, thus eliminating the need for a motion planner to dictate specific gait choices. SMP inherently allows the policy to adapt behaviors from the dataset, autonomously generating novel skills tailored to diverse tasks. For instance, as detailed in Section 8.3, SMP-trained agents in the Dodgeball task spontaneously develop adaptive behaviors, such as agile jumping and dodging maneuvers, akin to human strategies in dodgeball gameplay. Although these maneuvers were absent in the original dataset, their inclusion reveals strategies closely mimicking authentic human behavior. However, as demonstrated in Section 8.5, AMP exhibits suboptimal performance in this task. This inadequacy likely stems from the disparity between the required dodging skill and the motions within the dataset, which only encompass basic movement. Furthermore, the limitations of AMP policies using a static discriminator are not merely due to implementation choices but reflect a fundamental issue in the reapplication of adversarial methods. Such methods fail to generate natural movements or perform effectively, even within identical tasks. In contrast, SMP facilitates the utilization of a singular motion setup across various tasks and policies, thereby enhancing adaptability in different contexts. The results, illustrated in Figure 7, indicate that SMP demonstrates greater efficiency, consistently providing steady and beneficial guidance throughout the training process. In the domain of developing human and humanoid agents, the ability to rise after a fall emerges as a critical competency, supporting adaptive responses in dynamic environments. Challenges in acquiring this skill often arise due to supplementary rewards that constrain excessive movement. Nonetheless, AMP, when trained, naturally facilitates the development of motion setups that enable characters to autonomously learn methods for rising from a prone position. Equally, in a fixed motion setup, SMP fosters the development of a robust get-up skill effective from randomly fallen positions. As depicted in Figure 5(e), when the character is knocked down at a significant distance, it autonomously executes a sequence of actions, including rolling over, using its hands to push itself up, and ultimately regaining a standing position.
