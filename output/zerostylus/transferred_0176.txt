The system functions by enabling the mother agent to interact with other agents based on its existing grammatical framework. This represents a novel methodological approach, as computational models of language acquisition have traditionally relied on extensive textual datasets to facilitate learning processes (e.g., Alishahi and Stevenson, 2008; Alishahi and Chrupala, 2012; Conner et al., 2009; Matusevych et al., 2013). In this study, the term "language model" is employed in a broader sense, rather than being restricted to its conventional usage in natural language processing (NLP), where it typically refers to neural models designed for next-word prediction, such as GPT (Brown et al., 2020; Radford et al., 2018) or BERT (Devlin et al., 2019). Specifically, the term encompasses rule-based frameworks, statistical methods, and artificial neural networks, as all of these approaches contribute uniquely to addressing common NLP tasks. This distinction is critical, as the mother agent in our multi-agent system operates on explicit linguistic rules, while the language-acquiring agent employs statistical methods to infer a rule-based representation of linguistic phenomena, thereby highlighting a methodological contrast. The present investigation examines the MODOMA daughter agent's capacity to acquire and represent linguistic components, including functional and content categories, by leveraging a predefined Zipfian distribution embedded within the MODOMA architecture. More precisely, the study evaluates whether a multi-agent system can derive these linguistic components from synthetically generated sentences and encode them through a rule-based grammar. To address this research question, we first analyze whether the synthetically generated utterances exhibit statistical patterns analogous to those observed in human languages. Furthermore, we determine the minimum amount of input data required for the system to reliably detect such patterns. Subsequently, we explore whether these statistical regularities can be systematically translated into explicit grammatical rules. Finally, the evaluation process involves verifying the alignment of the inferred categories with the original grammatical framework and assessing the system's ability to autonomously acquire structured linguistic knowledge in an unsupervised manner.
