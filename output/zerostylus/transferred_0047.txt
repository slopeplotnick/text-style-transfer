Robustness and generalization remain significant challenges for visual learning algorithms (VLAs), particularly in dynamic and unpredictable environments. Despite the utilization of extensive and diverse training datasets, VLAs often fail to generalize effectively to out-of-distribution scenarios, exhibiting substantial degradation when exposed to novel camera perspectives, changes in lighting conditions, or perturbations in scene dynamics. Consequently, this brittleness limits their applicability in real-world settings where variability is inherent. To address these challenges, existing approaches can be broadly categorized into data-centric and representation-centric paradigms. Data-centric strategies primarily focus on increasing visual diversity, often leveraging large-scale, multi-view datasets such as Libero-Plus, which expose models to a wider range of visual contexts and perspectives. However, while this method improves robustness by introducing greater heterogeneity in training data, scaling data collection to naturalistic settings remains prohibitively costly and labor-intensive, thus posing practical limitations for continual adaptation. Conversely, representation-centric approaches aim to enhance robustness by improving geometric consistency, employing techniques such as multi-view supervision or specialized architectures designed to encode 3D spatial awareness. These methods strengthen viewpoint invariance by enabling VLAs to better accommodate variations in perspective. Nevertheless, despite their geometry-aware designs, representation-centric methods continue to exhibit sensitivity to extraneous visual factors, including complex backgrounds or illumination changes. Such limitations highlight the persistent gap between achieving viewpoint robustness and broader visual adaptability. Furthermore, prior work often presumes that improving robustness necessitates either increased data diversity or the refinement of 3D-consistent architectures. However, relatively few studies explicitly investigate whether these failures stem from deficiencies in spatial representation itself. To explore this hypothesis, recent frameworks propose decomposing VLAs into two distinct components: spatial modeling and physical modeling. Spatial modeling, performed by the visual encoder, constructs spatial relationships within the scene, including object positions, orientations, contact relations, and occlusion structures, thereby generating spatial representations required for downstream reasoning tasks. Physical modeling, on the other hand, integrates spatial information with task semantics, prior action histories, and contextual instructions to inform decision-making and produce executable action sequences. Notably, viewpoint shifts primarily affect the spatial configuration of the observed scene rather than the underlying task semantics or action dynamics. This suggests that performance degradation under viewpoint changes is predominantly attributable to misalignments within spatial modeling, while physical modeling components remain functionally robust. Consequently, while high-level reasoning and control capabilities persist, spatial distortions in visual embeddings disrupt coordination in downstream processes, underscoring the critical role of accurate spatial representation.

In our research, we analyze the impact of alterations in camera angles, observing that such changes primarily affect the spatial configuration of the scene while leaving the task's semantic interpretation and the execution of actions largely unaltered. This observation suggests that the decline in performance can be primarily attributed to misalignment in spatial modeling, although the efficacy of the physical modeling component remains intact. However, the model's cognitive and planning capabilities are preserved; it is the disruption in spatial information that leads to complications when executing actions based on this data. Consequently, addressing these issues necessitates a focus on correcting the spatial misalignment without altering the overall system architecture or requiring additional data. This approach aligns with recent advancements in parameter-efficient finetuning (PEFT), which enable models to adapt to new tasks with minimal changes to trainable parameters, thus maintaining efficiency and stability. As such, our research proposes a focus on lightweight adaptations of the visual pathway to enhance robustness against viewpoint changes, challenging the notion that global adaptations are necessary for maintaining performance.
