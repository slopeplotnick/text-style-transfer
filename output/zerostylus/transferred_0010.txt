Citation quality is assessed using standard metrics: Recall, Precision, and their harmonic mean, F1, to ensure a balanced evaluation. Recall evaluates the proportion of ground-truth references included in the citation set, reflecting the extent of literature coverage achieved. Precision, on the other hand, measures the accuracy of the citations provided by determining whether the references in the computer-generated survey are present in the human-curated list, thereby preventing the inclusion of arbitrary or fabricated sources. Citation alignment further evaluates the extent to which the references identified by the computational model correspond to those in the human-generated set, indicating its capacity to replicate human research standards. Comparative analyses reveal that the ability of computational systems to interpret literary texts can be juxtaposed against human performance, highlighting distinctions in interpretative frameworks and evaluative criteria. Consequently, the implementation of LLM-as-a-Judge is widely utilized for the evaluation of algorithmically generated text due to its scalability, consistency, and cost-effectiveness. However, this approach may lack the necessary rigor, particularly when applied to intricate tasks such as the administration of extensive surveys. Therefore, as detailed in our approach, we employ two configurations of LLM-as-a-Judge: one utilizing a human-authored survey to evaluate overall content quality, and another guided by predefined principles to assess the coherence of the outlined structure. To evaluate survey quality, we employ a structured setup in which the evaluation model is presented with a human-authored survey on the same topic alongside the computer-generated counterpart. This allows for a comparative analysis that enhances the quality of judgments, as the model is supplemented with insights derived from a human-conducted survey rather than relying solely on predefined guidelines. The configuration addresses several issues associated with solely adhering to guidelines. Initially, employing an expert example aids the model in avoiding the overvaluation of ambiguous or superficial content. Furthermore, to enhance the model's ability to identify key elements such as robust arguments and supporting evidence, we incorporate a comparison between the computer-generated survey and a detailed human-written counterpart, addressing potential limitations of relying solely on predefined guidelines. The use of a high-quality example mitigates the risk of assigning uniformly high scores to all systems, even when performance discrepancies exist. Ultimately, to facilitate a comprehensive comparison of systems, we employ an approach that allows for better identification of differences and an understanding of their strengths and areas for improvement in the development of surveys.
