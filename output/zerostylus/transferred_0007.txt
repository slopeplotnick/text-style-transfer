While model innovation continues to advance, practical deployments often depend on decisions that are frequently treated as peripheral in academic discussions. These choices include the definition of virality, the types of signals that are feasible to collect (e.g., textual versus numerical or metadata), and the selection of evaluation protocols that reflect real-world constraints, such as accessibility limitations, time restrictions, and class imbalances. This study adopts an evaluation-first perspective to address these design considerations. Specifically, we demonstrate, using two recent datasets, that decisions related to label design and input configuration (e.g., text-only compared to text augmented with additional signals) can be as impactful as modifications to the model architecture. To make these trade-offs explicit and reproducible, we provide structured and clearly detailed baselines. Although the focus of this work is not on developing novel models, we show that simpler architectures can achieve performance comparable to or even surpassing state-of-the-art multimodal and graph-based approaches on standardized benchmarks such as FAKENewsNET (Section 5). To this end, we examine two classification tasks within a defined experimental framework: (i) the detection of fake news and (ii) the prediction of early virality. A central question we address is which signals and definitions are most critical for achieving stable performance under realistic constraints. Our empirical analyses compare textual representations (e.g., sentence-level transformer embeddings) with lightweight numerical signals, such as user statistics, early engagement metrics, and temporal features. These distinct data types not only influence comparative evaluation outcomes but also shape interpretive frameworks and statistical correlation patterns. The evaluation is conducted on datasets with differing structures: one comprises self-contained news articles, while the other includes temporally ordered sequences of social media posts. This setup yields four experimental conditions across two supervised classification tasks: fake-news detection and virality prediction on the EvONS and FAKENewsNET datasets. For the task of fake-news detection, the goal is to assign a binary veracity label (fake or true) to a given item, which could be an article or a series of tweets. Experiments are conducted on both EvONS articles and FAKENewsNET tweet series, with performance evaluated using standard classification metrics. In the case of FAKENewsNET, tweet-series data are constructed from the repository, with instances corresponding to sequences of tweets referencing the same news article, effectively representing propagation dynamics. The dataset preparation process incorporates anonymization through identifier assignment and requires API hydration for data retrieval. While the Politifact split of FAKENewsNET was obtained via prior academic exchanges, the recovery of the GossipCop split was not possible due to current operational constraints. This limitation underscores the challenges associated with real-world data acquisition and highlights the importance of transparent and reproducible dataset construction practices.
