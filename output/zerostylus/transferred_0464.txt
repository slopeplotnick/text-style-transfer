Understanding real-world scientific laws is essential for accurately modeling physical outcomes across diverse scenarios. However, existing video benchmarks predominantly rely on commonsense physical reasoning, offering limited insights into video models' capabilities for scientific reasoning. To address this gap, we introduce VideoScience-Bench, a novel benchmark specifically designed to evaluate video models' understanding of undergraduate-level scientific concepts. Each test prompt encapsulates a composite scientific scenario, requiring the integration and reasoning of multiple scientific principles to generate accurate phenomena. The benchmark comprises 200 meticulously curated prompts spanning 14 topics and 103 concepts in physics and chemistry, ensuring comprehensive coverage of foundational scientific domains. To evaluate the performance of video models, we conduct expert-annotated assessments of seven state-of-the-art systems across T2V and I2V tasks using four dimensions: immutability, correct dynamism, spatio-temporal continuity, and phenomenon congruency. Notably, employing a visual language model (VLM) as a judge demonstrates strong alignment with human evaluations, indicating the reliability of this approach. To the best of our knowledge, VideoScience-Bench represents the first framework designed not only to assess video models' generative capabilities but also to evaluate their reasoning skills, requiring outputs to accurately reflect scientific phenomena consistent with physical and chemical principles. The corresponding data and evaluation code are publicly available at github.com/hao-ai-lab/VideoScience. Furthermore, advancements in video generation models, exemplified by the launches of Sora-2 and Veo-3.1, have transformed the landscape by delivering cinematic-quality videos and demonstrating unprecedented world-modeling capabilities. Consequently, the criteria for evaluating "good" video generation have evolved beyond traditional metrics, such as Fréchet Video Distance (FVD) and Fréchet Video Motion Distance (FVMD), toward assessing adherence to scientific principles and the ability to model real-world phenomena. Emerging models increasingly exhibit zero-shot reasoning abilities, enabling them to address tasks involving scientific, mathematical, and spatial reasoning without prior training. Human reasoners trained in science utilize a rich repertoire of principles to interpret natural phenomena and predict outcomes, such as the effectiveness of citric acid in removing iron stains or the behavior of an LED when powered with or without a resistor. This raises a critical question: can video models, beyond achieving visual fidelity and commonsense physical reasoning, accurately portray scientific phenomena and demonstrate robust scientific reasoning? True scientific world modeling necessitates the integration of multiple interacting concepts, enabling predictions of real-world processes. This leads us to our fundamental question: beyond merely adhering to aesthetic appeal and basic physical principles, do video models possess the capability to accurately depict scientific phenomena and engage in scientific reasoning concerning the empirical world? Understanding the world through scientific principles is vital for fostering discovery, as it allows models to predict the outcomes of real-world phenomena effectively. Scientific comprehension necessitates the integration of interconnected concepts; for instance, the principles of specific heat and heat transfer illustrate water's capacity to absorb heat, thereby enhancing the modeling of real-world processes. Consequently, unlike commonsense-based world modeling, authentic scientific world modeling involves reasoning across multiple interacting concepts. Moreover, this integration of scientific principles plays a significant role in advancing discovery by enabling models to anticipate the outcomes of real-world processes accurately.
