Recent advancements in the development and optimization of large language models (LLMs) have significantly enhanced their cognitive capabilities, thereby raising expectations for their ability to provide accurate, truthful, and reliable information. However, emerging evidence suggests that these enhanced reasoning capabilities may inadvertently lead to belief entrenchment, where models persist in adhering to their prior assumptions rather than revising their beliefs based on new evidence. To address this issue, this study introduces a novel methodological framework grounded in statistical principles, specifically leveraging the Martingale property, to systematically evaluate the extent to which LLMs exhibit this phenomenon of belief entrenchment. The Martingale property, rooted in Bayesian reasoning, implies that under a rational belief updating process, the expected value of future beliefs should remain consistent with current beliefs, thereby precluding the possibility of predicting future updates solely based on present convictions. Building on this principle, we propose the Martingale Score, an unsupervised, regression-based metric designed to detect violations of this property, which signal a failure to appropriately update beliefs. Through comprehensive evaluations conducted across diverse problem domains—including event forecasting, opinion-based question answering, and academic paper review—we identified frequent instances where models exhibited belief entrenchment, consistently favoring their pre-existing beliefs. This trend was observed across various models, reasoning strategies, and domains, highlighting the pervasive nature of this issue. Moreover, we systematically analyzed the cognitive frameworks and domains most susceptible to this effect, finding that models employing iterative reasoning techniques, such as chain-of-thought prompting, were particularly prone to entrenchment. Notably, we validated the utility of the Martingale Score by demonstrating its predictive capability in domains where ground truth labels are available. Even in regions lacking predefined answers, the Martingale Score proved effective as a proxy for evaluating the truth-seeking capacity of LLM reasoning processes. This is especially significant as it underscores the score's potential to operate as an unsupervised metric for assessing factual accuracy. To contextualize this phenomenon, one might consider the analogy of a stubborn individual who refuses to acknowledge their errors or a ruler who exclusively listens to agreeable subordinates—both scenarios exemplify the detrimental consequences of failing to update beliefs in light of new information. Similarly, human beings often exhibit belief entrenchment, whether due to cognitive biases, such as confirmation bias [Klayman, 1995], or external influences. Consequently, our findings underscore the critical need to design more adaptive reasoning frameworks that mitigate the propensity for belief entrenchment in LLMs.
