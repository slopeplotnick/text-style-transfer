In recent years, brain-computer interfaces (BCIs) have garnered significant attention as mechanisms that facilitate direct interaction between the brain and external devices or digital systems, attracting interest from scholars, medical professionals, and media outlets alike. These interfaces can be categorized into invasive, requiring surgical intervention, and non-invasive, which do not necessitate surgery. BCIs are multidisciplinary systems designed to assist individuals with severe motor or speech impairments, such as those caused by locked-in syndrome, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), spinal cord injuries, or strokes, in regaining communication capabilities or mobility that would otherwise remain unattainable. Consequently, the enhancement of non-invasive BCIs through the analysis of a broader spectrum of features derived from EEG data has become a critical area of focus. The efficacy of feature extraction techniques is pivotal for EEG data analysis, allowing for a concentrated examination of fundamental aspects such as temporal or frequency measures, or the employment of sophisticated methodologies that integrate multiple dimensions. Accurate classification of EEG signals and reliable system functionality rely heavily on extracting meaningful information from raw EEG data, employing time-domain statistics, frequency-domain features such as power spectral density, and hybrid approaches like wavelet transforms. Moreover, an equally significant consideration is the development of features capable of generalizing effectively across individuals, given the substantial variability inherent in EEG signals between subjects. Addressing temporal data variations and inter-dataset discrepancies through transfer learning and domain generalization techniques is imperative to ensuring feature reliability. However, determining the optimal combinations of feature extraction and classification techniques for EEG signal analysis remains challenging, particularly given the substantial variability in individuals' neural patterns. To address this challenge, contemporary research frameworks necessitate the application of evaluation methodologies specifically designed to mitigate the risk of overly optimistic outcomes, including participant-independent validation strategies and robust performance metrics.

To ensure robust evaluation, it is crucial to incorporate several methodological considerations. First, assessing data from subjects not included in the training phase, such as through leave-one-subject-out cross-validation, is essential. This approach allows for a more generalizable evaluation of the model's performance. Moreover, it is important to employ metrics that effectively address data imbalances, such as balanced accuracy and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC). Furthermore, validating outcomes with statistical tests that make minimal assumptions about the data ensures robustness in the results. Additionally, it is imperative to prevent data leakage, which can otherwise compromise the integrity of the evaluation process. In this context, any processes such as data filtering or normalization must be applied exclusively to the training data. Extending these operations to the test data would, however, undermine the validity of the evaluation, as it would introduce bias and confound the results.
