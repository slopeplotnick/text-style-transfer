Empirical analyses indicate that the sequence in which articles are presented to the model influences the resultant summaries; specifically, there is a discernible inclination towards the initial article, thereby affecting both the narrative construct and semantic alignment. Prior research has documented position bias in models when processing long contextual inputs. Our findings extend these observations to the multi-document context, demonstrating that models exhibit a propensity to allocate greater attention to initial segments, consequently diminishing focus on subsequent portions. Notably, BERTScore reveals a recurrent primacy effect across various analytical stances. This effect arises because the models preferentially adopt the vocabulary and argumentative structure from the first-seen document, thereby producing higher similarity scores for the initial article. Conversely, metrics such as ROUGE-L, which evaluates summaries based on word overlap, and SummaC, which verifies factual alignment without concentrating on content framing, show diminished sensitivity to this bias compared to BERTScore. Consequently, the initial article is distinctly favored over subsequent ones, with minimal distinction observed between the latter articles. The preference for the first article is not due to a diminished interest in subsequent articles but rather due to the sequencing of inputs. Such bias can significantly impact practical applications, particularly in tools designed to summarize information for users. For instance, Gemini models employed by Google Search tend to assign greater significance to earlier processed articles, often disregarding those introduced later in the sequence. This inclination to prioritize particular perspectives can notably influence individuals' comprehension of the subject matter, especially when substantial trust is placed in AI-generated outputs.
