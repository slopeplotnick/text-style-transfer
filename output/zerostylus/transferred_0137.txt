Predictions can be formulated utilizing machine learning methodologies, such as neural networks or generative AI techniques. Moreover, when conducting planning activities under uncertain conditions, practitioners can employ Monte Carlo Tree search algorithms to effectively address the challenges posed by such uncertainty. The fundamental components of AI encompass prediction and decision-making processes. Before delving into the specifics of these methods, I will elucidate upon their utilization in the context of exploration. To generate forecasts, it is essential to utilize datasets. The principal challenge in mineral exploration lies in the creation and organization of datasets, rather than in the selection of machine learning methodologies. These datasets often exhibit considerable complexity, characterized by numerous variables, three-dimensional structures, or extreme values. Such complexities are infrequently encountered in other domains, albeit to a lesser degree. However, challenges in data organization are frequently overlooked, notably the inconsistency in data scaling and the inadvisability of aggregating all data into a singular dataset. This diminishes efficacy in specific contexts where determining suitable from unsuitable applications is essential. Ground truth data, such as core samples, is frequently scaled to align with geophysical data. It is apparent that geophysical and geochemical datasets are occasionally integrated in manners that lack geological coherence. Subsequently, following the development of machine learning models within a specific domain, it is common for these models to be implemented in alternative domains without a thorough evaluation to ascertain their appropriateness. Such errors contribute to an increase in false positives, thereby undermining the perceived reliability of AI systems. However, the fundamental concern lies in the methodologies employed for data management. If variance differences across scales are recognized, it is essential to acknowledge that variance in data varies according to the scale at which it is measured. Consequently, when interpolated, geophysical data inherently exhibits reduced variance compared to the original measurements. Moreover, a process is employed where drilling data, initially recorded on a small scale, is systematically scaled up or adjusted to facilitate its comparison with geophysical data. This issue is exacerbated by the artificial correlation introduced between the labels and predictors due to the data handling methods employed. In light of these challenges, I will examine the prevalence of false positives in current maps used for assessing mineral potential or prospecting.
