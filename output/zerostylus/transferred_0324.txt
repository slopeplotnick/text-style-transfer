Natural Language Processing (NLP) [1] traces its theoretical origins to the work of Swiss linguist Ferdinand de Saussure, who argued that the meaning of language arises from the relational and differential connections among words. Furthermore, shared linguistic structures enable individuals to engage in effective communication. Building on such foundational ideas, in 1950, Alan Turing introduced the concept of a "thinking machine," proposing that a machine capable of conversing with humans via a teleprinter could demonstrate cognitive abilities. In modern applications, NLP has evolved into a crucial tool for interpreting human language and generating contextually appropriate responses. For example, intelligent assistants like Apple's Siri and Amazon's Alexa exemplify autonomous systems that integrate advanced algorithms to interact with users and perform tasks independently. In addition to these applications, the advent of Large Language Models (LLMs) [2] marks a significant milestone in artificial intelligence. These sophisticated models, developed using deep learning methodologies, are trained on extensive datasets and demonstrate the capacity to produce text closely resembling human-authored content. Moreover, LLMs leverage transformer architecture, which enables them to capture intricate linguistic nuances and establish connections across large textual passages. As a result, these models provide advanced functionalities such as machine translation, conversational interaction, and content generation. Consequently, LLMs have proven valuable across diverse domains, ranging from academic research to industrial applications. A notable example of this technology is OpenAI's ChatGPT [3], which has gained widespread usage in everyday contexts. However, despite their remarkable capabilities, language models occasionally exhibit errors known as "hallucinations" [4]. These errors occur when the model generates information that appears plausible but is factually incorrect, particularly in scenarios it has not encountered during training. Such inaccuracies can have varying consequences, from minor inconsistencies that confuse users to critical errors in sensitive fields such as translation, programming, and autonomous system operations. Therefore, addressing hallucinations is essential for ensuring the reliability, safety, and effectiveness of these models, especially in contexts where precision is paramount. To mitigate this issue, fine-tuned models [3] have emerged as an effective solution. These models are pre-trained on large datasets and subsequently optimized for specific task domains through targeted training on smaller, domain-specific datasets. During the fine-tuning process, model parameters are systematically adjusted to enhance task-specific performance and generalization capabilities. This approach not only leverages the foundational knowledge acquired during initial training but also refines the model's ability to address specialized challenges with greater accuracy.

The pre-trained models initially undergo task-specific modifications, wherein smaller, domain-focused datasets are employed to facilitate superior performance compared to general-purpose counterparts. Subsequently, the fine-tuning process requires meticulous adjustments to the model’s parameters. This careful calibration enhances the model’s accuracy and augments its capacity for generalization. Moreover, as illustrated in the initial phase, the model’s foundational linguistic proficiency, acquired during preliminary training, is progressively refined, thus enabling it to execute specialized tasks with increased precision and efficacy.
