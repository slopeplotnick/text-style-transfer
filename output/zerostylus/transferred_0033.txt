Our findings indicate that integrating prompt-engineered LLMs with Isolation Forest scores yields superior performance compared to traditional JETs and ML methods by effectively identifying anomalies and providing explanations in straightforward language. However, challenges persist regarding false alarms, implementation costs, and practical applicability. This research demonstrates the potential of AI-driven auditing to enhance both accuracy and interpretability. Significantly, we present the first demonstration that LLMs can effectively engage with tax ledger data, a critical area where clarity and reliability are paramount. Our findings also suggest that integrating LLMs with conventional anomaly signals offers an effective means to achieve a balance of precision, recall, and interpretability. Future investigations will expand upon these findings by examining larger and more diverse datasets, assessing efficacy across varying prompts and model modifications. The evaluation was conducted using a single anonymized tax ledger alongside a synthetic benchmark. It is crucial to acknowledge that ledgers exhibit variability across industries, systems, and regions, rendering the acquisition of accurate labels a challenging endeavor. We assess the impact of biases in fake labels generated by JETs and analyze how simulators establish fake anomalies. While further validation is required with larger and diverse datasets as well as authentic audit labels, this investigation underscores the necessity of proving the efficacy of the proposed method. Moreover, the stability and clarity of the model are contingent upon the phrasing employed and the specific model selected, with minor alterations potentially impacting outcomes and leading to discrepancies among differing models. Although our AuditCopilot design mitigates these discrepancies, it is not impervious to variations in phrasing, challenging inputs, or updates to the model. Plain-language explanations, while often considered useful by auditors, may not consistently align with established decision rules. Preliminary evaluations suggest that the application of LLMs in financial audits demonstrates promise; however, significant challenges remain, including susceptibility to errors, inherent biases, prompt design limitations, and the potential instability introduced by shifts in model weights. It is crucial to underscore that the implementation of our method should aid in decision-making processes, yet it necessitates human oversight, regulatory frameworks, and guidelines prior to actual application.
