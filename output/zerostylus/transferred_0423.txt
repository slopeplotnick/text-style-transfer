The model's training distribution encompasses both single-speaker inputs and multi-user interactions. However, when interleaved content from multiple speakers is aggregated without clear demarcation, it introduces greater contextual complexity and deviates from typical training paradigms. This structural incongruity, compounded by extended input lengths, can lead the model to disproportionately focus on early salient cues while neglecting broader contextual information due to instability in processing long-context inputs. Nevertheless, with task-specific supervision and systematic fine-tuning, our Menta model demonstrates robust performance across a range of binary and multi-level mental health classification tasks, including stress detection, depression severity assessment, and suicidal ideation and risk evaluation. This capability highlights Menta’s effectiveness in addressing diverse mental health outcomes within a unified and efficient framework. Moreover, Menta exhibits competitive performance relative to significantly larger language models, particularly excelling in depression and stress-related tasks, where it occasionally surpasses its larger counterparts. These findings underscore the practical potential of well-optimized small language models (SLMs) for continuous mental health monitoring, offering a cost-effective and high-performing solution for early detection in digital mental health applications. In this study, we address three key questions pertaining to the use of SLMs and our fine-tuned Menta model in predicting mental health outcomes. Specifically, we examine whether SLMs can effectively predict mental health status, while simultaneously identifying current limitations and outlining directions for future research. Our findings indicate that SLMs exhibit substantial capabilities in predicting mental health conditions from text-based social media data. Despite their smaller parameter sizes, the well-trained Menta model successfully handles complex classification tasks with high levels of performance, thereby demonstrating its potential to deliver accurate mental health predictions. This makes it particularly well-suited for deployment in low-resource settings or scenarios constrained by limited data availability. While previous research conducted by Jia et al. (2025) explored models within the 2–7 billion parameter range for mental health applications, our study specifically focuses on models strictly smaller than 7 billion parameters. Notably, our results reveal that models below approximately 3.8 billion parameters (e.g., those with around 1 billion parameters) consistently fail to perform adequately, often producing blank or arbitrary outputs in zero- or few-shot settings. This suggests that scaling down language models below a certain parameter threshold results in a sharp decline in task performance rather than a gradual degradation, thereby indicating the existence of a critical lower-bound capacity limit. Our work explicitly identifies this threshold within the context of mental health prediction, providing valuable insights into the parameter scaling dynamics in this domain.

The observation that reducing language model sizes beyond a certain threshold leads to a pronounced deterioration in task performance, rather than a gradual decline, thereby indicating a distinct lower boundary, is consistent with prior findings (Sengupta et al., 2025; Subramanian et al., 2025). Our findings specifically delineate this constraint within the realm of mental health prognostication. This aligns with the work of Shi et al. (2025), where the MentalQLM (0.5B) demonstrated robust capabilities in predicting various mental health outcomes, including depression, stress, and suicidal ideation. These results underscore the viability of fine-tuned smaller language models (SLMs) for mental health prediction, as they offer a favorable trade-off between computational efficiency and predictive accuracy. Furthermore, consistent with recent studies (Ren et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2024a), we observe that core reasoning and question-answering abilities are preserved post-fine-tuning, enabling these models to serve as multi-functional tools with superior performance in mental health tasks. In summary, our experiments reveal that SLMs within the 3-7B parameter range provide an optimal balance between efficiency and predictive performance when fine-tuned. While smaller models falter in generalization or recognizing task constraints, those within this size range proficiently classify psychological signals in both zero-shot and few-shot settings. With targeted fine-tuning, our model, Menta, establishes a clear lower bound for model size, achieving robust performance on complex mental health tasks while maintaining computational efficiency. This balance between model size, accuracy, safety, and real-world impact is crucial in mental health AI. When comparing the performance of SLMs and larger LLMs, fine-tuned SLMs exhibit comparable or superior results in depression and stress level classification tasks, albeit with lower performance in suicidal ideation prediction tasks. However, compared to non-trained SLMs, there is a significant improvement in performance, along with a more balanced accuracy across prediction tasks and classes. Our findings indicate that well-optimized lightweight SLMs can retain the core reasoning and contextual understanding necessary for mental health inference. The competitiveness of SLMs in this domain primarily arises because mental health language tasks often depend on localized lexical, affective, and syntactic cues—such as expressions of self-reference, emotional polarity, and temporal framing—which can be effectively captured by moderate-sized models without necessitating the extensive world knowledge encoded in larger language models (LLMs). As demonstrated in prior work (Ji et al., 2021; Shen et al., 2018; Benton et al., 2017), fine-tuning on domain-specific corpora substantially narrows the performance gap between smaller and larger architectures. Our findings extend this evidence by showing that parameter-efficient fine-tuning approaches, such as LoRA, combined with adaptive, weighted loss functions, enable SLMs to learn discriminative representations for mental health prediction at a fraction of the computational cost.
