The feasibility of large language models (LLMs) for addressing complex optimization challenges is demonstrated through systematic progression in task decomposition and allocation optimization. Initially, long-horizon construction tasks are decomposed into manageable subtasks using object-centric and skill-centric strategies adapted from the SMART-LLM framework (Kannan et al., 2024). Specifically, the object-centric approach identifies task components based on physical objects and their required manipulations, while the skill-centric approach organizes subtasks according to robot-specific capabilities, such as precision handling, force application, or careful maneuvering. These subtasks are then assigned to heterogeneous robots through capability-aware matching, ensuring alignment between individual robot specializations and task requirements. Furthermore, the LTAA framework employs LangGraph orchestration, which integrates probabilistic success modeling to optimize task completion likelihood by computing robot-specific success rates for each subtask. However, initial evaluations revealed workload imbalances across robot teams, wherein the pure emphasis on success-probability maximization led to overutilization of high-capability robots and underutilization of specialized units. Consequently, this observation informed the development of phase-adaptive allocation strategies. These strategies dynamically adjust decision priorities throughout project progression, transitioning from an early-phase focus on success rates to a later-phase emphasis on workload equity. Thus, the approach balances overall mission success while fostering equitable resource utilization across the system. The methodology for this development unfolded through three stages, as illustrated in Fig. 1. First, the implementation of the SMART-LLM framework assessed the feasibility of LLMs for multi-robot task allocation (MRTA), but revealed significant challenges, including validation inconsistencies and LLM output reliability issues. Second, the Self Corrective-Agent Architecture was introduced to address these deficiencies through multi-stage validation and hierarchical retry mechanisms. Finally, the LTAA framework integrated the validation principles from earlier stages with phase-adaptive strategies, enabling systematic benchmarking against traditional optimization methods. The authors further delineate the implementation of the SMART-LLM framework (Kannan et al., 2024), highlighting fundamental challenges encountered, such as validation inconsistencies, coordination limitations, and reliability concerns. In response, they developed the Self Corrective-Agent LangGraph Architecture, which employs multi-stage validation and hierarchical retry mechanisms to effectively resolve these issues. This iterative development process culminated in the proposed LTAA framework, which was rigorously evaluated using the TEACH dataset as the standard benchmark. Comparative analysis against Q-learning, DQN, and dynamic programming (DP) baselines under identical task scenarios demonstrated the framework’s effectiveness. Evaluation criteria included workload balance distribution, feature-specific performance across task types, and reasoning quality assessment, thereby validating both allocation efficiency and overall system reliability.

To achieve this objective, the study benchmarks performance by comparing it against conventional optimization techniques. Specifically, the LTAA framework evaluation employs the TEACH dataset, which serves as the standard benchmark for direct comparisons with Q-learning, DQN, and DP under equivalent task conditions. This standardized approach ensures a fair evaluation of the LTAA framework's capabilities. The analysis prioritizes three critical aspects: the equilibrium of task distribution, the execution of tasks based on predefined features, and the depth of reasoning required. These focus areas collectively assess both resource allocation efficiency and overall performance outcomes. By addressing these dimensions, the evaluation provides a comprehensive understanding of the LTAA framework's effectiveness in comparison to traditional optimization methods.
