To ensure statistical comparability in experiments, it is essential that independently collected study samples demonstrate dimensional invariance. To evaluate this, Multigroup-CFA was utilized to treat the two datasets—real-world and simulated—as independent groups, introducing progressively restrictive constraints to test for measurement invariance. This analysis was systematically divided into four stages: testing for configural invariance (H2.1), metric invariance (H2.2), scalar invariance (H2.3), and residual invariance (H2.4). Specifically, confirmation of configural invariance would indicate that the underlying pattern of factor loadings remains consistent across groups. Moreover, establishing metric invariance would allow the comparison of variances among latent constructs. Similarly, verifying scalar invariance would enable the comparison of latent mean scores, while residual invariance would permit the use of observed mean scores for further analyses (Putnick & Bornstein, 2016; Vandenberg & Lance, 2000). Collectively, achieving high levels of measurement invariance between the real-world and simulated datasets would suggest that the simulated data reliably approximate real-world data, thereby supporting their use in in silico studies. Building on this framework, we hypothesized that the responses from the real-world dataset and the simulated dataset would exhibit substantial agreement (H3). Specifically, we posited that there would be a strong correlation coefficient (H3.1) and high intraclass correlation (ICC) (H3.2) between the datasets. Furthermore, we hypothesized that the distributions of scores from the two datasets would not differ significantly (H4). To test this, we first employed a Mann-Whitney U test to assess differences in mean scores, as illustrated in Figure 5. Subsequently, we applied a Kolmogorov-Smirnov test to evaluate whether the distributional properties of the simulated data aligned with those of the real-world data. These analyses were designed to determine whether the simulated data share the same statistical properties as the empirical data, thereby validating their utility for research purposes.
