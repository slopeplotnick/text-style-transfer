Evaluating the energy consumption of large language models (LLMs) fundamentally differs from assessing their throughput or latency. Unlike traditional performance metrics, power consumption is influenced by three primary factors: the variety of hardware components employed, the complexity of the software applications used, and the manner in which tasks are executed, all of which vary significantly based on the operational context. Current evaluations of LLMs, however, do not adequately accommodate these variations, as they predominantly adhere to predetermined configurations, standardized model dimensions, and costly methodologies. As illustrated in Figure 1, TokenPowerBench addresses these challenges through a three-layered framework: initialization, execution and evaluation, and report generation. This framework facilitates a systematic and replicable evaluation of LLM energy consumption in contemporary settings. To assist in setting up the test environment, TokenPowerBench enables users to select the model, specify the runtime engine, and determine the prompts to be employed. Drawing from these considerations, TokenPowerBench is structured to facilitate ease of use through its tripartite architecture: a model pool that allows selection from any supported LLM, a prompt-dataset menu offering options such as Alpaca, LongBench, or user-defined prompts, and an inference-engine picker that includes vLLM, TensorRT-LLM, Transformers, or DeepSpeed. The model pool comprises two components: a design variation component, which includes models with differing architectural choices, and an evaluation component, which examines the impact of these design variations on power consumption. Moreover, various model architectures are explored, such as standard transformers exemplified by the LLaMA series and Mixture-of-Experts models exemplified by Mixtral, providing an analysis of their distinct approaches to parameter activation and memory utilization. The evaluation encompasses a range of model sizes, from smaller models with fewer than 1 billion parameters that can operate on a standard GPU, to larger models, such as Llama 3-405B, which require multiple GPUs or nodes. This analysis facilitates an understanding of how power consumption evolves as model architectures and hardware configurations scale in complexity. Additionally, details on the length and style of prompts, as well as their impact on power consumption during inference, are provided. For illustration, TokenPowerBench's support for two primary datasets is assessed: Alpaca, which features concise chat-based prompts, and Long-Bench, designed to handle extended contexts spanning up to 10,000 tokens. Furthermore, users have the capability to incorporate their own datasets, formatted in CSV or other compatible structures.
