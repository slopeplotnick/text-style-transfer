This paper undertakes an examination of a significant issue by proposing an innovative framework for conceptualizing safety. It argues that safety should not be construed solely as a characteristic of individual models; rather, it should be reinterpreted as an emergent property arising from the interactions and dynamics of interconnected systems. Consequently, understanding systemic instability necessitates a shift in focus toward interaction architectures, with the introduction of the Emergent Systemic Risk Horizon (ESRH) serving as a conceptual framework to elucidate the conditions under which systems may become unstable. The paper systematically elucidates this concept, beginning with an analysis of the dynamics between two entities and progressing to the cooperative function of multiple agents. Moreover, it examines various risk classifications and culminates in the proposal of Institutional AI as a framework for designing systems proficient at mitigating these risks. Current language models are designed to ensure safety by emphasizing human interaction elements, incorporating mechanisms such as prompts and filters to prevent harmful content and enforce compliance with established guidelines. However, achieving this presupposes that the model's behavior remains transparent and relatively stable. Language models exhibit complexity, influenced by factors such as their internal states, prior interactions, and the contexts in which they are employed. These variations demonstrate how subtle shifts in context can lead to divergent outcomes, even when adherence to apparent rules is maintained. This issue becomes more pronounced in scenarios where models begin to interact with one another. In addressing this, it is observed that the output of one model serves as the input for another, potentially establishing feedback loops that exacerbate minor issues. This underscores the variation in outcomes on complex assessments like JAILJUDGE, where the interaction of multiple models yields divergent results compared to those obtained from isolated model evaluations. Consequently, systems may emerge where, despite individual models being designed for fairness, their interactions can yield inequitable outcomes. This phenomenon reveals a critical discrepancy between the efficacy of individual components and the overarching safety of the integrated system. Therefore, it underscores the necessity of adopting a system-centric perspective to enhance understanding of communicative and interactive dynamics.
