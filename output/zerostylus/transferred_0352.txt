In this context, we begin by examining the deficiencies in the recent approach [16]. As illustrated in Figure 1(a), the model initially attends to multimodal inputs, such as audio and visual information. However, over time, its focus shifts predominantly toward the text it has already generated. Consequently, the model's subsequent reasoning is influenced by the emotional nuances present in the preliminary output it generates. Moreover, the process typically starts with video description, as the training data are structured to prioritize visual elements, followed sequentially by auditory information and reasoning tasks. However, by examining instances where a "visual-first" perspective proves inadequate, we highlight cases where emotional resonance is driven primarily by auditory elements or the semantic content of the language. In situations where the initial visual reasoning is flawed or deemed irrelevant, subsequent reasoning increasingly deviates from correctness, as depicted in Figure 1(b). Therefore, the initial step proves to be exceedingly significant as it affects reasoning coherence in various domains, where early indications misdirect logical processes [17]. To address this issue, we devised the Modality-Importance-Guided Reasoning (MIGR) framework. By initiating the reasoning process with the modality, whether audio, visual, or both, that optimally aligns with the targeted emotion, we maintain consistency of reasoning with that emotion throughout the analysis. Furthermore, by integrating a mechanism known as Modality Importance (MI), we ascertain which input type holds the greatest significance for emotion recognition. By evaluating the model's predictive accuracy across conditions where it has access to audio alone, visuals alone, or both modalities, we determine its effectiveness in emotion prediction. Through the analysis of the model's performance across different modality combinations, such as audio alone versus audio-visual inputs, we observe that visual cues may either contribute minimally to emotion recognition or introduce confusion when integrated. This observation underscores the necessity of prioritizing audio fidelity as the primary focus.

Our proposed methodology involves a two-step learning framework that integrates the Mutual Information (MI) concept with contemporary reasoning model training techniques [15, 16]. This framework entails modality-aligned Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) followed by modality-aligned reward optimization. Importantly, the SFT phase employs MI to segment the reasoning text according to input typology, which is subsequently followed by a systematic rearrangement of the segments. Furthermore, this approach contrasts with existing methods such as CoT2 (Gozeten et al., 2025) and CODI (Shen et al., 2025b), which utilize distinct frameworks, such as token mixtures for logical reasoning tasks and teacher-student distillation frameworks to enhance performance on GSM8k datasets respectively. Additionally, Token Assorted (Su et al., 2025) advances new token embeddings using a VQ-VAE, training Llama 3.1 8B for math and logical reasoning tasks. It is noteworthy that our LiteReason approach differs from these methods in several key aspects. Firstly, unlike approaches reliant on extensive datasets like GSM8k-Aug, we exclusively employ traces generated from the model itself due to the absence of such datasets for our tasks. Moreover, we focus solely on training a lightweight Reasoning Projector for latent reasoning rather than the entire model, hypothesizing that extensive training of the base-LLM for token-embedding generation may destabilize the model's token-generation capabilities. Furthermore, the interleaving of latent and discrete reasoning is permitted, with the hypothesis that the model will learn to balance them effectively during RL training. Although many approaches incorporate RL and compare against Chain-of-Thought prompting, to our knowledge, none assess final performance against a non-latent RL-trained model. We propose this baseline as a more realistic point of comparison for challenging tasks, thus including it in our experiments as an upper bound on performance and computational requirements. In addition, despite the focus of reasoning work on domains such as math and coding, substantial research applies LLMs to narrative understanding and generation tasks (Mostafazadeh et al., 2016; Fan et al., 2018; Huot et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2022, 2023; Huot et al., 2024; Xie and Riedl, 2024; Chhun et al., 2022; Karpinska et al., 2024; Sprague et al., 2023). These studies often prompt models to undertake specific sub-tasks, such as planning plot events or generating character details (Huot et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2023, 2022), thereby eliciting distinct reasoning styles. However, adapting these tasks to the RL domain presents challenges due to their lack of verifiable rewards. Consequently, this work concentrates on two specific tasks designed to evaluate narrative-based reasoning.
