Language comprehension is a sequential process in which understanding is incrementally developed through various stages. Initially, this process involves perceiving speech, followed by the identification of phonemes, which are synthesized into words. Next, grammatical structures are utilized to derive the meanings of these words, facilitating comprehension of the entire sentence. This study particularly examines our comprehension of language after word recognition, a sophisticated cognitive process unique to humans, and contrasts it with how advanced artificial intelligence systems manage language. The brain processes language hierarchically; simpler tasks like word prediction occur in lower strata, whereas more complex tasks, such as comprehending abstract concepts, are handled in higher strata. Furthermore, language acquisition in the brain involves the iterative refinement of predictions based on error detection, similar to the learning mechanisms employed in artificial intelligence systems. Thus, the methodology for language prediction and comprehension serves as a shared computational approach between the brain and artificial intelligence. Google's Neural Machine Translation (GNMT) system exemplifies this approach through its structured process, comprising three fundamental components: the encoder, attention mechanism, and decoder. The encoder sequentially processes the input sentence and synthesizes it into a signal that encapsulates the semantic content. This model includes eight layers, culminating in a final layer that synthesizes preceding elements to generate a unified signal representing the sentence's meaning. The attention unit assesses the significance of each word in the source sentence, transmitting this prioritized information to the decoder. Consequently, the decoder, operating layer by layer, predicts the subsequent word in the translation, thereby constructing the output representation step by step. Through supervised learning, the system incrementally refines its translation accuracy by comparing its outputs with human-generated ones and adjusting its parameters in response to discrepancies. In contrast, GPT-2 functions through an alternative mechanism, lacking distinct components akin to GNMT. Instead, it predicts the subsequent word in a sentence by considering preceding words. When presented with sentences containing gaps, it predicts the missing word and subsequently applies this capability to generate the next word, one word at a time. GPT-2 comprises a sequential architecture, where each layer incorporates attention mechanisms and basic neural networks, repeated multiple times.

In the process of generating predictions for subsequent word probabilities, such as "book," the model operates without requiring examples crafted by human intervention. This approach contrasts with traditional models that rely heavily on human-generated corpora for training, thus representing a significant advancement in computational language acquisition methods. Specifically, the model's ability to predict the next word independently highlights a novel direction in artificial intelligence applications, whereby linguistic patterns can be discerned without the explicit provision of human examples. Consequently, this method not only enhances the model's predictive capabilities but also underscores the potential for unsupervised learning systems to derive linguistic structures autonomously. Moreover, by eliminating the dependency on human input, the model demonstrates a capacity to adapt to diverse linguistic phenomena, thereby broadening the scope of language acquisition research.
