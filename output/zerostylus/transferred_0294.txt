We propose a method to extract semantic triples from images using multimodal large language models (LLMs), aligning them with domain-specific ontologies to build interpretable knowledge graphs (KGs). Furthermore, we introduce a graph-based influence analysis pipeline that compares generated images with their training counterparts via graph structures, thereby enabling human-understandable explanations of similarity. This approach proves useful in contexts requiring copyright attribution, interpretability, and dataset transparency, illustrating how such reasoning can enhance traceability in generative models. In evaluating our method, we conduct experiments on locally trained models and large-scale models, with a specific focus on Ghibli-style generations to assess our method's capability in detecting stylistic influences, even in black-box settings. The paper is organized as follows: Section 2 reviews related work on interpretability in generative models and knowledge graph construction using multimodal LLMs. Section 3 presents our proposed method, and Section 4 details our experimental setups and results across both local and large-scale generative models. Section 5 concludes with key findings and outlines future research directions. As generative models continue to evolve, the demand for interpretability has expanded across technical, ethical, and policy domains. Broad methodological approaches such as t-SNE, LIME, and attention visualizations offer insights into model behavior; however, they do not directly address the attribution of training data, which is an increasingly critical concern in applications involving authorship and copyright. To understand the influence of training data on model outputs, various attribution techniques have been proposed. Early methods include influence functions, retraining-based attribution, and Shapley value approximations. While these methods have demonstrated utility and effectiveness in well-understood contexts such as classification tasks, they are often impractical for large-scale models due to their reliance on gradients, retraining access, or substantial computational resources. Consequently, these approaches are seldom applied in modern deep learning pipelines, particularly in the domain of generative models where access to internal parameters is limited.

Contemporary deep learning architectures predominantly omit certain components, particularly within generative models where internal processes remain obscured. Therefore, such configurations emphasize external performance metrics over the transparency of internal mechanisms. Moreover, the exclusion of these elements is consistent with the broader trend towards optimizing model efficiency and scalability in generative tasks. However, this focus on external metrics may limit the interpretability of the model's internal workings, which is crucial for advancing the understanding of generative processes. Consequently, as the field progresses, the balance between model efficiency and interpretability remains a critical consideration for researchers aiming to enhance both performance and transparency in deep learning models.
