To address this significant issue, the research community has proposed two primary approaches: Fact Verification (FV) [12, 19, 27] and Hallucination Detection (HD) [3, 14, 25, 36]. Fact Verification is a well-established domain that emphasizes the assessment of factual accuracy utilizing sources such as web pages and databases. A defining characteristic of FV is its origin-agnostic nature; it evaluates the veracity of a statement, whether authored by a human or generated by a machine, by examining the available evidence. However, Hallucination Detection represents a more recent development, emerging alongside the advent and proliferation of large language models (LLMs). Unlike FV, which assesses text in isolation, HD specifically focuses on analyzing outputs generated by these models. Moreover, HD incorporates an analysis of the process by which the text was generated. The Hallucination Detection framework predominantly relies on intrinsic signals from the model, such as the model's confidence levels regarding individual lexical choices, activation patterns, and consistency across multiple iterations, while occasionally incorporating external information like that used in Fact Verification. Therefore, HD primarily depends on its internal model to identify fabricated information, even in the absence of external validation. Although both methodologies aim to identify factual inaccuracies, they have evolved independently, employing distinct evaluation frameworks, success metrics, and dissemination venues for their findings. This divergence arises from the fundamentally distinct methodologies employed in their evaluative processes. For instance, Fact Verification frameworks, such as FEVER [65], focus exclusively on the textual content, treating claims as discrete textual units.
