When examining these differences, it is imperative to interpret them with caution, particularly regarding their implications for prompt steerability. Detailed findings are presented in Tables 8 and 9. Our analysis reveals that prompt optimization induces notable distributional shifts, shedding light on how system prompts shape reasoning behaviors. However, the underlying mechanisms and their specific impacts on performance remain unexplored. To investigate this, reasoning patterns were analyzed both before and after prompt optimization. As illustrated in Figure 9 and detailed in Table 10, there is a significant reduction in the overall frequency of reasoning components following optimization. This reduction can be attributed to the streamlined nature of optimized prompts, which tend to be shorter and more concise. These prompts often omit superfluous instructions, such as "answer like Shakespeare," thereby reducing the length and variability of reasoning chains. Consequently, the proportion of the "Others" category decreases substantially, leading to more structured and interpretable model behavior. Moreover, the frequency of specific reasoning components, such as Logical reasoning and Reframing, exhibits considerable variation, underscoring the sensitivity of these behaviors to prompt formulation. The effects of optimization, however, are highly task-dependent. For instance, in mathematical tasks, optimized prompts lead to a marked increase in both Calculation and Logical reasoning, aligning closely with the demands of the domain. In contrast, these same optimized prompts fail to evoke similar reasoning components in other task types. In moral reasoning tasks, while the absolute frequency of most reasoning components declines, Logical reasoning remains the dominant mechanism in relative terms. Additionally, certain reasoning processes, such as Backtracking and Backward chaining, are consistently rare across all contexts. This infrequency likely stems from the absence of reflective capabilities in these models, unlike the reflective mechanisms present in reinforcement learning (RL) systems. Finally, our investigation considers whether the configuration of reasoning chains can serve as a predictor of prompt efficacy. Each reasoning chain is represented as a 9-dimensional vector, with each dimension reflecting the frequency of a specific reasoning component. This representation provides a structured approach to evaluate the relationship between reasoning features and prompt performance.
