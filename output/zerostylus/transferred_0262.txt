To manage increasingly complex systems, safety evaluation must shift from static compliance checks to dynamic system-level observation. This transition necessitates analyzing the propagation of influence across agents, assessing the extent to which oversight signals shape collective behavior, and identifying the critical threshold where information feedback becomes uncontrollable. Consequently, while foundational safety protocols remain indispensable, they are insufficient when models engage in interaction networks. Even impeccable local alignment cannot avert systemic failures arising from feedback dynamics, network topology, and collective incentive structures. Recent research has highlighted these emergent risks in multi-agent systems, introducing two notable frameworks: MAST (Multi-Agent System failure Taxonomy [3]) and MARAI (Multi-Agent Risks from Advanced AI [6]). Specifically, MAST categorizes failures into three distinct dimensions—system design flaws, agent coordination challenges, and verification difficulties—each rooted in the architecture of multi-agent systems. Conversely, MARAI delineates three modalities of inter-agent failure—miscoordination, conflict, and collusion—that collectively contribute to misaligned behaviors. Building on these insights, this paper presents a novel contribution by synthesizing both frameworks into a unified, system-centered approach. First, it structures the analysis to progress from agent-specific failures to risks that transcend individual system components. Second, it formalizes this perspective through the concept of the Emergent Systemic Risk Horizon (ESRH), which demarcates the point where localized reliability transitions into systemic instability. The ESRH introduces a comprehensive vocabulary and empirical indicators for investigating large language model (LLM) ecosystems as complex adaptive systems rather than isolated entities. This reframing serves as the conceptual foundation for the subsequent sections of the paper. Section 2 explores the empirical and theoretical shift towards multi-agent autonomy, emphasizing the regulatory implications of distributed causality. Section 3 elaborates on the ESRH taxonomy and its accompanying metrics. Finally, Section 4 proposes the Institutional AI architecture as an innovative design framework for embedding governance directly within the interactions of model collectives.
