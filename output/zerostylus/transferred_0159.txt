Energy consumption is a critical consideration across various domains, including mobile applications and software systems utilized within data centers. Determining and minimizing the energy consumption of even basic software systems presents a challenge, particularly as contemporary systems with dynamic optimizations exacerbate the complexity of this task. Specifically, programming languages such as Java, Scala, and Kotlin operate on the Java Virtual Machine via Java bytecode, a platform-independent intermediate representation. The energy consumption of software is contingent upon several factors: the compiler, which influences code optimization; the Java Virtual Machine (JVM), which affects execution efficiency; the garbage collector, responsible for memory management; the operating system, which governs system-level resource allocation; and the underlying hardware, which determines the physical execution environment. Consequently, to comprehend the impact of software design decisions on energy consumption, it is essential for a software engineer to possess comprehensive knowledge of both the distinct system layers and the underlying platform on which the software operates. Previous studies have investigated methods for profiling energy consumption based on software workloads, incorporating two primary components: the execution component, which involves running the software within a controlled environment that measures energy usage; and the analysis component, which determines the energy consumption attributable to each segment of the code. However, implementing this in practical scenarios requires specialized hardware, thereby complicating its consistent application in software engineering. While the energy consumption of specific hardware components, such as the CPU, can be measured by certain systems, few are capable of providing a comprehensive assessment of the energy usage across the entire platform. The modeling component constructs a Gaussian distribution for each quartet of descriptors pertaining to a code segment, categorizing each element to elucidate their influence on energy consumption, including the effect of operations, data types, data sizes, and devices. The proposed model comprises two components: the analysis component, which examines the impact of varying data types within an algorithm on energy consumption; and the explanatory component, which elucidates the relationship between these changes and overall energy use. The analytical focus is on the worst-case scenario of energy consumption for individual code statements, employing predictive methods to assess the energy utilization of functions and entire programs. In contrast to relying on a single estimate, which cannot account for all potential variations, these distributions represent the worst-case energy consumption by incorporating random fluctuations, such as those caused by temperature changes or interference, into the analysis. This is particularly important as it retains its statistical characteristics, such as the average, skewness, and mode, while also enabling the computation of measures like entropy and probability functions, which are not achievable through single-point estimates. Using worst-case energy consumption estimates ensures predictable estimation for the worst-case scenario, thereby guaranteeing that software systems can meet energy specifications, which are vital for battery and low-power devices and are cost-effective for applications and software executing in data centers. Consequently, program analysis or software verification tools can utilize this methodology as the foundation for their energy estimates.

Energy consumption is increasingly recognized as a critical concern across diverse domains, ranging from mobile devices to large-scale data centers. To address this issue, developers require precise and detailed insights into the energy consumption characteristics of their software. While prior methodologies have primarily focused on estimating energy usage at a broader level—such as specific functions or entire programs—they often neglect the granularity of source code statements. Furthermore, these approaches predominantly emphasize CPU energy consumption through point estimates, thereby overlooking the contribution of other hardware components and limiting their applicability for statistical reasoning and explainability. To overcome these limitations, we propose a novel methodology aimed at predicting energy consumption in statically typed JVM-based programming languages, including Java, Kotlin, and Scala. Specifically, this approach measures the energy consumption of bytecode patterns, which represent the transformation of source code statements into their corresponding Java bytecode. By leveraging these measurements, we construct a statistical model that advances beyond traditional frequentist approaches. Instead, we employ Bayesian statistics, which enables the prediction of expected energy consumption through statistical distributions while facilitating the analysis of individual contributing factors. The resulting model incorporates three static factors derived directly from the code—data size, data type, and operations—as well as a dynamic factor tied to the hardware platform on which the code executes, namely the device. To validate our methodology, we implemented it for Java by focusing on its bytecode patterns and evaluated its predictive performance on previously unseen programs. Notably, our findings reveal that all four factors significantly influence energy consumption. For instance, two devices of the same model may exhibit substantial variation in energy usage, and differences in operations and data types are also pronounced. Moreover, experimental results demonstrate that the predictions closely align with the actual energy consumption of programs, thereby confirming the robustness of our methodology. Importantly, this work introduces a foundational methodology for constructing energy models, which future research endeavors—such as energy-aware program analysis or verification tools—can utilize to enhance their energy estimation capabilities.
