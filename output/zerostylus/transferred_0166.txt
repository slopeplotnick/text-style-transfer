Recent licensing models, such as the Nwulite Obodo Open Data License (NOODL) [41, 42] and the Esethu License [17], exemplify a significant paradigm shift in the governance of data and resource sharing. Notably, NOODL differentiates between users in Africa or other developing regions and those in more developed areas, thereby enabling regionally tailored benefit-sharing mechanisms and requiring that adaptations be redistributed within the local context. Similarly, Esethu strategically reinvests licensing revenues into initiatives such as expanding local resources and supporting employment opportunities, thus fostering community agency and ensuring the long-term sustainability of resources. However, as experiences with Indigenous and low-resource language datasets have demonstrated, unrestricted openness, without the implementation of robust ethical guidelines, risks perpetuating existing inequities. Therefore, the evolution of open data practices has increasingly emphasized that responsible data sharing must extend beyond the use of standard licensing; it necessitates deliberate attention to consent, privacy, equity, and the establishment of mechanisms to ensure sustained community benefits. For instance, our selection of the CC BY 4.0 license for Swivuriso was motivated by a commitment to openness. Yet, it also highlighted the inherent limitations of conventional licenses when applied to datasets originating from African and low-resource languages. Consequently, emerging frameworks like NOODL and Esethu reflect a growing recognition within the field of the need for more ethical and equitable governance practices. In this context, it is no longer sufficient for dataset publications to simply declare adherence to a standard open license; authors must also articulate their strategies for ensuring ethical, equitable, and community-driven data stewardship. Swivuriso represents a significant advancement in the development of automatic speech recognition technologies and other speech-based tools specifically tailored to South African languages. The dataset encompasses seven linguistically and geographically diverse languages and includes both domain-specific and culturally relevant unscripted speech. This design enables a wide range of applications, including health communication, agricultural advisory services, and the creation of multilingual language technologies. Moreover, the dataset is crafted not only to support benchmark-driven evaluations—such as improving word error rates and other performance metrics—but also to facilitate the development of speech systems that are both culturally informed and responsive to societal needs.
