Based on the resulting clusters detailed in Figure 2, languages have been categorized as In-Domain (ID) languages. Our evaluation framework involves testing the model on both In-Domain (ID) and Out-of-Domain (OOD) languages. To ensure comprehensive representation of diverse linguistic contexts, 11 low-resource languages have been selected for inclusion in the Out-of-Domain group; these languages are chosen to serve as strong representatives of their respective clusters. For evaluation purposes, we benchmark our model using six safety classification datasets: Aegis-Content-Safety-2.0-Test (Aegis-CS2) introduced in 2025, HarmBench compiled by Mazeika et al. in 2024, Redteam2k developed by Luo et al. in 2024, JBB-Behaviors (subcategorized as JBB-Behav for Behaviors and JBB-Judge for Judge-comparison) created by Chao et al. in 2024, and StrongReject assembled by Souly et al. in 2024. These datasets collectively encompass various harm categories, including Hate/Identity Hate, Sexual, Suicide/Self-Harm, Violence, Guns/Illegal Weapons, PII/Privacy, Sexual Minor, Toxicity, and Abuse, thus rendering them suitable for a comprehensive evaluation of safety mechanisms. In terms of training configuration, we employ the XLM-RoBERTa-Base model, comprising 279 million parameters, and the XLM-RoBERTa-Large model, featuring 560 million parameters, as the foundational multilingual encoders. These models, pre-trained on a corpus of 100 languages, are particularly adept at handling cross-lingual tasks, which aligns with our generalization objectives. A single-layer classification head is appended to the encoder, matching the hidden dimensions of the encoder outputs and resulting in a binary output layer. Full-weight training is conducted on both the pretrained encoder and the classification head using the aggregated training dataset. All training and evaluation procedures are performed on an NVIDIA H100 GPU cluster utilizing Bfloat16 precision to enhance computational efficiency and overall performance. For baseline comparisons, we evaluate our model against smaller guardrails, including DuoGuard-0.5B (2025), PolyGuard (PG)-Qwen-Smol, and WalledGuard-C (2024), as well as larger guardrails, such as PolyGuard (PG)-Qwen (2025), LlamaGuard3 (2024), and Aegis-Defensive (2025). These baseline models span a spectrum of language coverage, ranging from monolingual English configurations to multilingual systems supporting up to 29 languages. This diversity allows for a comprehensive comparison of the scalability, efficiency, and robustness of our proposed safety guardrail in both high-resource and low-resource language settings.
