We will commence by examining the foundational work on language models employing Bayesian methods and n-grams, which were instrumental in developing early systems for speech recognition. These models addressed word sequences and uncertainties in spoken language, establishing the groundwork for contemporary systems. Subsequently, we will explore modern end-to-end speech models that incorporate contextual information directly into their processes. Unlike earlier approaches, these models integrate not only lexical content but also paralinguistic features such as tone, emotion, and speaker identity, thereby enhancing both comprehension and generation of speech.  Further, we will analyze how large language models (LLMs) contribute to mitigating errors in automatic speech recognition (ASR) systems. A key focus will be on addressing fairness and bias issues, particularly the impact of accents, dialects, and diverse speaking styles on speech processing. We will discuss recent advancements designed to address these challenges as well as the ethical implications of ensuring equitable system functionality across various user groups.   The discussion will extend to theoretical frameworks on LLM adaptation and their interplay with speech input. Topics include risk evaluation for different demographic groups and the feasibility of transferring model capabilities from ASR systems to LLMs, which informs diverse design strategies for spoken agents. Based on these insights, we will assess training methodologies that integrate text and speech, demonstrating how the alignment of multimodal learning objectives improves overall understanding. Moreover, we will consider how LLMs can assess voice quality and accommodate speaker variability in real-time adaptive scenarios.
