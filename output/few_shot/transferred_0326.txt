A journal entry in accounting serves as a fundamental record of a financial transaction, typically encompassing both a debit and a credit entry within one or more accounts. Each entry contains detailed information, including the date, an identification number, the currency involved, the individual responsible for the entry, applicable tax rates, amounts, and occasionally a descriptive annotation. These components can comprise numerical data, categorical data, or textual information, rendering journal entries a suitable medium for evaluating systems designed to detect atypical patterns in mixed data types. Both synthetic and anonymized datasets retain these core characteristics, thereby facilitating consistent model development and testing. Researchers have increasingly employed machine learning (ML) techniques to enhance the detection of anomalous or suspicious entries in accounting data. Unsupervised methods, such as clustering and Isolation Forest, have been applied to journal entries to identify deviations from standard patterns (Herreros-Martinez et al., 2025). Recent investigations utilizing synthetic general ledger data have demonstrated that these methods may outperform traditional rule-based systems (Gronewald et al., 2024). Nevertheless, challenges persist. Conventional ML models typically operate on structured data and may lack the contextual understanding required to accurately discern between genuinely risky entries and those that merely appear anomalous. In contrast, Large Language Models (LLMs), such as GPT-4, have paved new avenues in auditing practices. These models exhibit proficiency in language comprehension and reasoning, and preliminary research indicates their potential utility as AI assistants for financial data analysis (Gu et al., 2023). Emerging studies reveal that LLMs can also aid in anomaly detection, exemplified by SigLLM for time-series data (Alnegheimish et al., 2024) and AnoLLM for tabular data (Tsai et al., 2025), achieving strong performance without necessitating extensive data preprocessing. However, the deployment of LLMs in critical tasks such as auditing necessitates caution. LLMs can occasionally produce inconsistent outputs, exhibit biases, or fabricate information. Consequently, the development of tools to scrutinize the outputs of these models, such as AuditLLM (Amirizaniani et al., 2024a) and LLMAuditor (Amirizaniani et al., 2024b), has commenced. These tools underscore the significance of implementing governance and oversight measures when utilizing LLMs for decision-making in contexts such as auditing.
