Researchers have made significant efforts to enhance the performance of content generators. Approaches such as Mirror-Consistency, Self-Contrast, and Step-Back Prompting adopt mechanisms like iterative back-and-forth verification or multi-perspective evaluation to achieve improved outputs. Similarly, the Self-Check method employs a stepwise process to identify and rectify errors. While these methodologies aim to refine the internal mechanisms of content generators, they often incur additional computational costs and increased latency. In contrast to these approaches, our work focuses on optimizing the post-generation stage, specifically the aggregation of results. Rather than attempting to correct the generator’s potentially inconsistent outputs, we accept them as they are and utilize a specialized layer to determine the most accurate outcomes.  An important consideration when employing a language model as a referee for comparative evaluation is whether the model should be allowed to declare a “tie” or be constrained to choose only one option. Our findings reveal that forcing the model to make a definitive choice can lead to bias. Conversely, allowing the model to indicate a “tie” introduces sensitivity to the specific configuration of the judging framework. Despite this, permitting ties proves to be an effective strategy to mitigate bias. For instance, one prevalent type of bias, positional bias, arises when the order in which options are presented influences the model's preference. In an experimental setup where models were allowed to select “tie” as an option, we observed a significant reduction in positional bias across multiple tested models. This suggests that allowing ties not only facilitates the representation of equivalence between options but also plays a critical role in ensuring a fairer adjudication process.  However, challenges remain with tie decisions, particularly in scenarios requiring the model to evaluate and compare three or more options. Here, the choice dynamics become highly sensitive to the specifics of the experimental configuration, resulting in inconsistency in outcomes. These findings underscore the need for developing more robust mechanisms to aggregate and interpret results in multi-option decision-making tasks effectively.
