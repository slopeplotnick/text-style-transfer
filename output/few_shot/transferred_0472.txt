Recent developments in the global mining industry illustrate rapid evolution over the last decade, particularly as the industry becomes integral to the ongoing energy transition. The increasing demand for critical minerals is anticipated to rise alongside numerous political, social, and environmental challenges. Consequently, this has led to the emergence of innovative enterprises, frequently supported by venture capital, which aim to revolutionize the mining sector analogously to transformations witnessed in healthcare, renewable energy, and climate technology sectors. Concurrently, advancements in digital technologies, such as large language models, are poised to potentially transform economic, educational, societal, and industrial practices. Although the extent of their impact remains uncertain, there is pervasive concern regarding potential missed opportunities. It is imperative for the mining industry to perceive these developments as opportunities for leveraging artificial intelligence (AI). This paper explores potential avenues for such integration and the role of AI in enhancing industry operations.  Our research group, Stanford Mineral-X, has successfully utilized AI in identifying a substantial copper deposit in Zambia, notably aiding in drilling planning and the development of subsurface models. This initiative has stimulated the inception of several start-ups concentrated on the application of AI in mineral exploration. While the present discourse does not attempt to encapsulate an exhaustive list of AI applications in exploration or review existing resources—tasks already addressed in existing literature—it seeks to examine AI as a means to enhance decision-making based on an emerging scientific framework. The initial section of this paper will delineate the principal decision-making processes in exploration, emphasizing how a scientific approach can augment exploration efficiency. Notably, the mineral exploration sector currently lacks a standardized scientific methodology, relying instead on expert-driven tasks, each characterized by distinct disciplinary-specific strategies. This paper contends that the absence of a comprehensive scientific method constitutes a significant barrier to improving exploration efficiency, implying that more efficient exploration could lead to reduced discovery costs.
