Online videos have become a significant medium for communication, facilitating the rapid dissemination of harmful content. This proliferation exacerbates discrimination and social division and has the potential to incite real-world violence. Traditional approaches to addressing this issue involve extracting features from video, audio, and text; however, these approaches often fail to adequately capture the interplay between these modalities. Two primary challenges exist in the detection of hateful videos: firstly, the necessity of contextual understanding—such videos frequently employ subtle cues across temporal spans and various media types, such as the alignment of specific images and words or the correlation between visual and auditory elements. Secondly, the integration of multimodal information—contemporary methods extract features from video, audio, and text but lack effective interaction between these modalities. Both challenges necessitate sophisticated comprehension techniques that conventional methods struggle to provide. Emerging approaches aim to enhance understanding by leveraging models that integrate visual and linguistic elements. These methods incorporate reasoning processes, yet direct reasoning often yields superficial descriptions lacking connection to the underlying hateful intent. Furthermore, some recent approaches may generate erroneous explanations that do not accurately reflect the model’s rationale, posing significant concerns for sensitive tasks. In addition to reasoning, recent research highlights that hate cues in videos may emerge abruptly in brief segments or be distributed across the entire video. These signals also convey intricate meanings that must be synthesized with basic features. These challenges underscore a critical gap: existing systems are insufficient for reliable reasoning or the effective integration of multimodal information necessary for the identification of hateful videos.
