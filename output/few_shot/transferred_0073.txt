This manuscript explores the limitations of existing safety frameworks designed for human-model interactions when applied to interactions among large language models (LLMs). Predominantly, current methodologies aim to ensure the safety of individual models through mechanisms such as prompt engineering, fine-tuning, and moderation, thereby regulating the behavior of a single model. However, these approaches do not adequately address the complexities arising from the interactions between multiple models. Traditional setups presume a one-to-one interaction between a model and a user, maintained under continuous human supervision. Nonetheless, both academic research and industry practices are rapidly progressing towards systems where models engage with each other, utilizing each other's outputs as inputs in iterative processes. In such configurations, even if each model operates as intended, systemic failures can still occur. We advocate for a paradigm shift from a model-centric safety focus to a systemic perspective. We introduce the concept of the Emergent Systemic Risk Horizon (ESRH) to elucidate how risks emanate from the interactions among models rather than the isolated actions of individual models. This paper presents (i) a theoretical framework for understanding interaction-induced risks, (ii) a methodology for linking various levels of failure, and (iii) a proposal for "Institutional AI," which offers a governance model for enhanced oversight in multi-agent systems. Current safety protocols presume models operate under human monitoring, where inappropriate behavior can be mitigated through prompt adjustments, human feedback learning, or output regulation. While such methods have enhanced the reliability and civility of models, they are limited to addressing individual instances. They regulate the response of a single model to a solitary input rather than the collaborative dynamics of multiple models. As LLMs increasingly become integral to tools, agents, and multisystem environments, the efficacy of single-model safety paradigms diminishes. Models that perform well independently may collectively engender challenges unanticipated in solo configurations, due to feedback loops, mimicry, or emergent coordination. This scenario reveals a disconnect between ensuring the performance of individual models and maintaining the integrity of the system as a whole, highlighting that ensuring individual component performance does not necessarily translate to overall network safety.
