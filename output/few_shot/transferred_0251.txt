When constructing narratives, individuals may either begin with an event and subsequently determine the characteristics and progression of the entities involved, or alternatively, commence with an entity and create a series of events surrounding it. For instance, E2, a doctoral candidate in literature, posits that stories can be deconstructed into fundamental components such as outlines, sequences, ideas, and basic elements of dialogue. By assembling these components in reverse order, a cohesive narrative can be developed. An idea may consist of an entity, an action, or a characteristic; several ideas can form a sequence, and multiple sequences combine to create a full narrative. However, simply aggregating these components does not suffice to produce a compelling story. The connective elements, termed relationships, are integral to ensuring cohesion and coherence.   Through interviews with two subject-matter experts, it was identified that narratives inherently comprise four essential components: entities (e.g., characters or objects), events, relationships, and structural outlines. These components align with findings from prior research, as detailed in Table II, which provides definitions and explanations of each. Further analysis of the expert interviews revealed nuanced interpretations of these components. While events constitute the core of a narrative, the explicit representation of events may not always be apparent in the storytelling process. Events can function independently but derive deeper significance and emotional tension through their interrelations. Moreover, the entities within a narrative, along with the relationships between them, can influence the unfolding of events. The attributes of entities and the dynamics of their relationships can also be independently examined to enhance understanding of the narrative structure.
