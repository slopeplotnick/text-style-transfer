This study investigates whether a computational model simulating human language acquisition is capable of independently discerning grammatical structures and lexical categories by interacting with a language model named Delilah, which provides exemplars of adult language usage. Upon acquiring this grammatical framework, the model, referred to as the daughter model, engages in dialogue with another computational entity, designated as the mother model. The daughter model constructs novel sentences utilizing the acquired grammar and shares these constructs with the mother model during their interaction. Conditional on the parameters set, the mother model may provide evaluative feedback regarding the grammatical accuracy, which the daughter model can subsequently employ for refinement. Additionally, the daughter model leverages its grammatical knowledge to interpret new sentences from the mother model, facilitating the identification of lexical categories used by the latter. Prior research indicates that function words, which serve grammatical roles, exhibit high frequency of occurrence, whereas content words with specific semantic content appear less frequently, often manifesting only once or a few times within a text. This distribution aligns with Zipf’s law, a principle asserting that the most frequent words in a language vastly outnumber their less frequent counterparts, as examined by researchers such as Mandelbrot and Zipf. Furthermore, there exists an association between word frequency distributions in languages and the grammatical categories scrutinized by linguists. This paper explores whether a similar correspondence is observable in language generated by computational systems.
