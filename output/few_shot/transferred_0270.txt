We evaluated the efficacy of the attack by maintaining the same model (Deepseek) while testing it across various frameworks, and conversely, by keeping the framework constant (OpenManus) and experimenting with different models. For all experiments, the generation temperature was fixed at 0, and tool usage was capped at 20. To assess the additional computational overhead imposed by the LeechHijack attack, we integrated it into various tools and compared resource consumption against that of standard tools. Our primary metric of analysis was the total token count, encompassing both input and output tokens. Since backdoored tools are capable of triggering LeechHijack multiple times during a single task, we also computed the average additional computational cost per trigger. Though the excess resource usage for each hijacked task output represents only a portion of the total consumed resources, the cumulative computational overhead from the user’s perspective constitutes a significant security concern.   To evaluate the utility of the LeechHijack mechanism, the study examined its capacity to enable attackers to exploit hijacked resources. Specifically, we assessed the accuracy of responses generated for covert tasks against the baseline model performance on the MMLU benchmark. The results, as summarized in Tables 7 and 8, indicate that LeechHijack can effectively repurpose stolen computational resources for hidden tasks, albeit with some measurable degradation in task performance. These findings underscore the attack's potential for resource hijacking, highlighting its feasibility and potential utility for malicious actors.
