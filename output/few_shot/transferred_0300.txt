Our investigation focuses on elucidating potential parallels between brain function and artificial intelligence (AI) by examining the concept of a "world model." A world model is essentially a system that facilitates prediction and comprehension of environmental stimuli. We conducted a comparative analysis of information processing in the brain and AI systems, revealing convergent methodologies, notably within large language models. This led to the proposition of a novel hypothesis regarding large-scale information processing in the brain. Evidently, brain regions such as the neocortex and cerebellum construct these world models by predicting future events, learning from predictive discrepancies, and deploying these models across various tasks, including sensory perception and motor control. This framework integrates classical theories such as the internal model theory and the mirror neuron system, positing that such a system is crucial for executing diverse tasks using a foundational structure. This perspective may bridge neuroscientific research and AI, advancing our understanding of human-like intelligence mechanisms. In particular, the cerebellum functions as a predictive module, assimilating external information, forecasting subsequent events, and rectifying errors based on sensory feedback. Studies indicate that its learning focus is on anticipating the forthcoming sensory input rather than processing higher-level signals.
