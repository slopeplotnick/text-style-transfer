Certain tasks exhibited no functionality within specific categories, highlighting areas requiring improvement. The study validated three key findings: (1) employing Large Language Models (LLMs) to coordinate multi-robot task allocation is effective; (2) implementing structured validation methods enhances reasoning reliability; and (3) retry mechanisms address issues in LLM outputs. Following these findings, the authors proposed the LTAA framework to improve LLM-MRTA systems. To evaluate LTAA, the TEACH dataset—widely regarded as a benchmark for comparing Q-learning, Deep Q-Network (DQN), and Dynamic Programming (DP) within MRTA research—was employed. Notably, the dataset transitioned from household tasks to construction robotics tasks, facilitating consistent performance comparison across algorithms under identical conditions.    LTAA concentrates on task allocation for robots, akin to traditional algorithms, utilizing pre-decomposed tasks to ensure comparability between LLM-based and algorithm-based approaches. Building on previously discussed self-corrective methodologies, LTAA employs a nine-step workflow. Unlike the Self-Corrective Agent Architecture, which managed all aspects of task completion, LTAA focuses exclusively on robot-task assignment, ensuring parity in testing conditions across task types.    The LTAA framework introduces three primary innovations aimed at rivaling traditional optimization algorithms while maintaining clarity in reasoning: (1) it dynamically adjusts task allocation strategies as projects evolve; (2) it incorporates multi-stage validation with retry mechanisms and self-correction to uphold LLM reasoning accuracy; and (3) it integrates context-aware reasoning to align decision-making with robotic capabilities. These innovations are embedded within a structured nine-step LangGraph workflow, which processes tasks systematically through stages of structured reasoning.
