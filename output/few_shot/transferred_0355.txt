We conducted a series of evaluations using various benchmarks, such as WMT23 for assessing machine translation quality and Reward Bench 2 for evaluating reward models. Our findings indicate that our method, which modifies vote distribution, significantly outperforms traditional approaches reliant on self-consistency. By meticulously analyzing all votes, our method transforms disparate individual model decisions into more reliable ratings, matching or even surpassing human performance relative to a gold standard based on human consensus.  Our methodology involved several key steps: Firstly, we identified the limitations of conventional approaches to aggregating results from Large Language Model (LLM) judges during inference, emphasizing the critical importance of improved result combination techniques. Secondly, we introduced a novel approach employing Expected Risk Minimization (ERM) in conjunction with a Bradley-Terry-Davidson model, validated on limited calibration data. This approach demonstrated superior performance across diverse tasks, including reward benchmarks and machine translation.  Thirdly, for machine translation specifically, we employed a consensus-based evaluation to generate more accurate ground truth labels in instances of noisy data, facilitating a fair comparison with human raters and illustrating that LLM judges can occasionally surpass human performance. Recently, LLMs have been utilized as "judges" in the evaluation of generated text, proving highly effective and offering a scalable alternative to traditional quality metrics. These models have demonstrated strong alignment with human judgments across various domains.  Existing methods include utilizing general-purpose LLMs directly, such as G-Eval or JudgeLM, or optimizing models specifically for evaluative tasks, like Prometheus or Auto-J. Despite their robustness, these approaches face challenges, such as prompt design sensitivity or bias towards specific candidate orders or longer outputs. Additionally, LLM judges exhibit variability in decision-making, with some being more stringent than others regarding minor differences or ties. Our research focuses on mitigating this variability and enhancing decision consistency through an improved result combination strategy.
