In Experiment 2, it was observed that employing a singular system prompt enhanced performance across all tested languages, indicating a systematic alteration in model behavior. To analyze the changes in model output, the reasoning steps were examined to ascertain the relationship between reasoning modifications and performance across various languages. A critical question was whether optimizing the prompt alters the model's language preference. The investigation began with an analysis of the languages utilized in the reasoning steps generated by the model. As illustrated in Figure 7, even under identical random prompts, the model exhibits varying language preferences contingent upon the question's language. These variations align with the availability of training data for each language. For instance, due to the extensive presence of Chinese data in model families such as Qwen, the model responds to Chinese queries in Chinese nearly as frequently as it does in English. Post prompt optimization, these language preferences intensify, with the model predominantly responding in the question’s language rather than defaulting to English, which was observed with the initial system prompt. However, for languages with limited training data, such as Hindi, the frequency of English responses remains relatively unchanged following prompt optimization. Furthermore, it was discovered that languages other than the task language and English provide minimal utility, as they nearly vanish following prompt optimization. Additional information on language identification is available in Appendix A.9, with further data provided in Tables 6 and 7. Figure 8 explores the reasoning behavior types across different languages. Although overall patterns are largely uniform, certain languages exhibit specific preferences; for example, English favors Reframing, Chinese emphasizes Logical Reasoning, and Spanish favors calculation. Conversely, languages with sparse training data, such as Hindi, display a higher incidence of the Other category, suggesting limited exposure to structured reasoning during training. Despite these consistent trends, they appear less attributable to prompt design and more reflective of language-specific patterns inherent in model pretraining.
