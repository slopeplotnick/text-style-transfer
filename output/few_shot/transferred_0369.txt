When evaluating model performance, it becomes evident that the order of articles provided significantly influences the generated summaries. Specifically, these models exhibit a tendency to prioritize the first article in the sequence, affecting both the framing of information and the degree of similarity between the summaries and the original articles. This observation aligns with prior findings indicating that language models preferentially attend to earlier portions of lengthy inputs (e.g., Brown et al., 2020; Raffel et al., 2021). In our study, we confirmed that such positional biases extend to multi-document scenarios, where the first article consistently receives more attention in the information-ranking process. Utilizing the BERTScore metric [Zhang et al., 2019], we observed a recurring pattern: words and arguments from the initial article are disproportionately represented in the summaries compared to subsequent articles, resulting in consistently higher similarity scores for the first article. Alternative evaluation metrics such as ROUGE-L [Lin, 2004], which focus on word overlap, and SummaC [Laban et al., 2021], which assesses factual consistency, do not capture this framing bias as effectively as BERTScore does. Despite all three metrics indicating a salient distinction between the first article and the others, no substantial differences were observed between the second and third articles. This suggests that the observed bias stems from the prioritization of the first article rather than a progressive decline in attention to later inputs.  This positional bias has important implications for real-world applications, particularly in AI-driven summarization tools. For instance, Google's AI-powered Gemini models, used for generating search result summaries, may exhibit a tendency to prioritize information from the first processed articles over subsequent ones. Such a bias could inadvertently amplify particular viewpoints while neglecting others, potentially influencing user understanding of a given topic. This issue is especially pertinent given the high degree of trust that users may place in AI-generated content. Addressing this bias is thus critical for ensuring fairness and accuracy in automated summarization systems.
