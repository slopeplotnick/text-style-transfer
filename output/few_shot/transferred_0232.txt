This study examines the capability of computational systems to acquire language categories, utilizing an adult language model named Delilah alongside a subsidiary agent. The subsidiary model is specifically designed for the MODOMA framework and employs graph-based representations to depict grammatical knowledge, facilitating sentence generation and comprehension, which improve as language acquisition progresses. Delilah incorporates a parser and generator devised by Cremers and Hijzelendoorn to engage with Dutch grammar, enabling both sentence generation and comprehension. Consequently, MODOMA necessitates a novel investigative approach towards Delilah: in addition to enhancing Delilah's sentence generation and understanding capabilities, there is a need to analyze Delilah's outputs to assist the subsidiary model in identifying learning patterns. To achieve this, methodologies from linguistics and related disciplines can be employed to interpret machine-generated language similarly to human language. Given the proliferation of large language models, assessing the similarity between machine and human language is of paramount importance. This paper presents findings from experiments on this topic. Notably, Delilah's sentence generation during interactions with the subsidiary agent results in isolated examples, in contrast to the typically connected sentences found in corpora. Nonetheless, analogous grammatical patterns emerge in both datasets. This observation suggests that these patterns may be more strongly associated with individual sentences rather than entire corpora. The study demonstrates that distinct frequency patterns of language categories, well-documented for human language data, also manifest in the output of Delilah. Importantly, Delilah is not explicitly programmed to replicate these patterns, indicating that the observed patterns stem from the employed grammar model.
