The articles were divided into smaller segments to ensure both the summary and each article segment conformed to a 512-token limit. The summary was then compared to each segment, and the final SummaC score was computed by averaging the scores across all segments, yielding a value between 0 and 1. Heatmaps and a one-way ANOVA analysis were employed to examine whether the input position influenced the generated summaries. The findings indicate that summaries were more closely aligned with the first article in the input sequence. As illustrated in Figure 3, the average scores for three evaluation metrics are reported across six input orders and three stance categories. Averages for each condition were calculated by aggregating scores across all article groups with identical input orders and stances. Variations in shading and scores underscore the impact of input position on the patterns of similarity in LLM-generated summaries. Summaries exhibited higher ROUGE-L and BERTScore values when compared with the first article in the sequence. Furthermore, BERTScore values for Position 1 were consistently higher than those for Positions 2 and 3, suggesting that the lexical and semantic contributions of the first document significantly shape the generated summaries.   In addressing Research Question 1 (RQ1), these results reveal a consistent trend: the summaries generated by LLMs are more similar to the first article in the input sequence than to subsequent articles. Notably, the metric values reported here diverge from those typically observed in summarization research. This divergence arises because the generated summaries were evaluated directly against the source articles, rather than against human-crafted reference summaries as is standard in prior work. Generated summaries averaged approximately 220 words in length, whereas the source articles ranged between 300 and 1600 words, which inherently reduced token overlap and similarity scores. Consequently, the analysis focused on relative score patterns across different input conditions rather than on absolute metric values.   Another limitation relates to the current dearth of metrics designed to evaluate alignment between summaries and their source documents. Most existing evaluation frameworks are reference-based, offering limited tools for assessing source alignment. SummaC, a framework that employs sentence-level entailment for evaluation, yielded consistently low scores across conditions and was largely insensitive to positional variations. This limitation is attributable to the abstract nature of generated summaries, which often omit specific details present in the source text, thereby attracting penalties for unsupported or implied statements. Moreover, although models exhibited a positional bias toward the first article in the input sequence, they seldom reproduced the exact source phrasing verbatim, constraining SummaC's ability to capture subtle content biases unless explicitly supported sentences were preserved.
