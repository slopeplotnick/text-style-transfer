We propose a methodology to predict the potential improvement in solver performance when utilizing a verifier to select correct answers. To validate this hypothesis, we experimented with various combinations of solvers and verifiers derived from a set of 12 models, selecting the three smallest models from each of four distinct groups. For each problem, the solver persisted in attempts until the verifier confirmed a correct answer or up to nine attempts; if no correct answer was identified, the last attempt was retained. The empirical outcomes, juxtaposed with anticipated improvements, are illustrated in Figure 4. While minimal improvements are sometimes obscured due to the nine-attempt limitation, the overall results align with our predictions. Notably, self-verification is less effective than cross-group verification. The primary conclusion is that our predictive approach successfully forecasts improvements without substantial additional effort. It is well-documented that individuals exhibit a cognitive bias towards answers resembling their own, and our findings suggest a parallel bias in solver-verifier interactions. Prior research has demonstrated that models proficient in reasoning derive limited benefit from self-verification but perform better under verification by different models, reflecting a similar bias. In summary, when a solver and verifier possess congruent cognitive patterns, the verifier is more prone to accept erroneous responses, thereby diminishing improvement. Employing a verifier with disparate reasoning patterns mitigates this issue.
