Language models have demonstrated significant utility in enhancing the monitoring of mental health, surpassing traditional methodologies. They exhibit superior performance compared to conventional computational programs in comprehending context and assimilating information, thereby rendering them advantageous for large-scale mental health assessment. However, despite their potential, large language models (LLMs) present challenges: they are computationally expensive and pose privacy risks due to the processing of sensitive data on remote servers. Additionally, their substantial size renders them unsuitable for deployment on resource-constrained devices, highlighting the necessity for more efficient alternatives.   In contrast, smaller language models offer comparable, if not superior, utility for digital health applications by virtue of their simplicity, reduced operational costs, and ability to function directly on devices. This capability allows these models to infer mental health conditions from social media data without retaining personal information, facilitating rapid and ethical monitoring. Despite their potential, the exploration of small language models in this context remains limited. This study investigates the efficacy of small language models in predicting mental health outcomes, demonstrating their viability as a solution for assessing multiple mental health conditions on personal devices.   We evaluate these models on tasks related to stress, depression, and suicide risk assessment. We introduce a compact model, Menta, which is fine-tuned through a specialized approach and trained across diverse datasets to prioritize accuracy. Menta exhibits strong performance across various mental health tasks, particularly in identifying stress and depression. Furthermore, we demonstrate its practicality by running it on an iPhone 15 Pro Max, where it operates in real-time with minimal memory usage, thereby providing private and scalable mental health support directly on the device.
