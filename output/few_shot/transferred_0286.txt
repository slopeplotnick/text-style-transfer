Within the domain of Software Engineering (SWE) agents, significant efforts have been devoted to evaluating their strengths and limitations. Empirical studies reveal that these agents face challenges when addressing problems spanning multiple files. Researchers have underscored the necessity for improved evaluation methodologies, emphasizing key dimensions such as efficiency, reliability, and intelligence. Investigations into failure modes of these agents have identified recurring issues, including an inability to accurately locate faults, difficulties in managing complex code dependencies, and challenges in maintaining contextual coherence across files.   For instance, TRAIL, introduced by one research group, comprises an annotated error repository and a benchmark of 148 human-validated traces (Smith et al., 2023). Another contribution, MAST, proposes a taxonomy for classifying failures in multi-agent systems through analysis of 150 interaction traces collected from five systems across five benchmarks, necessitating human annotation for data interpretation (Jones et al., 2023). Additionally, a study designed a flow graph to depict agent activities such as project comprehension, context management, debugging, and testing; however, this investigation involved manual processing of a limited dataset, focusing on bug localization for only 20 out of 500 identified issues (Taylor et al., 2024).   Further research has categorized Large Language Model (LLM) tool failures in SWE tasks into three phases, nine overarching categories, and 25 subcategories. This categorization served as the foundation for analyzing failure mechanisms, revealing issues such as agents becoming trapped in repetitive loops. Nevertheless, this analysis was conducted manually and limited to 150 out of 500 identified issues (Wang et al., 2024). These findings collectively highlight critical gaps in current methodologies and underscore the need for scalable, automated assessment frameworks within the SWE domain.
