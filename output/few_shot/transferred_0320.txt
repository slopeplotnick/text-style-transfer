Enhanced taxonomic frameworks emphasize that robots equipped with specialized skills face unique challenges when deployed in complex construction tasks. These challenges differentiate construction sites from controlled environments where conventional methods have historically been effective. Construction sites are inherently dynamic, characterized by fluctuating material flows, unpredictable weather conditions, and intricate interactions among various trades. Such environments necessitate systems capable of managing spatial constraints, temporal dependencies, and safety-critical operations. Moreover, the nature of construction demands human oversight that cannot be predetermined or easily programmed. Addressing these challenges requires flexible and adaptive coordination mechanisms capable of operating in evolving and unstructured settings.  Traditional optimization techniques have been employed to address these challenges through diverse algorithmic strategies. Classical optimization methods form the bedrock of established multi-robot coordination frameworks, providing mathematical rigor and performance guarantees through computational methodologies. The seminal work of Kuhn (1955) introduced the Hungarian method, which laid the theoretical foundation for solving optimal assignment problems using linear programming. This method demonstrated the efficient allocation of tasks and resources but was confined to simple one-to-one assignments and lacked the capacity to incorporate heterogeneous robot skills or task interdependencies.   In construction contexts, robots often possess specialized skills, such as heavy lifting or precision assembly, and task workflows require coordinated sequences with explicit dependencies. Recognizing these limitations, more advanced optimization techniques were developed. For instance, Atay and Bayazit (2006) introduced mixed-integer linear programming (MILP) to address multi-robot task allocation problems, accommodating constraints related to robot skills, task sequencing, and communication capacities. In parallel, advancements in military and aerospace applications refined MILP-based coordination approaches, introducing sophisticated methods to manage diverse skill sets and temporal constraints. MILP provided significant advantages in modeling complex multi-robot coordination scenarios, ensuring precise constraint satisfaction.  However, MILP approaches rely on centralized optimization frameworks, which face scalability challenges when coordinating large robotic teams. Construction projects, characterized by expansive, unstructured sites and often constrained communication networks, pose substantial feasibility issues for centralized coordination methods. These scalability challenges have driven the adoption of distributed coordination techniques, which offer greater adaptability to the complexities of large-scale, dynamic construction environments.
