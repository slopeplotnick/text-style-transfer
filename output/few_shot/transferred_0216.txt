To construct an effective relevance model, it is imperative to comprehend items, understand queries, and map pathways with precision. The model must be capable of processing a vast array of items while addressing diverse user needs, whether straightforward or abstract. Achieving this entails several key components: the integration of external knowledge and logical reasoning to clarify ambiguities in queries and comprehend abstract user objectives when mapping paths. Additionally, the model must adeptly handle both textual and visual data to ensure alignment and matching of details, which is crucial for items reliant on visual content. Furthermore, the model must adhere to and learn complex rules to ensure that the path-mapping process remains consistent and dependable.  In the context of training large models for relevance tasks, the endeavor is analogous to a classification problem. Initial methodologies employed prompts for task setup and responses to demonstrate outcomes, refining the models through supervised learning. However, this simplistic approach proved inadequate when the dataset diverged from the model's training data. It was observed that prompting the model to engage in sequential reasoning before providing answers enhanced its performance in challenging scenarios. Given constraints on computational resources and model deployment, there is a propensity towards utilizing smaller models, which tend to exhibit less effective reasoning and instruction-following capabilities when reliant solely on prompts.  Consequently, the prevailing approach involves utilizing larger teacher models, such as GPT-4 or DeepSeek-R1, to generate reasoning data. Subsequently, smaller models are trained via supervised learning to acquire these reasoning skills. Some researchers augment this process by incorporating a reinforcement learning phase, deploying techniques like offline reinforcement learning (RL), such as Direct Preference Optimization or KTO, or online RL, exemplified by Group Relative Policy Optimization.
