Recently, brain-computer interfaces (BCIs) have garnered substantial attention as mechanisms enabling direct interfacing between the brain and digital devices or systems. These interfaces can be classified into invasive (requiring surgical intervention) and non-invasive (without surgical necessities) modalities. Researchers, clinicians, and media outlets have shown a vested interest in BCIs due to their capacity to assist individuals with severe motor or communicative impairments—conditions induced by locked-in syndrome, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), spinal cord injuries, or strokes—facilitating communication or mobility otherwise hindered. Consequently, enhancing non-invasive BCIs by broadening the analysis of features derived from electroencephalography (EEG) data has become increasingly crucial. Various methodologies exist for feature extraction from EEG signals, which are essential for data analysis. These techniques range from straightforward temporal or frequency domain approaches to sophisticated multi-dimensional strategies. Transforming raw EEG data into actionable information—such as time-domain statistics, frequency-related features like power spectral density, or time-frequency combined methods like wavelet transforms—is imperative to ensure accurate signal classification and optimal system performance. Robust features across individuals are also vital due to significant inter-subject variability in EEG signals. Challenges such as temporal data shifts and inter-dataset variability necessitate employing techniques like transfer learning and domain generalization to ensure feature reliability. A critical inquiry involves identifying optimal combinations of feature extraction and classification techniques, particularly given the substantial inter-individual variability in EEG signals. Addressing this requires employing evaluation methods that mitigate overly optimistic results, including utilizing data from previously unencountered subjects in testing (e.g., leave-one-subject-out cross-validation), leveraging metrics that effectively handle data imbalances (e.g., balanced accuracy and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC)), validating results with robust statistical tests, and preventing data leakage. For instance, preprocessing steps such as filtering or normalization should be applied exclusively to training data, avoiding application to test data.
