Apart from Study 1, all synthetic datasets replicated the factor structure of their corresponding scales. Moreover, Multigroup Confirmatory Factor Analysis (M-CFA) indicates that both synthetic and authentic participants exhibit similar response patterns to the scales. These observations align with prior studies on group-level synthetic data. Notably, Studies 3 and 4 demonstrate higher levels of measurement consistency between datasets compared to Studies 1 and 2, suggesting that scales derived from synthetic samples may exhibit greater applicability to real-world data rather than the converse. This phenomenon could be attributed to the tendency of Large Language Models (LLMs) to interpret scale items in a more standardized manner, with reduced susceptibility to variability and biases arising from study conditions and population characteristics. Consequently, factor structures gleaned from synthetic samples may generalize more effectively to diverse samples than those derived from real-world data. Future research should explore the determinants influencing the comprehension and response patterns of LLMs and human participants to survey questions. Despite the favorable outcomes observed in CFA, synthetic samples fail to replicate the pairwise correlations, distribution, and variance seen in authentic samples. In aggregate, these findings reveal that although both datasets share the same factor structure, their score distributions diverge significantly across all assessed domains. Thus, while group-level data and their structures may be sufficiently robust for pilot testing, individual-level synthetic data are inadequate for this purpose. These limitations corroborate the findings of Petrov et al. It is noteworthy that while Studies 2, 3, and 4 yield consistent results, Study 1 deviates markedly. This discrepancy could be attributed to several factors: first, the factor structure of this particular scale was derived using an alternative method (MINRES) compared to other scales, potentially leading to divergent response patterns. Second, this scale employs a 5-point Likert scale for responses, whereas others utilize a 6-point or 7-point scale, possibly contributing to the observed variations.
