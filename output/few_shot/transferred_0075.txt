The process of understanding language is a sequential and multifaceted undertaking. Initially, auditory input is perceived, followed by the recognition of phonemic elements, which are subsequently combined into lexical units, and interpreted through syntactic analysis to comprehend entire sentences. This study is focused on the cognitive mechanisms involved once lexical recognition has occurred, a sophisticated mental process exclusive to humans, and contrasts these mechanisms with the capabilities of advanced artificial intelligence systems in language processing. Empirical evidence indicates that the human brain processes language in hierarchical layers, with rudimentary tasks, such as lexical prediction, occurring in the lower strata, and more intricate tasks, like the comprehension of abstract concepts, in the higher strata. The cerebral acquisition of language is facilitated through iterative predictive error corrections, paralleling the learning paradigms utilized by artificial intelligence. Both the brain and AI systems employ predictive processing for language comprehension.  For instance, the Google Neural Machine Translation (GNMT) system employs a tripartite architecture comprising an encoder, an attention mechanism, and a decoder. The encoder sequentially processes input lexical items, generating a composite signal encapsulating the semantic content of the sentence across eight layers, culminating in a unified sentence representation. The attention mechanism discerns the relative significance of input lexical items for translation, relaying this data to the decoder, which sequentially predicts subsequent lexical items in the target language. The system refines its translation accuracy by iteratively comparing its output to human-generated reference translations and adapting to discrepancies.  In contrast, GPT-2, a variant of AI, eschews distinct modular components such as those found in GNMT. Instead, it engages in autoregressive modeling, predicting subsequent lexical items based on preceding context. During its training phase, GPT-2 is exposed to syntactically incomplete sentences and learns to infer the missing lexical items, a skill it later employs to autonomously generate text by predicting successive lexical items one at a time. The architecture comprises multiple layers of recurrent blocks incorporating attention mechanisms and fundamental neural networks. These layers process lexical sequences and output probabilistic estimates of forthcoming lexical items, learning through autonomous next-word prediction exercises without reliance on human-curated examples.
