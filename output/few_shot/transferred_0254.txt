In this study, we investigate a particular type of attack characterized by the random triggering of a backdoor when users engage with a tool, indicating that the vulnerability is inherent from the onset. A more severe variant of this attack presents as initially benign, with attackers refraining from immediate malicious actions and allowing the tool to function ostensibly normal. Once the tool gains widespread adoption, the attack can be remotely activated. This deferred strategy, akin to supply chain attacks, enhances the threat's severity and obscurity. The attack's subtlety can be further enhanced by targeting specific users. For instance, attackers may analyze user data to selectively target high-frequency users, particularly those who have successfully passed security screenings, thereby rendering the attack less detectable during testing phases.   In this paper, we introduce a novel attack paradigm termed "implicit toxicity," which exploits authorized tool functionalities to inflict harm. We demonstrate this through the development of a method called "LeechHijack," which covertly commandeers a system's computational resources during routine tool usage. This method is compatible with any MCP tool provider, infiltrating systems without requiring elevated permissions and misappropriating system resources for unintended tasks. This highlights vulnerabilities in MCP tool dissemination, where malicious tools can compromise systems without necessitating additional access privileges. Extensive experimentation across various systems indicates that LeechHijack operates effectively and is not mitigated by existing defense mechanisms. Our investigation also explores mitigation strategies, establishing a foundational framework for enhancing future defenses.   The findings underscore a critical security vulnerability in systems utilizing large language models, emphasizing the necessity for rigorous assessment of third-party tools. All experimental procedures adhered to stringent ethical and security guidelines. The LeechHijack attack detailed herein is purely demonstrative, serving to underscore the issue rather than incite malicious conduct. No real systems, platforms, or services were compromised during this study. All experiments were executed within secure, controlled environments specifically established for this research, without interfacing with real servers to prevent any potential data breaches.
