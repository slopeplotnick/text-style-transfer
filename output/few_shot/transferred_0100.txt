Recent advancements in large language models (LLMs) have primarily centered around leveraging increased computational resources during inference. Notable examples include models such as OpenAI's o1 and DeepSeek-R1, which extend their reasoning processes during testing by iteratively working through ideas, generating hypotheses, and correcting errors prior to providing a final response. This trend of enhancing inference-time reasoning has gained traction due to its observed improvements in model accuracy and problem-solving capabilities. However, elongating the reasoning process does not unilaterally lead to better results and, in some cases, may degrade performance, such as when models exhibit "overthinking" behavior. Empirical evidence suggests that correct solutions often arise from relatively more concise reasoning processes.  An alternative strategy involves executing multiple reasoning processes in parallel and aggregating their results, typically by selecting the most frequent answer in cases where the task has a singular correct solution. This approach has demonstrated success in structured domains, such as mathematical problem-solving or multiple-choice question answering. Nevertheless, many real-world tasks lack a definitive or singularly correct answer. For instance, in code generation, the objective is to produce functional programs, while in research-oriented tasks, success may require leveraging external tools, developing comprehensive plans, or generating detailed explanations. In such contexts, selecting the most frequent response is insufficient, as no single answer may be universally optimal, despite the effectiveness of this method for simpler tasks.  This limitation raises a critical research question: how can the advantages of parallel reasoning processes be harnessed to address tasks without well-defined answers, without relying solely on majority-based aggregation? To address this challenge, we propose THINKMERGE, a novel methodology designed to synthesize the outputs of multiple reasoning processes during inference to construct a high-quality response.
