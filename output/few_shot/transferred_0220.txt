To address these challenges, it is imperative to develop tools capable of tracking the evolution and propagation of reasoning over time, rather than merely evaluating the correctness of outcomes at specific moments. The concept of the *Emergent Systemic Risk Horizon* represents the threshold wherein the assumption that processes are independent or reversible is no longer valid. Beyond this horizon, safety considerations must encompass the dynamics of the entire interconnected system, emphasizing stability, coherence, and adaptability in environments characterized by continuous interaction. Accomplishing this necessitates novel experimental methodologies, longitudinal data collection, and frameworks for analyzing group behavior across temporal scales. Furthermore, the development of governance mechanisms—including regulatory systems and oversight strategies—must operate at a scale commensurate with the complexity of these expansive networks.  The subsequent section introduces the concept of *Institutional AI*, a paradigm aimed at embedding oversight and governance directly within systems composed of highly interconnected components. As language model systems (LMS) increase in complexity, adaptive management strategies that evolve synchronously with the systems themselves become critical. When LMS engage in autonomous activities such as reasoning, negotiating, and collaborative interaction without persistent human supervision, traditional approaches—including centralized control, static safety protocols, or periodic evaluations—prove insufficient. These methods presuppose stable, easily monitored systems with explicit accountability, conditions seldom applicable to such advanced, dynamic architectures.  Institutional AI proposes an integrated framework whereby mechanisms for governance—such as rules, evaluation criteria, and monitoring protocols—are inherently embedded within the systems rather than externally imposed. This approach draws selectively from established principles of human institutions, including shared decision-making frameworks, procedural transparency, and rule adaptability, while reengineering these features to accommodate the unique requirements of model networks. In contrast, *Constitutional AI* involves training individual models to adhere to predefined rules or principles, ensuring alignment and consistency in isolated contexts. However, this approach is insufficient for addressing systemic risks arising from interactions among multiple models. Institutional AI seeks to address these broader challenges by focusing on coordination, adaptability, and systemic safety across interconnected networks.
