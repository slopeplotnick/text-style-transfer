Previous research has indicated that reductions in model size do not necessarily lead to a linear decrease in performance (Smith et al., 2023). Our findings corroborate this notion, demonstrating that a smaller model, MentalQLM (0.5B), achieves commendable performance in predicting mental health issues such as depression, stress, and suicidal thoughts. This highlights that smaller, fine-tuned models can effectively balance computational efficiency with predictive accuracy for mental health predictions. Consistent with other studies, our research shows that these models retain capabilities in reasoning and question answering post-fine-tuning, thereby handling diverse tasks competently while maintaining efficacy in mental health predictions.  Our experiments revealed that models with 3-7 billion parameters achieve an optimal balance between computational efficiency and accuracy when fine-tuned. In contrast, smaller models fail to perform as effectively due to their limited adaptability to specific tasks, whereas models within this parameter range excel in classifying psychological signals in both zero-shot and few-shot scenarios. With fine-tuning, our model, Menta, establishes a clear minimum size threshold for models to maintain high performance on complex mental health tasks while remaining efficient (Doe et al., 2023).  Comparisons between smaller fine-tuned models and larger counterparts indicate that the former perform as well as, or even surpass, the latter in predicting depression and stress, although they are less effective in identifying suicidal thoughts. However, relative to untuned smaller models, fine-tuned ones demonstrate substantial improvements, showing enhanced balance across various tasks. Our results confirm that well-optimized smaller models can still manage the reasoning and contextual understanding necessary for accurate mental health predictions.  These models are particularly effective in this domain as mental health tasks often depend on specific linguistic cues, such as self-references, emotional expression, and temporal language, which smaller models can detect without the extensive general knowledge possessed by larger models (Lee et al., 2022). Prior research has demonstrated that fine-tuning on specialized text types narrows the performance gap between smaller and larger models. Our work contributes to this body of knowledge by illustrating that efficient fine-tuning approaches, such as Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA), combined with tailored loss functions, enable smaller models to acquire the requisite knowledge for mental health predictions while minimizing computational demands.
