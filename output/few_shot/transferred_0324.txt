Natural Language Processing (NLP), originating from the foundational ideas of Swiss linguist Ferdinand de Saussure, emphasizes that the meaning of language emerges from relationships between words and their distinctions from one another (Saussure, 1916). Communication is facilitated by shared language structures. In 1950, Alan Turing proposed the concept of a "thinking machine," suggesting that a machine capable of interacting with humans via a teleprinter could demonstrate cognitive abilities (Turing, 1950). Contemporary NLP is pivotal for understanding human language and generating contextually appropriate responses. Prominent applications include intelligent assistants, such as Siri and Alexa, which autonomously process user queries and execute tasks.    Advanced AI systems known as Large Language Models (LLMs) leverage deep learning techniques, enabling the generation of human-like text (Brown et al., 2020; Kaplan et al., 2022). LLMs rely on transformer architectures (Vaswani et al., 2017), which facilitate the processing of intricate linguistic features and extended text dependencies. Consequently, these models excel at tasks like language translation, conversational engagement, and content creation, proving valuable across diverse sectors of research and industry. A notable example is OpenAI’s ChatGPT, a widely utilized LLM in everyday contexts.    Despite their capabilities, LLMs occasionally produce errors termed "hallucinations," wherein the models generate plausible-sounding but inaccurate or entirely fabricated information (Ji et al., 2023). Such errors typically arise in scenarios beyond the model's training scope and can lead to consequences ranging from minor misunderstandings to significant disruptions in domains like language translation, software development, or autonomous systems. Addressing hallucinations is critical for enhancing the reliability, safety, and utility of these models, particularly in contexts demanding high levels of precision. One solution involves employing fine-tuning techniques, which calibrate pre-trained models to excel in specific tasks by training them on curated, domain-specific datasets (Howard & Gugger, 2018). Fine-tuning builds upon the foundational linguistic knowledge acquired during initial training, refining the model's ability to generalize while improving task-specific accuracy.
