When concepts are not distinctly defined in data features, probing techniques are frequently employed to identify neurons or neuron groups that optimally represent target concepts. These probes are predominantly linear but can be non-linear when concepts are not linearly separable. Probes may be trained using either supervised methods, requiring labeled data for the desired concept, or unsupervised methods, which identify concepts that are significant to the model but not necessarily comprehensible to humans. This research extends beyond mere observation analysis to modeling structural equations, incorporating various modeling levels, from Fully Specified Causal Models (FSCMs), which are readily interpretable with domain knowledge, to Partially Specified Causal Models (PSCMs) that encompass all FSCMs compatible with the data. This highlights the critical importance of selecting the appropriate modeling approach and how partial information can be advantageous when complete FSCMs are unavailable. Galhotra et al. (2021) discuss an Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) method leveraging FSCMs to compute probabilities of sufficiency and necessity. Their focus is on tabular data, integrating causal modeling into data features, assuming these represent high-level concepts and allowing for the inclusion of additional concepts not present in the model input. This method is effective only when features correspond to meaningful concepts and does not require a causal generator. It presumes a preference for model output explanation, indicating which input values can alter a prediction favorably or which values are necessary to maintain a preferred prediction outcome.
