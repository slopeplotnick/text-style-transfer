Faulty code is non-executable, presenting challenges for our methodology, which relies on the execution of erroneous lines in at least one failing test instance. Lines of code that are never executed cannot be analyzed for faults. Consequently, insufficient test coverage may lead to undiscovered errors. To address this limitation, our approach leverages multiple failing tests in conjunction with lightweight static analysis techniques.    Regarding Bounded Model Checking (BMC), the method employs restricted program versions, incorporating constraints such as loop bounds and depth limits. While these constraints improve computational feasibility, they inherently omit some program behaviors, potentially overlooking valid faults. Furthermore, truncated iterations may lead to findings that are not representative of the complete program's behavior. These limitations are carefully calibrated and disclosed alongside the results, yet remain intrinsic drawbacks of BMC.    Concerning pointers, arrays, and undefined behavior, as detailed in Section 5.1, the CFAULTS framework integrates tools such as CPPCHECK and CLANG-TIDY to detect issues such as uninitialized variables and runtime errors (e.g., division by zero). Upon identifying such faults, CFAULTS terminates the analysis and alerts the user. Moreover, CFAULTS employs CBMC's memory-safety checks to detect violations, including out-of-bounds memory access and unsafe pointer arithmetic. However, CFAULTS adheres to CBMC's interpretation of ANSI C standards; thus, any behavior deemed permissible by CBMC is treated as acceptable, while issues not flagged by CBMC may remain undetected. Enhancing CBMC’s configuration to better align with intended C specifications constitutes future research.    Regarding external validity, the proposed approach was evaluated using benchmark and student programs that compile correctly and provide well-defined specifications, such as assertions or expected outputs. Application to larger-scale programs incorporating extensive input/output operations, concurrency, or complex library dependencies, as well as to programs with specifications expressed in more advanced formats, may require further adaptation and could yield different outcomes.
