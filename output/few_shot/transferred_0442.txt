Large Language Models (LLMs) frequently generate inaccurate content, presenting information in a fluent and convincing manner while failing to maintain factual correctness. These inaccuracies, commonly referred to as "hallucinations," undermine trust in such models and hinder their adoption in real-world applications. To address this issue, researchers have proposed two distinct approaches: hallucination detection, which focuses on identifying inaccuracies generated by the model itself, and fact verification, which involves cross-referencing outputs against external knowledge sources. Despite their shared goal of ensuring factuality, these two subfields have evolved independently, utilizing distinct methodologies, datasets, and evaluation frameworks, thereby slowing progress in addressing the problem comprehensively.  In this study, we aim to bridge these divergent approaches through the development of a unified framework, termed *UniFact*. This framework facilitates a direct comparison of hallucination detection and fact verification by generating model outputs and systematically assessing their factual accuracy. Our empirical evaluations, which include extensive testing across multiple models and methodologies, yield three key insights: (1) neither approach consistently outperforms the other; (2) each method specializes in identifying different categories of factual inaccuracies; and (3) a hybrid approach that combines elements of both methodologies achieves superior performance. Beyond evaluating these methods, we analyze the historical divergence between the two subfields and provide evidence for the necessity of their unification.  The findings underline the imperative of developing integrated strategies to address hallucinations in LLMs, particularly given their transformative impact on digital systems. LLMs have become indispensable in advanced search systems and complex tasks once considered infeasible. However, their deployment in critical domains such as healthcare, legal systems, and journalism is constrained by their propensity to produce fluent but factually incorrect content. In these contexts, where accuracy is paramount, hallucinations not only erode user trust but also propagate misinformation and potentially cause considerable harm. Thus, establishing robust mechanisms for detecting and mitigating factual inaccuracies is not merely a technical challenge but a foundational step toward building reliable and trustworthy intelligent systems.
