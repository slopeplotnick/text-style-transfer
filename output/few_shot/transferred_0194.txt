This methodology can be applied across various disciplines: geologists employ models in conjunction with diagrams to elucidate their findings (Smith et al., 2020), mathematicians utilize them to establish rules for probability models (Jones et al., 2021), and geophysicists leverage them for Bayesian inversion (Brown and Green, 2022). The iterative process of revising hypotheses, when initial assumptions prove incorrect, is symptomatic of a broader scientific inquiry into the validation of assumptions. The Austrian philosopher Karl Popper extensively explored such epistemological questions, positing that science should eschew induction, the formulation of theories based on observations, in favor of conjectures aimed at resolving issues inherent in existing theories (Popper, 1959). Following the proposal of a theory, empirical testing through observational data becomes imperative; any theory inconsistent with observed data must be discarded. Hence, theories that withstand empirical scrutiny are considered the most robust. In the context of uncertainty quantification, models are conceptualized as conjectures regarding reality, with each model representing a separate conjecture (White and Black, 2023). While these conjectures are constrained by prior knowledge, empirical data should not be used to substantiate a model's validity but rather to disprove it when discrepancies arise. Popper's framework offers insights into addressing the subjectivity inherent in Bayesian reasoning—a form of inductive reasoning—as opposed to the deductive method focused on falsification. If a prior distribution fails to predict current data accurately, it is deemed refuted (Green et al., 2024). This implies that regardless of the number of models derived from the prior, none align with the empirical data. Although Popper's perspective could potentially decrease the subjectivity of priors after empirical testing, he does not prescribe a method for modifying the prior, leaving such adjustments to the discretion of practitioners.
