Concerns regarding the legal and ethical implications of generative models have been escalating, particularly in relation to the utilization of copyrighted materials during the training processes of these models. A critical issue revolves around the attribution of creative contributions and the moral rights associated therewith. Several lawsuits have been filed alleging that unauthorized use of copyrighted content infringes upon intellectual property rights. Notably, The New York Times has initiated legal action against OpenAI, accusing it of utilizing its news archives without consent, and Getty Images has similarly filed a lawsuit against Stability AI for employing its database content without authorization. In addition to these legal proceedings, there is a growing discourse among artists. Esteemed musicians such as Elton John, Dua Lipa, along with 200 other artists, have formally petitioned technology companies to safeguard human creativity against AI capabilities that replicate voices and styles without proper authorization. In response to these concerns, policy recommendations are emerging. The European Union has introduced the AI Act, which mandates transparency of AI systems. Concurrently, the United Kingdom and UNESCO have established guidelines aimed at ensuring responsible AI usage that respects privacy and intellectual property. Denmark has proposed an amendment to its copyright legislation granting individuals rights over their voice, likeness, and physical representation, marking a pioneering move within the European continent. This proposal intends to address the legal challenges posed by AI replication of personal attributes, as emphasized by the Danish Minister of Culture, underscoring the importance of safeguarding individuals' rights to their personal identity in the face of generative AI advancements. Despite these regulatory efforts, many generative models remain opaque, complicating the analysis of how training data influences their outputs. Even open-source models present challenges due to their vast scale, rendering exact reproduction impractical. Existing methodologies, such as influence functions and retraining-based attribution, necessitate insights into the internal mechanisms of these models.
