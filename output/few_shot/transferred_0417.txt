Recent advances in engineering have introduced supervisory systems where intermediaries act as facilitators between human users and worker agents. These supervisory entities review outputs, identify errors, and intervene when operational thresholds are exceeded. This concept is situated within the framework of "Institutional AI," which will be elaborated upon in subsequent sections. However, the implementation of supervisory systems alone is insufficient to ensure robustness. Notably, supervisors operate using the same underlying architectures as the agents they oversee, rendering them susceptible to identical errors or recursive failure modes. Excessive agreement among supervisors can exacerbate this issue, potentially resulting in collective errors or intentional malpractice. Addressing these challenges necessitates the introduction of additional supervisory hierarchies, diversified evaluation mechanisms, redundant observation systems, and a clear delineation of authority among supervisory entities.  When autonomous systems comprise multiple interacting agents, safety considerations become increasingly complex. Centralized control paradigms and static regulatory frameworks are inadequate in such contexts; instead, decentralized networks capable of detecting, mitigating, and resolving emergent issues autonomously are imperative. Failures within one model propagate throughout the network, amplifying risks and cascading systemic vulnerabilities. Ensuring the safety of artificial intelligence systems requires a comprehensive understanding of the origins and propagation of these group-level failure phenomena. Furthermore, the interactions between language models highlight the limitations of existing risk frameworks addressing bias, misuse, or adversarial weaknesses. While individual models can undergo rigorous evaluation, the complexities of multi-agent interactions give rise to unforeseen failure modalities that remain poorly understood.  In response to these challenges, the concept of the Emergent Systemic Risk Horizon (ESRH) has been proposed. ESRH delineates the critical threshold at which the collective behavior of interacting models begins to deteriorate, even when individual models perform reliably in isolation. This paradigm underscores the necessity of expanding current methodologies for evaluating and mitigating risks in interconnected AI systems.
