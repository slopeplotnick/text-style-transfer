Our investigation revealed that the Menta model demonstrates superior performance in the identification of depression and stress compared to the detection of suicide risk. Both fine-tuned and standard language models, including larger variants, tend to perform better on tasks related to depression and stress than on those concerning suicide risk. For instance, a review by Bauer (2024) indicates that language models frequently struggle to accurately interpret subtle indicators of suicidality, primarily due to the paucity of data and the models' reliance on context or timing information, which may not be apparent in isolated posts. Additionally, research conducted by Lamichhane (2023) reported an F1-score of 0.86 for depression detection, in contrast to a significantly lower score of 0.37 for suicidality detection using social media data. This discrepancy suggests that depression and stress have more distinct patterns and are supported by more extensive datasets, making them easier to detect. In contrast, suicidality is less prevalent, more context-dependent, and poses greater challenges for model interpretation. Consequently, language models may be more effective for general screening applications, such as identifying depression or stress, but may not be as proficient in detailed suicide risk detection without incorporating diverse data types or longitudinal tracking. There are, however, limitations to our findings. Although models like Qwen-3 (4B) exhibit robust predictive capabilities for mental health issues, several factors hinder the generalizability of our results to other contexts. Our study exclusively analyzed social media posts, omitting critical factors such as temporal changes in writing style, user profile information, and interaction patterns that could enhance mental health comprehension. Integrating these elements could strengthen future research, rendering it more applicable and realistic. Another limitation is the narrow focus on depression, stress, and suicidal thoughts, which represent only a fraction of the spectrum of mental health disorders. Other conditions, such as anxiety, bipolar disorder, and emotional disturbances, were not encompassed in our study. Moreover, we utilized public, anonymous social media data rather than data from clinical environments. This approach preserves privacy and facilitates reproducibility but restricts the assessment of safety, ethical considerations, and long-term reliability within real healthcare contexts.
