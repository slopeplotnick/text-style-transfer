Significant risks emerge when a system begins to behave in ways that deviate from the intentions of its developers, effectively exhibiting autonomous dynamics. At this stage, addressing isolated components or connections proves ineffective, as the system has surpassed a critical threshold of controllability. Once a system attains this level of autonomy, it operates independently of external input, rendering proactive interventions insufficient; mitigation efforts are confined to reactive measures, and conventional safety protocols often fail to provide adequate safeguards. Importantly, the risks discussed herein do not necessarily arise from deliberate malfeasance. Many systemic failures occur intrinsically, driven by the complex interactions within the system, emergent feedback loops, iterative processes, or self-reinforcing mechanisms, even when all agents involved adhere to their prescribed roles and objectives.   This category of risk is attributable to systemic configurations—such as feedback dynamics, shared operational contexts, or goal alignment—rather than intentional sabotage. Nevertheless, malicious actors could exploit these naturally occurring vulnerabilities. For example, an adversary capable of altering communications, modifying instructions, or disrupting system linkages could accelerate failure modes, induce adverse cooperative behaviors, or undermine collective decision-making processes.   This framework conceptualizes systemic risks as existing on a continuum, encompassing both naturally emergent and intentionally exacerbated failures. At one extreme, autonomous disruptions arise from internal interactions and system design; at the other, external agents exploit these mechanisms to instigate or amplify system failures. Such risks underscore the inadequacy of attributing large-scale system failures to isolated component malfunctions. As interconnections intensify, the distinction between causation and outcomes becomes increasingly ambiguous. Actions beneficial at the component level may inadvertently propagate adverse effects across the broader system.   Traditional evaluation methodologies, which focus on discrete outcomes or static testing conditions, fall short in addressing these challenges. Such approaches primarily assess compliance with predefined rules or specifications, rather than analyzing systemic behaviors under dynamic and realistic conditions. To address safety within this paradigm, it becomes imperative to shift from part-level evaluations to a holistic perspective. This entails examining the interactions among components, feedback mechanisms, and the system's capacity to adapt across multiple interconnected elements.
