The increased transferability of adversarial attacks due to adversarial training (AT) does not stem from specific architectural designs but rather from how AT alters the processing mechanisms in models. This phenomenon is observed across various model architectures, making it a critical consideration for system security. To investigate why AT enhances transferability, researchers analyzed the decision-making explanations of models using the integrated gradients method [Sundararajan et al., 2017]. Specifically, they compared two instances of the DenseNet121 model: one trained with adversarial training (AT) and another trained with standard training (ST). The analysis revealed that AT-trained models tend to focus on salient features of an image, such as the head, body, and legs of an object (e.g., an ostrich), whereas ST-trained models predominantly attend to fine-grained details, such as feather textures or background elements. This distinction is significant, as AT models emphasize generalizable features that are common across various images, whereas ST models emphasize specific features that are less transferable across models. Consequently, adversarial examples generated by AT-trained models are more effective across diverse architectures, as they target shared, robust features. For instance, architectures such as DenseNet121 and DenseNet201 exhibit similar feature processing patterns, and MobileNetV3 shows comparable vulnerabilities due to its design foundations in EfficientNet [Tan and Le, 2019]. As a result, MobileNetV3 demonstrates greater susceptibility to attacks originating from EfficientNet-based models.
