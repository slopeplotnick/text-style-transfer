Psychometric scales and questionnaires serve as pivotal instruments in research domains such as psychology, medicine, social sciences, education, and human-computer interaction. When a novel concept or theory is introduced and investigated, researchers typically necessitate the development of instruments for its measurement. However, the construction of these tools demands substantial effort, time, financial investment, and resources (Stefana et al., 2025). This study examines the feasibility of streamlining this process through the utilization of computer simulations to generate psychometric scales with data originated from Large Language Models (LLMs). The article elucidates the underlying rationale, delineates four empirical studies that assess its efficacy, and concludes with an analysis of the findings alongside recommendations for subsequent research. The recruitment of a participant sample that accurately represents the general population is often costly and time-intensive, leading researchers to frequently rely on convenience samples of university student volunteers. This practice challenges the generalizability of the findings to broader populations (Hanel & Vione, 2016; Wild et al., 2022). Recently, scholars have increasingly adopted online platforms such as Prolific.co and Amazon MTurk for participant recruitment (Buhrmester et al., 2018; Douglas et al., 2023; Huang et al., 2015). These platforms expedite and reduce the costs associated with participant recruitment and can occasionally facilitate the assembly of samples that more accurately reflect specific demographics, such as those in the UK or US. Nonetheless, ethical concerns have been raised regarding the use of these platforms, as some participants may originate from disadvantaged backgrounds (Huang et al., 2015; Huff & Tingley, 2015; Moss et al., 2023). When researchers explore a nascent concept and lack a validated scale for its measurement, or when scale validation is unfeasible, it is common for them to devise bespoke questionnaires informed solely by theoretical frameworks. Although this approach conserves resources and time, it may yield results with limited external validity (M. K. Lee et al., 2020).
