The primary question under investigation is whether modern language models (LLMs), which are trained merely to predict subsequent words without explicit instruction on understanding concepts like perception, inherently develop analogous methods for information processing. Understanding how these models manage uncertain information could enhance the development of systems capable of handling varied input quality. To examine this, methodologies from psychophysics—a discipline concerned with human perception—were employed to analyze model behavior as black boxes, meaning the internal mechanisms were not scrutinized, but rather the focus was on the models' behavioral responses. By controlling input uncertainty and assessing for Bayesian processing indicators—where decisions are made based on probabilities—we aimed to ascertain whether these models exhibit human-like perceptual behaviors, despite lacking targeted training in this area. Our findings indicate that certain classical psychophysics tasks are well-suited for these models, revealing intriguing patterns. These tasks effectively evaluate model capabilities in handling diverse input types, particularly within controlled environments where precision measurement is feasible. Unlike real-world tasks fraught with excessive noise that impedes accurate measurement, this approach facilitates the testing of specific strategies and the identification of weaknesses undetectable in standard benchmarks. The study presents three substantial contributions: Firstly, the development of a psychophysics framework for LLMs encompassing four tasks that assess attributes such as length, location, distance, and duration, allowing for manipulation of factors like noise and context to observe changes in model behavior. Secondly, the introduction of a new benchmark, BayesBench, which evaluates model performance, information integration efficiency, and consistency with Bayesian reasoning through a novel scoring mechanism. Thirdly, the discovery that certain advanced models exhibit Bayesian reasoning-consistent behaviors, albeit uncovering a critical limitation where even highly accurate models fail to apply reliable strategies in specific contexts. Psychophysics, through its exploration of human perception, has demonstrated that consistent patterns endure in the relationship between physical stimuli and human perception, as encapsulated by established principles such as the Weber-Fechner law and others.
