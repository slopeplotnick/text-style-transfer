In all three models, the influence of the variable "young" on both image and prediction remains consistent, indicating its robustness as a measure irrespective of the granularity level. However, if an explanatory model considers variables in isolation and disregards their interdependencies, it reflects solely the direct effect. This direct effect may equate to the total effect in the initial model but could be null in the final one, suggesting that direct influence is not always a reliable metric. Section 5.3.1 elucidates the challenges arising from incorrect model assumptions, advocating for cautious model selection. When assumptions introduce bias into explanations, it becomes imperative to either modify the model or ensure the explainer is aware of the biased context within which the explanations operate. This does not imply that complex models inherently surpass simpler ones. To ensure that explanations are consistent with user comprehension, the causal model should align with the individual's understanding of the world. Simplifying explanations by omitting intricate causal mechanisms in favor of a more straightforward presentation of crucial information enhances understanding. Moreover, an advantage of the causal explanation model discussed in Section 5.3.1 is its utilization of expert knowledge to more effectively identify and eliminate spurious correlations compared to purely data-driven models. Thus, the explanation model is capable of detecting false relationships identified by the predictor, even when the same data informs the explanation model's learning process. With increased accessibility to expert knowledge through Large Language Models (LLMs), as demonstrated in the example, this capability becomes particularly valuable for enhancing models by identifying unintended behaviors.
