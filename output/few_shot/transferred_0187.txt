The classification of temporal phases, workload balancing, and strategic decision-making for each phase are integral components of the workflow initiated by Node 1, referred to as the Phase Identification Node within the nine-node LTAA framework. Node 1 serves as the initial checkpoint, assessing the system's state and determining the temporal context for subsequent steps in the process. The Phase Detection Node is responsible for evaluating task progress and updating workload metrics. As illustrated in Figure 9a, this node synthesizes four primary inputs from the system: the number of completed tasks, the total task count, the distribution of workload across robotic units, and the ideal workload configuration. These parameters establish the contextual groundwork for subsequent decision-making within the workflow.  Within the Phase Detection Node, the framework evaluates progress to ascertain whether the system is operating in the early, middle, or late phase of task execution. Each of these phases corresponds to distinct decision scenarios that shape strategies for optimizing success rates and ensuring equitable workload distribution in subsequent nodes. Furthermore, the node calculates a balance score to quantify the uniformity of task distribution and assesses any deviations from the ideal workload configuration. These metrics collectively encapsulate progress and workload dynamics in a streamlined manner. The node concludes its operation by updating the system state with four key variables—allocation_phase, balance_score, imbalance_severity, and operational_mode—which are then transmitted to the next node in the sequence. This mechanism ensures that subsequent nodes are equipped with up-to-date information regarding progress and workload distribution.  As depicted in Figure 9a, the workflow adheres to a unidirectional flow, wherein the Phase Detection Node converts raw system data into structured decision-making information for the next phase. Effective phase allocation strategies necessitate robust reasoning capabilities from Large Language Models (LLMs). However, the variability in LLM outputs may undermine their reliability for high-stakes tasks such as construction-related operations. To address the limitations identified during the implementation of SMART-LLM, the authors devised a validation framework incorporating a weighted assessment and feedback mechanism to evaluate and enhance reasoning quality.
