The model is trained using both single-user inputs and multi-user conversational data. In such scenarios, posts may interweave content from different individuals without clear demarcation, which complicates understanding and diverges from standard training conditions. This discrepancy, combined with extended input lengths, can lead the model to prioritize earlier significant segments, potentially neglecting broader contextual information due to challenges in maintaining stable long-context processing. With precise guidance and tailored adjustments, the proposed Menta model demonstrates strong performance in mental health-related tasks, such as stress detection, assessment of depression severity, and suicidal ideation and risk evaluation, effectively handling diverse mental health outcomes. Notably, the Menta model performs competitively with larger models, particularly in depression and stress prediction tasks, occasionally outperforming them. These findings underscore the potential of well-optimized smaller models in supporting continuous mental health monitoring, offering a cost-efficient and high-performing solution for early detection in digital mental health applications.   This study addresses three key questions regarding the utility of smaller models, with a focus on the fine-tuned Menta model, in predicting mental health outcomes, alongside a discussion of current limitations and directions for future research. First, can smaller models effectively predict mental health status? The findings indicate that smaller models are indeed capable of identifying mental health conditions from social media textual data. Despite their reduced parameter count, the well-trained Menta model successfully manages complex tasks, demonstrating its potential for accurate predictions in low-resource or constrained settings. While prior research (Jia et al., 2025) explored smaller models in mental health applications, primarily focusing on models in the 2-7 billion parameter range, the present study evaluates models strictly below 7 billion parameters. Specifically, the results reveal that models with fewer than approximately 3.8 billion parameters—such as those around 1 billion—exhibit significant performance degradation, frequently producing blank or random outputs in zero- or few-shot settings. This finding suggests that reducing language model sizes below a critical threshold results in an abrupt rather than gradual decline in task performance, thereby identifying a distinct lower limit. This work makes a novel contribution by delineating this limit specifically within the context of mental health prediction tasks.
