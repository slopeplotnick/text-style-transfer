Researchers have investigated the evolution of communication within multi-agent systems, with a particular emphasis on the potential pathways for language evolution. A seminal example is the Talking Heads project, developed by Steels (2000, 2015). This project demonstrates the emergence of language as agents interact and reference environmental objects without initially using language. Through interactive games, such as naming objects in their environment, these agents develop an artificial language (Steels, 2001; see also Steels and Kaplan, 2001; Steels and Loetzch, 2012 for further discussion on lexical development). More recent advancements in this project have explored the emergence and acquisition of syntax, or sentence structure (Steels and Beuls, 2017; Steels et al., 2018; van Trijp, 2016). A notable distinction between the Talking Heads and MODOMA projects lies in the former's involvement of both agents in the creation and learning of the language, in contrast to MODOMA's focus on a single agent acquiring segments of a natural language. Additionally, Talking Heads agents develop an artificial rather than a natural language. Alishahi's research has examined language acquisition using algorithms applied to both human-produced and artificially generated datasets. These studies (Alishahi and Chrupala, 2009, 2012; Alishahi and Stevenson, 2008) investigate the learning of word categories, meanings, and fundamental sentence structures. Matusevych et al. (2013) sought to enhance artificial input simulating interactions akin to those between children and adults, comparable to data derived from the CHILDES database (MacWhinney, 2014). However, unlike MODOMA, their language-learning algorithm does not participate in the dialogue. Beekhuizen et al. (2014) adopted an alternative methodology by analyzing the learning of grammatical patterns through a single agent processing sentence representations and their meanings. Finally, Conner et al. (2008, 2009) implemented the Baby SRL project, investigating how algorithms can learn to annotate the syntactic roles of words within sentences utilizing data from the CHILDES database or artificially constructed sentences.
