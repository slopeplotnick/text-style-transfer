Model innovation continues to advance; however, in practical applications, certain decisions that are often underrepresented in academic discourse are of significant importance. These decisions encompass the definitions of virality, the types of data that can be collected (e.g., textual or numerical), and the evaluation of models considering real-world constraints such as access, time, and class imbalance. This paper prioritizes evaluation when making these determinations. We analyze two recent datasets to demonstrate that the design of labels and the nature of the data (text alone versus text with additional signals) are as crucial as modifications to the model itself. We provide explicit examples illustrating these trade-offs. Despite our focus on evaluation design rather than model innovation, we illustrate that simpler models can match or surpass the performance of contemporary multimodal and graph-based systems on standard benchmarks such as FAKENewsNET. Our study particularly emphasizes two tasks: fake news detection and early virality prediction, aiming to determine the most critical data and definitions for maintaining performance stability under real-world conditions. We compare textual data (such as sentence-level transformer embeddings) with basic numerical data (such as user statistics, early engagement figures, and timing information). These tasks are evaluated on datasets with varying structures: one dataset comprises isolated news items, while the other contains sequential social media posts. We address two supervised classification tasks across two datasets, thereby creating four test scenarios: fake news detection and virality prediction on EvONS and FAKENewsNET. For the fake news detection task, we predict whether an item (either an article or a tweet series) is false or true, utilizing both EvONS articles and FAKENewsNET tweet series, and report outcomes using standard metrics. In the case of FAKENewsNET, we utilize tweet series sourced from the repository. The data is anonymized using IDs and requires API hydration; we obtained the Politifact split through prior academic sharing, although the GossipCop data was irretrievable due to current constraints. Each instance comprises a series of tweets regarding the same news article, illustrating its dissemination.
