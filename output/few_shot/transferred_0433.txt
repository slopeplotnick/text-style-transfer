A novel metric, termed *Verifier Gain*, has been introduced to evaluate the utility of verifiers beyond their standalone accuracy. Simply assessing verifier accuracy does not provide a comprehensive understanding; instead, Verifier Gain quantifies the improvement achieved during testing when a verifier is employed. Our investigation examined how verifiers contribute to rejecting incorrect responses, revealing that this metric aligns well with observed real-world performance. Additionally, we analyzed the efficacy of different verification paradigms, including self-verification, intra-group verification, and inter-group verification. Results indicate that inter-group verification consistently outperforms self-verification, primarily due to the reduced effectiveness of verifiers when they are too similar to the solver. As language models improve—whether through increased scale, extended training, or enhanced accuracy—they exhibit diminished proficiency in self-verification but demonstrate superior effectiveness in verifying outputs from models of differing configurations.  Further, we assessed the relative ease of verification across various task types. Tasks with lower inherent complexity are generally easier to verify, although self-verification proves ineffective across numerous task domains. Mathematical and logical tasks, in particular, often yield higher verifier effectiveness. Verifiers can operate in two principal modes: evaluating the correctness of final outputs or validating the intermediate steps leading to those outputs. Recent research has proposed several approaches enabling models to self-verify, such as predicting intermediate elements of a question from the solution, asking self-directed fact-checking questions, or training verifiers specifically for assessing final outputs or collaborating with solvers. Other studies have focused on models capable of scrutinizing the procedural steps underlying the problem-solving process.
