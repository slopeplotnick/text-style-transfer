The results indicate that employing reasoning to enhance safety alignment improves the efficacy of omnimodal guardrails across diverse input modalities, facilitating understanding and generalization in complex safety scenarios where reliance solely on labels proves inadequate. To investigate the transferability of safety knowledge acquired from one input modality to another, a series of experiments was conducted. Specifically, OMNIGUARD was trained using data from three input modalities and evaluated both on those modalities and a holdout modality to assess its performance on novel input types. Performance metrics, namely average F1 scores and accuracy, are provided in Table 5.   Two primary findings were derived from these experiments. First, OMNIGUARD demonstrates strong cross-modal generalization capabilities, achieving comparable performance on unseen input modalities relative to those used during training (mean F1: 79.4 vs. 81.8). This suggests that OMNIGUARD can effectively identify harmful patterns across text, images, videos, and audio, thereby learning safety rules applicable across these modalities. Second, an exception was observed with audio data. The F1 score for audio data used during training (64.6%) was slightly lower than the F1 score for audio data excluded during training (65.1%). This discrepancy is attributed to the variant of OMNIGUARD trained without text data, which exhibited reduced performance on the WildGuard-TTS test—a dataset comprising audio samples generated from text using a text-to-speech model. The absence of textual data during training impeded the model's ability to process audio effectively, highlighting the interdependencies between different modalities (e.g., text and speech) in learning safety rules. Furthermore, the findings underscore the potential for knowledge transfer across modalities that convey equivalent semantic content.
