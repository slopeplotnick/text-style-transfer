The researchers utilized the AI2-THOR dataset in lieu of their previous dataset, the TEACH dataset, to facilitate reproducibility and ensure equitable comparisons among algorithms. This modification permitted indirect comparisons of performance across analogous tasks. Notably, in their evaluation of LTAA against conventional MRTA methodologies, the researchers employed a consistent dataset. Furthermore, the authors are in the process of assessing SMART-LLM with the TEACH dataset to enhance the fairness of comparisons. The study was conducted on a limited scale, allowing for a comprehensive analysis of fundamental capabilities. However, it is important to note that the study did not encompass the full complexity of real-world construction tasks, which often involve intricate temporal and spatial challenges. Generalized features, such as elemental/compound and heavy/dexterous, were used to describe construction tasks, rather than specific ones, to confer a broader applicability to the study. This generalization was necessitated by the absence of an extensive database of authentic construction operations. The authors posit that the existence of such a database would enable the testing of LTAA across a broader range of scenarios involving dynamic planning and resource constraints, thereby demonstrating the robustness of the framework. Additionally, the variability in LLM outputs raises safety concerns in construction contexts. Despite conducting multiple evaluations to avert failures, the inherent randomness allowed for detailed reasoning but could potentially result in varied allocations. For construction sites where precision is imperative, more rigorous verification processes than those currently employed may be required. Regarding computing resources, the researchers were unable to quantify SMART-LLM's costs on a per-stage basis since all stages utilized a single combined prompt. To draw comparisons with LTAA, which focuses solely on allocation, they estimated SMART-LLM's costs by dividing the total tokens and time by three. Although this estimation is acceptable, it may not accurately reflect allocation-specific costs, and future research incorporating detailed assessments would provide a clearer understanding.
