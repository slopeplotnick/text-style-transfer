Our study demonstrates that prompt-engineered Large Language Models (LLMs), when paired with Isolation Forest scores, outperform traditional Joint Event Trees (JETs) and machine learning (ML) methods in anomaly detection and explanation tasks. Specifically, these systems excel in providing both accurate anomaly detection and interpretable, plain-language explanations. While challenges such as false positives, implementation costs, and real-world applicability persist, this research highlights the potential of AI to enhance auditing accuracy and accessibility. Notably, this study is the first to illustrate the applicability of LLMs in analyzing tax ledger data—a domain that demands high levels of transparency and reliability.   The findings of this research suggest that integrating LLMs with conventional anomaly signals achieves a balanced tradeoff among accuracy, recall, and interpretability. Future work will focus on employing larger, more diverse datasets to generalize these results and exploring the robustness of the method across varying prompts and model architectures. The current evaluation was limited to a single anonymous tax ledger and a simulated benchmark, factors that may constrain generalizability. Industry-specific, system-specific, and regional variations in real-world ledgers, coupled with the lack of ground truth labels, pose significant challenges. Additionally, synthetic labels generated by JETs and simulated anomalies may introduce biases, necessitating further validation using authentic audit datasets.  Model stability and interpretability remain contingent on prompt phrasing and the specific LLM architecture employed; minor modifications in phrasing or the use of different models can yield inconsistent outcomes. While our AuditCopilot framework mitigates discrepancies to some extent, it does not fully eliminate sensitivities to nuanced inputs, linguistic variations, or model updates. Moreover, although plain-language explanations are intended to facilitate audit processes, they may diverge from formal decision rules—even if auditors perceive them to be helpful.  Despite promising experimental outcomes, financial auditing applications of LLMs are subject to risks such as algorithmic errors, biases, prompt-related uncertainties, and evolving model weights. As such, this study underscores the importance of leveraging LLM-assisted methods as decision-support tools rather than standalone solutions. Human oversight, rigorous controls, and well-defined regulatory frameworks are essential prerequisites before deploying these methods in practical auditing scenarios.
