Energy conservation is a critical concern in various domains, including mobile applications and data center software. Determining and minimizing the energy consumption of software, even at a fundamental level, presents significant challenges, particularly when considering modern systems that perform dynamic optimizations. Programming languages such as Java, Scala, and Kotlin, which are executed on virtual machines with Java bytecode, illustrate this complexity. The energy consumption of software is influenced by multiple factors, including the compiler, the Java Virtual Machine (JVM), the garbage collector, the operating system, and the underlying hardware. Comprehensive understanding of how software design choices impact energy consumption necessitates familiarity with these diverse layers and the specific platform on which the software is deployed.  Previous research has examined methods for profiling energy consumption based on workloads. This involves executing software with a predetermined workload in an environment specifically designed for energy measurement, enabling determination of the energy usage attributable to various code segments. However, the practical application of this approach is hindered by the requirement for specialized hardware, limiting its accessibility in routine software engineering practices. While some hardware components can depict energy consumption for particular elements, such as the CPU, few are equipped to measure energy consumption across the entire platform.  To address these limitations, a model has been proposed that employs a Gaussian distribution to characterize four descriptive attributes of code segments, categorizing these components to assess their impact on energy consumption. Factors such as operational types, data types, data sizes, and device specifications are analyzed to elucidate how modifications, such as altering data types within an algorithm, affect energy consumption. The model emphasizes worst-case energy consumption scenarios for individual code statements, utilizing predictions to estimate energy consumption for functions and entire programs. The distributions offer insights into worst-case scenarios because single estimates fail to account for the full range of potential energy variations due to stochastic factors, such as temperature fluctuations or interference.  The model retains its statistical properties, including mean, skewness, and mode, enabling the computation of metrics such as entropy and probability functions that are not possible with singular estimates. By focusing on worst-case energy consumption, the model facilitates the prediction of energy demands, ensuring that software can meet energy requirements—a critical consideration for battery-operated devices and cost-efficient data center operations. This framework provides a foundation for program analysis and software verification tools to incorporate energy consumption estimates, thus enhancing energy efficiency in software development.
