In this context, we begin by exploring the limitations of this recent approach [16]. As shown in Figure 1(a), the model initially attends to both audio and visual inputs; however, as the reasoning process progresses, its focus shifts increasingly toward the text it has already generated. This shift causes the model to anchor its reasoning on the emotional cues present in the initial segment of generated text. Furthermore, the reasoning process typically starts with generating a video description, as the training data are structured such that visual descriptions precede auditory ones, followed by the reasoning component. However, this "visual-first" reasoning approach may not always be suitable, particularly in cases where emotional content is primarily conveyed through vocal tone or semantic meaning. When the initial visual-based reasoning proves irrelevant or inaccurate, the subsequent reasoning becomes progressively misaligned, as depicted in Figure 1(b). Consequently, the initial step of reasoning becomes disproportionately influential. Similar challenges arise in other domains, where early cues divert the reasoning process from the foundational evidence [17]. To address this issue, we propose Modality-Importance-Guided Reasoning (MIGR), a framework designed to mitigate such biases by ensuring that the reasoning process begins with the modality—audio, visual, or both—that most effectively captures the target emotion. To achieve this, we introduce Modality Importance (MI), a mechanism that identifies the modality most critical for accurate emotion recognition by evaluating the model's predictive performance across three conditions: audio-only input, visual-only input, and a combination of both. For example, if the model accurately identifies the target emotion using auditory cues alone but performs worse when visual information is included, this suggests that visual inputs may be irrelevant or even misleading. In such cases, auditory signals should be prioritized as the dominant modality. Building on the MI concept and leveraging recent advancements in reasoning-based model training [15, 16], we propose a two-stage learning framework: (i) modality-aligned Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) and (ii) modality-aligned reward optimization. During the SFT stage, we use MI to segment the reasoning text based on input modality and reorganize it to enhance coherence and alignment with the reasoning framework.
