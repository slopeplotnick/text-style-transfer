This paper explores why safety mechanisms designed for human-model interactions fail to scale in environments where large language models (LLMs) interact with one another. Current governance practices predominantly rely on single-agent safety measures, such as prompts, fine-tuning, and moderation layers, which constrain individual model behavior but overlook the complexities of multi-model dynamics. These approaches assume a dyadic framework: one model interacting with one user under consistent oversight. However, both research and industry are rapidly advancing toward ecosystems of interacting LLMs, where outputs are recursively reused as inputs across chains of agents. In these systems, local compliance can aggregate into systemic failure, even when each individual model is well-aligned. Therefore, this paper advocates for a shift from model-level safety to system-level safety, introducing the concept of the Emergent Systemic Risk Horizon (ESRH) to formalize how instability arises from interaction structures rather than isolated malfunctions. The paper contributes (i) a theoretical framework for understanding collective risks in interacting LLMs, (ii) a taxonomy linking micro, meso, and macro-level failure modes, and (iii) a design proposal for Institutional AI, an architecture for embedding adaptive oversight within multi-agent systems. While current safety methodologies assume a single model operating under direct human supervision—where undesired behaviors can be mitigated through prompt engineering, reinforcement learning from human feedback, or output moderation—these techniques remain pointwise. They govern how one model responds to one input but fail to address the collaborative dynamics of multiple interacting models. As large models are increasingly deployed in tool-using, agentic, and multi-agent systems, the single-agent safety paradigm begins to falter. Models that are individually well-aligned can collectively produce outcomes that no single instance was trained to prevent, due to feedback amplification, imitation, or emergent coordination. This creates a disconnect between local alignment and systemic safety, where ensuring compliance at the component level does not guarantee control over the network as a whole.
