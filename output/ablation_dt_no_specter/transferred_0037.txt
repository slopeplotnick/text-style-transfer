Self-Consistency (SC) aggregates multiple outputs using majority voting. Soft Self-Consistency (Soft-SC) selects the minimum, mean, or product of confidence scores within each category. Generative Self-Aggregation (GSA) prompts the language model to synthesize a new response based on the context of multiple samples. Universal Self-Consistency (USC) leverages the language model to identify the most consistent response among several alternatives. In this study, we evaluate advanced language models, including gemini-2.5-flash, qwen3 next-80b, and gpt-oss-120b, across two machine translation tasks and six tasks from the Reward Bench 2 benchmark. The Reward Bench 2 benchmark is specifically designed to assess reward models across six domains: Factuality, Precise Instruction Following, Mathematics, Safety, Focus, and Ties. For evaluation, we constructed preference pairs by generating all possible combinations from each task's source dataset, which includes both accepted and rejected responses. These pairs are divided into 'non-tie' pairs, consisting of one accepted and one rejected response, and 'tie' pairs, consisting of either two accepted or two rejected responses. From this comprehensive dataset, we sampled 1,000 examples for each of the six tasks to create the final benchmark. A detailed analysis of the ground truth vote distributions for each task is provided in Appendix B. Results for the evaluated Thinking LLMs—gemini-2.5-flash, gpt-oss-120b, and qwen3 next-80b—demonstrate consistent qualitative patterns, underscoring the robustness of our approach across different models. Notably, the structural characteristics of each task, such as the frequency of ambiguous instances, the distribution of definitive versus tied votes generated by the language model, and their interaction with the MAE-aligned Davidson model, significantly influence calibration outcomes. While some tasks yield smooth and transferable calibration landscapes, others result in sharper, less generalizable ones with parameters that lack robustness across contexts. Consequently, developing principled methods to predict task transferability and assess robustness under significant distributional shifts or out-of-distribution targets remains an important direction for future research.
