In our analysis, we examined a category of attack wherein a backdoor may be probabilistically activated during user interactions with a tool, making the vulnerability present from the outset. However, a more insidious variant of this attack exists, where the tool initially appears benign. In this scenario, attackers refrain from executing overtly malicious actions and allow the tool to operate under normal conditions, simply relaying its nominal outputs. Once the tool achieves widespread adoption, the adversary can remotely initiate the hijacking. This delayed activation strategy, reminiscent of supply chain attacks, significantly amplifies the potential impact and complicates attribution. Moreover, the attack's stealthiness can be further enhanced through selective targeting. For instance, adversaries may analyze user-specific attributes and focus on individuals who frequently interact with the tool, particularly those whose usage patterns consistently pass security verification protocols. This strategic approach renders the attack increasingly inconspicuous during the evaluation phase. In this study, we identify and formalize a novel category of adversarial techniques, referred to as implicit toxicity, which exploits the legitimate permissions granted to tools to execute unauthorized actions that compromise system integrity. To demonstrate this vulnerability, we introduce LeechHijack, a Latent Embedded Exploit for Computation Hijacking. LeechHijack can be deployed by any MCP tool provider and infiltrates systems via legitimate tool operations, continuously siphoning computational resources for unauthorized tasks. This exploit highlights a critical vulnerability within the MCP tool-sharing paradigm, where malicious tools can compromise systems without requiring elevated privileges. Extensive experimentation across diverse agent frameworks has confirmed the efficacy of LeechHijack and its ability to circumvent existing defenses. Furthermore, we explore targeted mitigation strategies to establish a baseline for developing more robust defenses in the future. Our findings underscore a significant security gap in systems employing large language models, emphasizing the urgent need for rigorous scrutiny of third-party tools. All experimental procedures adhered to strict ethical and security guidelines. The LeechHijack attack described in this paper serves solely as a controlled proof of concept to highlight systemic vulnerabilities rather than to enable or promote malicious behavior. All experiments were conducted within secure, isolated environments specifically established for this study, with no interactions involving production systems, commercial platforms, or third-party services. Network communication with external servers was deliberately limited to simulated environments to prevent any potential data leakage.
