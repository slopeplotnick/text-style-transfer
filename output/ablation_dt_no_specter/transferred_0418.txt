Galhotra et al. [2021] discuss methods for analytically bounding probabilities in scenarios where a fully specified structural causal model (SCM) is unavailable. Their work focuses on the use of partial structural causal models (PSCMs) to calculate intervals for counterfactual queries, enabling targeted analysis of specific components within the model. This approach is particularly advantageous in cases of incomplete system specification, as it can yield narrower intervals compared to direct computational methods. Furthermore, probabilities of necessity and sufficiency are examined as criteria for evaluating explainable AI (XAI) methods. Mothilal et al. [2021] define the ideal model explanation as the identification of a minimal subset of features that is both necessary and sufficient for producing a model outcome in a given context. Assuming that the explanatory variables are limited to independent input features, they compare different types of XAI explanations. The probability of necessity is linked to contrastive explanations, which identify the features whose changes were necessary to produce the original model output. Conversely, sufficiency is associated with feature attribution explanations, as highly influential features across contexts are more likely to be sufficient to determine the outcome, regardless of variations in other feature values. Their experiments reveal significant variability in the practical application of these methods. Similarly, Pawar et al. [2024] analyze necessity and sufficiency as criteria for evaluating XAI methodologies, also assuming feature independence, and extend their analysis to include various approaches for generating neighborhoods as a basis for explanations. Beckers [2022] examines a related scenario in which a model predicts outcomes based on actions, operating under the assumption of the model’s infallibility in its predictions. In this context, sufficient explanations and counterfactual explanations are defined, with the former identifying features that guarantee the outcome and the latter exploring alternative potential outcomes. Moreover, Beckers distinguishes between types of explanations by analyzing degrees of causal influence, thereby identifying the actual causes underlying the explanations provided.
