To address this critical challenge, the research community has developed two distinct paradigms: Fact Verification (FV) and Hallucination Detection (HD). Fact Verification is a well-established, text-centric research area rooted in domains such as information retrieval and fake news detection. It primarily evaluates the factuality of statements by leveraging external knowledge sources, such as web documents or databases. A key feature of FV is its origin-agnostic approach: it assesses the truthfulness of a statement regardless of whether it is authored by a human or generated by a machine. In this paradigm, the text is treated as an independent, static entity, with factuality determined by comparing the claim against retrieved evidence. In contrast, Hallucination Detection (HD) represents a more recent research direction that has emerged alongside the development of large language models (LLMs). The primary distinction between the two paradigms lies in their focus: while FV evaluates text in isolation, HD specifically targets content generated by LLMs and incorporates an introspective analysis of the generative process. Although HD can utilize external knowledge retrieval similar to FV, it predominantly relies on model-centric signals, such as token-level uncertainty, activation patterns, and consistency across sampled outputs. As a result, HD is inherently model-centric, enabling it to detect hallucinations even in the absence of external evidence. Despite their shared goal of identifying factual inaccuracies, these two paradigms have developed in relative isolation, as evidenced by their distinct benchmarks, evaluation metrics, and publication venues. This divergence stems from a fundamental difference in their evaluation methodologies: FV is intrinsically text-centric, with benchmarks like FEVER treating claims as standalone textual entities.
