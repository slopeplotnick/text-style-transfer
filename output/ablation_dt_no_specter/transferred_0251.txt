When constructing a narrative, individuals can begin by focusing on a specific event and then shaping the traits and development of the entities associated with that event. Alternatively, they may take an entity as the focal point and build a series of interconnected events around it. E2, a PhD student specializing in literature, suggests that narratives can be systematically broken down into fundamental units: story outlines, sequences, propositions, and parts of speech. By progressively combining these structural units in reverse order, one can ultimately create a coherent story. For instance, parts of speech—such as a person, an action, or an attribute—can form a proposition. Several propositions can then be organized into a sequence, and two or more sequences can collectively constitute a complete narrative. However, the mere aggregation of these units is insufficient to produce a coherent story. The elements that bind these units together, referred to as relationships, are essential for ensuring the cohesion and functionality of the narrative. Semi-structured interviews with two domain experts identified four foundational constructs integral to narratives: entities, events, relationships, and outlines. During discussions with the experts, the attributes of each foundational construct were examined, revealing that each can encompass multiple interpretations. Events, as the primary components of a narrative, may have attributes that are not always explicitly apparent within the narrative structure. While events can exist independently within a story, their interactional dynamics are crucial for constructing narrative tension and meaning. Moreover, the identity of entities and the nature of relationships between them significantly influence the unfolding of events. Nevertheless, the specific attributes of an entity’s identity and the content of a relationship can also be independently categorized within their respective constructs.
