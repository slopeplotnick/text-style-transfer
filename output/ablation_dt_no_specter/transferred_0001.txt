Certain models may be more reliable than others. This review addresses the influence of model size, structure, and training data on the occurrence of hallucinations. A recent study illustrates these tendencies across different models and offers solutions such as improving training techniques and imposing limitations on the generated output to address the issue. Similarly, another study highlights that hallucination remains a pervasive problem in large language models (LLMs), regardless of their size or training methodology. While it is suggested that careful model selection and training strategies can help mitigate hallucinations, the authors argue that it is unlikely to completely eliminate them. This insight is crucial for setting realistic goals and assessing the suitability of a model for specific tasks. When selecting an LLM to reduce hallucinations, it is essential to consider factors such as model architecture, the quality and diversity of the training data, and strategies like fine-tuning or retrieval augmentation. Several models are currently available, including GPT, BERT, and T5. Among these, GPT is often noted for generating text that is logically coherent and cohesive; however, it is also prone to producing fabricated information, commonly referred to as hallucinations. In contrast, BERT demonstrates a comparatively lower tendency to generate hallucinations due to its use of bidirectional training for specific tasks, though it can still fabricate information under certain conditions. Additionally, alternative models such as PaLM and LLaMa have been developed with the aim of reducing hallucination rates. However, their effectiveness varies depending on the methodologies employed in their training and architectural design. A thorough understanding of the strengths and limitations of these models is essential when selecting an appropriate tool for applications where accuracy is critical and minimizing hallucination rates is a priority.
