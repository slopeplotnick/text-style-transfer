Because concepts are not explicitly defined within data features, probing techniques are commonly employed to identify neurons, or combinations of neurons, that best represent the target concepts. These probes are typically linear; however, they can also be non-linear when the concept is not linearly separable. Moreover, probes can be trained using either supervised or unsupervised methods. The supervised approach requires labeled data corresponding to the human-specified concept, whereas the unsupervised approach discovers concepts that are significant to the model but may not be interpretable to humans. This study extends the modeling of structural equations beyond observation-based estimation under general assumptions. It considers various levels of modeling, ranging from FSCMs, which are designed to be interpretable through domain-specific knowledge, to PSCMs, which include all FSCMs consistent with the empirical data. This highlights the critical role of modeling choices in defining structural functions and emphasizes the value of partial information, such as counterfactual intervals, when complete FSCMs are unavailable. Galhotra et al. [2021] propose an XAI method that utilizes FSCMs to calculate probabilities of sufficiency and necessity. Their approach is particularly suited to tabular data, where causal modeling is applied to data features, assuming these features represent high-level concepts. Additionally, their framework allows for the inclusion of supplementary concepts not present in the model input. While the method relies on features being meaningful representations of concepts, it does not require a causal generator. Furthermore, it assumes a preference for explaining the model's output, framing explanations in terms of which subsets of input values are sufficient to shift a prediction to a more favorable outcome or which values are necessary to preserve a preferable prediction when the desired outcome remains unchanged.
