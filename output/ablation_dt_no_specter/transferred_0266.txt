Advanced reinforcement learning techniques, such as domain randomization and reward shaping, have been utilized to address simulation-to-reality discrepancies. Tobin et al. (2017) demonstrated that domain randomization enables successful sim-to-real transfer for robotic tasks, while Grzes and Kudenko (2010) developed reward shaping strategies to improve learning efficiency in multi-agent scenarios. These approaches enhance the robustness of construction robotics to environmental variability. However, they require significant computational resources and meticulous hyperparameter tuning, which become impractical for real-time coordination in dynamic construction environments where conditions frequently change. Additionally, the interpretability of learned policies remains a challenge, making it difficult to validate decision-making processes. To address these limitations, game-theoretic approaches have been explored as an alternative. By providing sophisticated mathematical frameworks for modeling strategic interactions among autonomous agents, game theory offers valuable tools for multi-robot coordination. For instance, Martin et al. (2023) employed Shapley values within a game-theoretic framework to develop a multi-robot task allocation strategy, achieving superior performance and enabling formal analysis of equilibrium solutions and stability properties. Nevertheless, game-theoretic approaches continue to face challenges in interpretability and rely on assumptions of rational agent behavior, which may not hold in unpredictable construction environments. Furthermore, these methods require precise utility function specifications, which are inherently difficult to define for complex construction tasks. Despite these advancements, learning-based and AI-driven methodologies remain limited in construction coordination scenarios that demand explainable decision-making. The opaque nature of deep learning and game-theoretic models hinders the transparency of coordination processes, which is critical for construction managers to ensure safety oversight and effective project management. Consequently, these limitations have spurred interest in Large Language Models (LLMs) as a novel approach to coordination. LLMs possess the ability to articulate reasoning in natural language, thereby enhancing the transparency of decision-making processes. Their integration into multi-robot systems represents a transformative paradigm, enabling natural language understanding, contextual reasoning, and adaptive decision-making in response to novel information. Unlike traditional optimization methods that require exhaustive problem specification, LLM-based approaches facilitate intuitive human-robot interaction and address the explainability challenges of learning-based methods by providing transparent, natural language reasoning.
