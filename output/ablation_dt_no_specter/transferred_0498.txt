The perceived anonymity afforded by online environments tends to diminish social restraint and exacerbate expressions of anger or frustration. Moreover, the dynamics of group identity can further intensify and polarize interactions. Although computational techniques have advanced in identifying and flagging inappropriate behavior, they predominantly operate in a reactive manner, focusing on content removal or punitive measures rather than addressing the root causes. Consequently, while online platforms may temporarily suppress harmful discourse, they often fail to address the underlying social dynamics, allowing such hostility to reemerge. Our study explores an alternative approach by employing mediation rather than mere moderation. Instead of solely identifying negative content, we investigate the potential of language models to engage in conflict resolution by monitoring disputes and facilitating de-escalation. We posit that these models could contribute by comprehending dialogues, assessing the fairness of arguments, and generating responses that promote a more constructive and balanced discourse. Achieving this requires an analysis of contextual dynamics, emotional states, escalation trajectories, and established conversational conventions. To address this, the task is bifurcated into two interrelated components: judgment and steering. The first component entails the identification and evaluation of unfair assertions, emotional provocations, and escalation points. The second component involves facilitating de-escalation and encouraging participants to address underlying issues rather than engaging in conflict. To evaluate the efficacy of these models, we compiled an extensive dataset of real-world online disputes sourced from Reddit and developed a systematic framework for assessment. This evaluation focuses on three key dimensions: first, the model's adherence to established conversational norms; second, the behavioral responses of participants following the model's intervention to determine its effectiveness in mitigating conflict; and third, a comparative analysis of the model's interventions against human-generated responses. Extensive testing indicates that premium models outperform free counterparts in both conflict resolution and topic adherence. Furthermore, these models effectively mitigate negative discourse and emotional intensity in simulated conflicts. While they exhibit neutrality and clarity, they lack the readability and engagement characteristic of human interactions.
