Psychometric scales and questionnaires are essential tools in quantitative research across various fields, including psychology, medicine, social sciences, education, and human-computer interaction. When a novel construct or theoretical framework is introduced and subjected to empirical investigation, developing corresponding psychometric instruments becomes necessary. However, this process is resource-intensive, requiring meticulous planning, time, materials, and substantial financial investment (Stefana et al., 2025). In this article, we explore the potential of facilitating the scale development process by using computer simulations to construct psychometric scales with data generated by Large Language Models (LLMs). We first outline the rationale behind this approach and describe four empirical studies designed to assess its efficacy. Finally, we discuss our findings and offer recommendations for future research. The recruitment of a representative sample can be both costly and time-consuming, often leading researchers to rely on convenience samples, such as university student volunteers, which significantly limits the generalizability of findings (Hanel & Vione, 2016; Wild et al., 2022). Recently, researchers have increasingly turned to online participant recruitment platforms, such as Prolific.co and Amazon MTurk, to address these challenges (Buhrmester et al., 2018; Douglas et al., 2023; Huang et al., 2015). These platforms have substantially reduced the financial and temporal costs associated with participant recruitment and, in some cases, have facilitated the collection of more representative samples for specific populations, such as those in the United Kingdom and the United States. However, ethical considerations arise due to the potential inclusion of participants from disadvantaged backgrounds (Huang et al., 2015; Huff & Tingley, 2015; Moss et al., 2023). Furthermore, when researchers investigate novel constructs without access to validated measurement scales, or when validation is impractical, they often develop bespoke questionnaires grounded solely in theoretical frameworks. While this approach economizes both time and resources, it often compromises the external validity of the findings, resulting in limited applicability to broader populations beyond the study sample (Lee et al., 2020).
