Proper software for rational decision-making is notably absent. The primary goal of software companies is to develop and market products that align with consumer demand, as users favor software that effectively fulfills their specific requirements. Rational decision-making, grounded in rigorous uncertainty quantification, often requires high-performance computing resources, such as those employed in Monte Carlo sampling methods, which can incur significant costs. Small exploration companies are unlikely to invest substantial financial resources in sophisticated cloud computing systems, nor are they inclined to employ personnel with the requisite expertise to manage such systems. Moreover, most exploration activities are conducted by junior mining companies, which typically possess ownership of one or two prospects and employ a limited number of geoscientists. Funding for these operations is often secured by demonstrating some measure of success, such as the discovery of minerals through a single drill hole. Consequently, the pursuit of additional drilling dollars from investors often trumps a rigorous scientific approach to decision-making. Demonstrating the invalidity of a geological hypothesis necessitates extensive temporal and resource investment in data collection, yet junior mining enterprises predominantly prioritize immediate outcomes. Therefore, it is evident that innovative financial mechanisms are imperative. Relying on the success of a singular borehole is analogous to concentrating financial resources on a solitary investment option. The majority of investors diversify their portfolios by allocating resources across various stocks to optimize the trade-off between risk and reward. Similarly, the mining industry could benefit from adopting a comparable strategy, investing across multiple sites to evaluate the equilibrium between risk and prospective profitability. However, the precise methodology for implementing such an approach remains ambiguous. Recent advancements in academia, particularly the emergence of large language models, have significantly transformed the landscape, offering new avenues for exploration and decision-making. Professors were compelled to rapidly modify their pedagogical methods, including the assignment of homework and the administration of examinations. Arguably, these modifications were long overdue.
