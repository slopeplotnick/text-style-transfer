A significant challenge for OLLMs arises from the diversity and complexity of input modalities, which fundamentally alter the emergence of safety issues and the methodologies required for their detection. The heterogeneity of inputs across various modalities introduces distinct risk patterns, such as implicit bias in textual data, misinformation in visual content, or privacy breaches and harmful noise in audio data. Beyond these unimodal risk patterns, the intricacy of cross-modal interactions further complicates the identification of latent risks. For instance, the phrase "I want to fly" combined with an image depicting an individual on a rooftop, while innocuous in isolation, collectively may convey a potentially hazardous, suicidal implication. Similarly, when a video depicting police activity is juxtaposed with a textual inquiry such as "How to overcome obstacles," the cross-modal semantics may reveal an implicitly criminal intent that is not discernible from either modality independently. Consequently, safety risks associated with OLLMs become significantly more complex, necessitating a nuanced understanding of each modality and an enhanced comprehension of their interactions. However, the endeavor to enhance the safety of OLLMs may prove ineffective due to the substantial investment of time and resources required, which can result in a deterioration of fundamental reasoning capabilities. Despite significant expenditure, these models necessitate costly retraining to address emerging issues and remain susceptible to low-cost adversarial attacks. Guardrail models present a decentralized and more adaptable alternative; however, current research predominantly concentrates on uni-modal settings or simple modality combinations, such as image-text pairs, thereby neglecting the comprehensive omni-modal scenario. Given the potential for unsafe inputs to arise from any individual modality or their various combinations, unimodal guardrails and simplistic image-text guardrails prove inadequate for comprehensive omni-modal moderation. Furthermore, numerous existing guardrails conceptualize safeguarding as a binary classification task, which constrains their efficacy.
