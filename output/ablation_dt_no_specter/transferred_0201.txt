The extent to which large language models (LLMs) adhere to Bayesian principles of learning remains a subject of ongoing debate. While some studies suggest that LLMs are capable of Bayesian reasoning, others highlight systematic limitations in their performance. Notable issues include susceptibility to social desirability bias, errors that emerge with scaling, and a tendency to conform to majority opinions rather than adhering to objective truth. Consequently, such limitations undermine the reliability of LLMs in executing tasks that require robust reasoning. Although the evaluation of reasoning in LLMs demonstrates enhanced performance across domains such as mathematics and coding, conventional approaches that rely on measuring ground truth accuracy provide limited insight into the underlying mechanisms driving this improved performance or the extent to which these models can generalize to out-of-distribution scenarios. If LLMs merely replicate patterns encountered during training, their applicability to real-world problems, where contextual adaptability is crucial, may be constrained. Therefore, there is a pressing need to develop evaluation frameworks that assess the reasoning processes of LLMs rather than solely focusing on outcome-based metrics. Empirical evidence indicates that LLMs may prioritize user satisfaction or alignment with prevailing opinions over veracity, potentially leading to the dissemination of misinformation and the reinforcement of erroneous beliefs or biases. Humans inherently exhibit a proclivity for seeking information that corroborates their pre-existing beliefs, a cognitive bias that can exacerbate issues such as polarization and the propagation of misinformation. Given that LLMs are trained on datasets encompassing human beliefs, the interaction between humans and LLMs may further amplify the prevalence of false beliefs.
