In 2024, content is systematically divided into coherent functional and semantic sections, summarized in multiple languages, and made available for interactive question-and-answer sessions. To support these tasks, the transcribed multimodal speech-to-text output serves as the primary context. While modern large language models (LLMs) are capable of handling extensive contexts, their performance is constrained by context window limitations. Therefore, we adopt a strategic approach to ensure efficiency and clarity. Lectures are first translated into multiple languages and then divided into chapters, which not only prevents context-window overflow but also enhances conceptual coherence by limiting each chapter to a single topic. For each chapter, we produce several compressed representations, including transcript compressions at varying levels—50 percent, 70 percent, and 90 percent—as well as length-controlled summaries tailored to the size of the original section. To leverage the superior performance of LLMs with English text, all summaries are initially generated in English and subsequently translated into the target languages. A similar methodology is applied to the question-and-answer process: English transcripts, segmented into chapters, are used with Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) to query an LLM, and the resulting answers are then translated into the target languages. Another significant challenge in making lectures accessible lies in translating slides that contain both editable text and images with embedded text. For editable text, we utilize a Python-based PowerPoint parser to extract text blocks, which are translated using standard unimodal machine translation methods, avoiding the computational costs of multimodal models. However, translating text embedded in images is more complex, as it often lacks contextual information and depends on visual elements for interpretation, necessitating a multimodal translation approach. After translation, it is crucial to reintegrate the text into the original image to preserve its layout and visual semantics. To address this, we propose an image-translation pipeline that systematically detects, recognizes, translates, and re-renders text within slide images.
