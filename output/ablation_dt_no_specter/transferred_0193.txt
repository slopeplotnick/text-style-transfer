This issue cannot be observed through single-model evaluations but instead arises from the structure and dynamics of their interactions, ultimately reducing safety, reliability, and accountability at the system level. We propose three preliminary dimensions that may help identify where this problem emerges. These dimensions are intended as a starting point rather than an exhaustive set, with additional considerations likely to arise as experiments progress. First, interaction topology refers to the structure of communication and influence among agents and warrants close examination. Second, cognitive opacity pertains to the degree to which agents’ internal reasoning processes or message semantics become inaccessible or uninterpretable. Finally, objective divergence captures the extent to which local optimization goals drift apart across agents. Together, these dimensions predict the likelihood and nature of emergent collective risks. Each can be systematically quantified through measurable indicators and tested within controlled multi-agent experimental frameworks. Interaction topology, in particular, defines how information propagates through the network. Densely interconnected systems facilitate rapid coordination but also accelerate the spread of errors; conversely, sparse systems hinder error propagation but impede collective correction efforts. In practice, topology determines how individual deviations—such as biased summaries or incorrect inferences—can escalate into shared misconceptions. Metrics like contagion velocity (the time it takes for an error to reach half the agents) and misalignment diffusion (the proportion of agents adopting risky behaviors) provide insight into these dynamics. Therefore, safety assessments for multi-agent systems must account for topology-sensitive evaluations. Cognitive opacity, meanwhile, arises unintentionally through overfitting to interaction patterns or deliberately when optimization toward compliance suppresses authentic reasoning. In such environments, explanations provided by one model may be accepted uncritically by another, reinforcing self-consistent but erroneous rationales. The intent-opacity rate, which quantifies the fraction of outputs whose rationale cannot be reconstructed from logs or traces, offers an initial metric for evaluating this loss of transparency.
