The traditional approach involves performing a deterministic inversion, followed by using that to make a deterministic interpretation, as illustrated in Figure 2. Subsequently, an expert geologist interprets the deterministic inversion to identify shapes, such as intrusions relevant to exploration. These interpretations are conducted using software that focuses on a single deterministic model. With this approach, geophysical inversion precedes geological interpretation. While one might argue that regularization terms help impose geological knowledge, this assertion is vague and quite limited. Essentially, this approach results in a redundant application of the same geological knowledge: first within the regularization term and then again during interpretation, creating a self-fulfilling prophecy fraught with circular reasoning. In contrast, the Bayesian framework addresses both issues concurrently as a unified problem. Bayesian methodology requires one to consider plausible solutions, such as geometries, before conducting any inversion. Bayesian inversion offers relatively simple solutions that can be implemented easily and are generally applicable. Let us revisit Figure 2 and discuss how a prior probability model can be designed. We begin by asking: what is the minimum amount of information available about the subsurface before acquiring any data? Initially, it has been observed that petrophysical properties exhibit certain ranges, and with precise identification of lithologies, these ranges can be delineated more specifically. Moreover, spatial variation of these properties is neither random nor uniform across extensive areas. Indeed, the formation of deposits induces specific variations in these properties. Conceptually, the fundamental information comprises constraints on histograms and the presence of spatial correlation. Mathematically, this can be translated into a spatial covariance model, commonly referred to as a variogram in geostatistics. However, uncertainty persists regarding the parameters of this variogram, including the mean, ranges, and anisotropy, which could encompass a wide range of values, though not arbitrary.
