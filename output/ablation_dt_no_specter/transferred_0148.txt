We propose a novel approach to delineate perception and cognition in artificial intelligence models by introducing the "see-before-thinking" rationale structure, which explicitly separates perception and reasoning components within the generated rationale. By enhancing training data with negative rationales, the model learns to distinguish between correct and incorrect reasoning paths. Moreover, we present a self-training framework, termed STL, that leverages structured rationales to jointly improve both perception and reasoning, contrasting prior approaches that emphasize reasoning alone. Our methodology was evaluated across domains such as commonsense, language, and science, demonstrating enhanced performance while avoiding the limitations of human or proprietary supervision. In the realm of vision-language reasoning, reasoning has been shown to play a critical role in enhancing the performance of Vision-Language Models (VLMs). Although recent VLMs achieve strong results on general benchmarks, effectively incorporating visual information into the reasoning process remains a persistent challenge, particularly for open-source models. One direction has explored prompt-based strategies that assign functional roles to MLLMs via system prompts, enabling modular step-by-step reasoning. For instance, Cantor structures reasoning into context analysis followed by high-level feature generation, while CCoT leverages scene graphs to capture object–attribute relations and guide a two-stage process of graph construction and reasoning. Another direction relies on learning-based approaches that fine-tune VLMs on datasets containing multimodal reasoning chains. However, such datasets are typically curated using powerful teacher models or through costly human annotations, raising concerns about scalability. In contrast, our work avoids this reliance by enabling VLMs to enhance their reasoning abilities through self-learning, without requiring curated rationales. Self-training is a semi-supervised paradigm where a model improves by generating supervision from its own outputs. In Large Language Models (LLMs), this approach has been widely used to strengthen reasoning: models generate intermediate rationales for unlabeled data, which are then reused as training signals in subsequent iterations. This iterative process enhances reasoning while reducing dependence on human annotations. Seminal works have advanced LLM reasoning by sampling rationales, filtering them for correctness, and fine-tuning on positive samples. However, self-training for VLMs remains relatively underexplored.
