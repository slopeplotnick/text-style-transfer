The phenomenon of adversarial training enhancing the transferability of attacks is not rooted in specific architectural designs but rather in the fundamental way it reshapes information processing within models. This effect is observed consistently across diverse architectures, highlighting its significant implications for security. To better understand how adversarial training promotes transferability, researchers have utilized integrated gradients to analyze how models justify their predictions. For example, a comparison between two DenseNet121 models—one trained adversarially (AT) and the other under standard conditions (ST)—revealed striking differences in feature attribution. The AT model focused on high-level, semantically meaningful regions, such as the head, body, and legs of an ostrich, while the ST model concentrated on fine-grained textural details and background elements. This contrast demonstrates the ability of AT models to prioritize generalized, transferable features over localized, less transferable ones. Consequently, adversarial examples generated by AT models exhibit greater cross-model effectiveness, as they exploit features commonly shared across architectures. For instance, DenseNet121 and DenseNet201 share similar image processing mechanisms, while MobileNetV3 demonstrates comparable behavior due to its design principles derived from EfficientNet.
