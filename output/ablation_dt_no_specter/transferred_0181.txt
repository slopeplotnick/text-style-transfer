Demonstrate the feasibility of LLMs for addressing complex optimization problems. Assess the computational efficiency of LLM-driven resource allocation using dynamic prompting, and validate LLM performance against algorithmic solutions such as DQN and Q learning. The proposed framework systematically progresses from task decomposition to balanced allocation optimization. Initially, long-horizon construction tasks are decomposed into manageable subtasks using object-centric and skill-centric strategies adapted from the SMART-LLM framework (Kannan et al. 2024). The object-centric decomposition identifies task components based on physical objects and their required manipulations, while the skill-centric decomposition organizes subtasks according to robot capability requirements, such as precision handling or force application. These decomposed subtasks are then assigned to heterogeneous robots through capability-aware matching, considering robot specializations and task requirements. The LTAA framework employs LangGraph orchestration with probabilistic success modeling to maximize task completion likelihood by computing robot-specific success rates for each subtask based on feature-capability alignment. However, initial testing revealed significant workload imbalance across robot teams, as pure success-probability maximization led to overutilization of high-capability robots and underutilization of specialized units. This observation motivated the development of phase-adaptive allocation strategies that dynamically adjust decision priorities throughout project progression, transitioning from success-rate emphasis in early phases to workload equity prioritization in later phases, thereby balancing overall mission success with fair resource utilization. The methodology development proceeded through three stages. First, the implementation of the SMART-LLM framework assessed LLM feasibility for MRTA but revealed validation inconsistencies and output reliability issues. Second, the Self Corrective-Agent Architecture addressed these challenges through multi-stage validation and hierarchical retry mechanisms. Finally, the LTAA framework integrated these validation principles with phase-adaptive allocation strategies to enable systematic benchmarking against traditional optimization methods. The authors describe the process of implementing a classic LLM-based MRTA framework, SMART-LLM (Kannan et al. 2024), and the fundamental challenges encountered during implementation. These challenges, including validation inconsistencies, LLM output reliability issues, and coordination limitations, motivated the development of a phase-adaptive and self-corrective Self Corrective-Agent LangGraph Architecture, using multi-stage validation and hierarchical retry mechanisms to address the identified deficiencies. These contributions culminated in the proposed LTAA framework. Moreover, this study introduces a systematic performance benchmarking against traditional optimization methods. The LTAA framework evaluation employs the TEACH dataset as the standard benchmark for direct comparison with Q-learning, DQN, and DP baselines under identical task scenarios. Evaluation criteria include workload balance distribution, feature-specific performance across task types, and reasoning quality assessment to validate both allocation and performance metrics.
