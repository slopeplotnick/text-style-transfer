The purpose of this paper is to provide a forward-looking discussion on the application of artificial intelligence in critical mineral exploration. This perspective is personal, subjective, and informed by three decades of experience in the use of data science, decision science, and artificial intelligence in the context of Earth resources exploration and development. In 1998, I authored a paper (Caers and Journel, 1998) on the use of neural networks trained on outcrop data to enhance geological realism in reservoir models. As a junior professor eager to make a significant impact, I presented this work at the Society of Petroleum Engineers Annual Technical Conference and Exhibition in New Orleans, even as Hurricane George approached Louisiana. The incorporation of geological realism is essential for accurately predicting fluid flow in porous media, which, in turn, leads to better decision-making in the exploration and development of oil and gas fields. In a subsequent publication (Caers and Ma, 2002), I demonstrated that neural networks can learn relationships between borehole and geophysical data, enabling improved predictions of reservoir properties, such as porosity, even in areas without boreholes. A critical contribution of this research was addressing the challenge of reconciling the differing scales of data, such as those from borehole cores versus seismic signals at significant subsurface depths. This issue of scale will be explored in detail in this paper. While the oil and gas industry has long embraced geological uncertainty assessments as a prerequisite for constructing offshore platforms, the mining industry continues to rely heavily on simplistic, deterministic models. In mineral exploration, the use of basic modeling and interpretation of multi-physics data remains widespread, reflecting a notable lag in adopting the quantitative risk assessment methods that have advanced significantly in the oil and gas sector.
