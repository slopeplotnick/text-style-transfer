Software Product Lines (SPLs) are now a well-established method for efficiently developing highly configurable software systems. Conceptually, an SPL can be seen as a software supply chain composed of multiple interconnected components. Variability models delineate the available product features and define the permissible combinations of these features within specific product configurations. An asset base provides the concrete software components that implement these features. Tools within the SPL facilitate the selection of features to create a specific configuration and subsequently generate the actual product by assembling components from the asset base. This approach becomes particularly advantageous when a substantial number of configurations are required. However, planning for the introduction of a new product type presents significant challenges for companies. Therefore, many organizations initially develop a family of similar software systems before committing to the transition of their development processes to a product line framework. Despite documented successful cases in the literature, transforming an existing software family into a cohesive SPL platform remains a formidable task, and organizations may exhibit reluctance in adopting this approach. Concerns frequently emerge due to the absence of standardized procedures to guide the process, coupled with uncertainties regarding the cost-benefit analysis. Consequently, despite possessing extensive repositories of well-documented code, companies continue to manually construct customized applications from the foundational code to satisfy client-specific requirements, predominantly employing a strategy known as clone-and-own. Our collaborator, ITK - Predict and Decide, is a small to medium-sized enterprise comprising 120 personnel at the time of this writing, including 19 agronomists and 29 developers, among whom are 2 UI/UX designers and 4 system administrators. Approximately one-third of the company's workforce comprises tech-oriented collaborators. The organization employs an agile methodology in the development of its systems, characterized by an efficient development and delivery process. The applications developed by the company facilitate decision-making for farmers by enabling the prediction of diseases and crop yields for both plants and livestock. The primary components of these applications consist of simulators developed by agronomists. Subsequently, the IT teams implement additional code to facilitate the acquisition of data from IoT devices or online services, which is then utilized to populate the simulators and visualize their outputs through dashboards. The software's back-end architecture is constructed using Kotlin, while the front-end is implemented with Angular, with extensive reliance on Typescript.
