To ascertain the interoperability of diverse systems, we are establishing experimental frameworks wherein artificial intelligence can enforce, interpret, and modify shared protocols. This approach aims to evaluate whether these methodologies effectively mitigate the issues previously identified. The objective of this work is to outline a research trajectory for AI safety that integrates theoretical foundations, empirical validation, and eventual standardization. Consequently, this paper serves as a conceptual framework, with subsequent studies intended to provide formal proofs and experimental evidence to substantiate these ideas. However, as previously discussed, prioritizing the safety of individual AI systems—such as through prompt engineering or output filtering—proves insufficient in scenarios where multiple AI agents interact and influence one another. As artificial intelligence systems increasingly engage in collaborative interactions, adherence to localized operational constraints proves inadequate for mitigating large-scale failures. Therefore, the distinction between ensuring the safety of individual models and safeguarding the integrity of interconnected systems necessitates a broader conceptual framework. To address this, we introduce the Emergent Systemic Risk Horizon (ESRH), a theoretical construct designed to delineate the juncture at which system-level vulnerabilities emerge and traditional assumptions of isolated safety measures become inadequate. The analysis demonstrates that risks can escalate from minor issues to extensive systemic challenges as interactions, complexity, and divergent objectives converge. These risks do not solely arise from the malfunction of an individual AI but are inherent to the collaborative dynamics of networked systems. By enabling agents to adapt rules and collaborate, the objective is to maintain stability in collective behavior without sole reliance on centralized control mechanisms. This marks merely the inception of our inquiry. The ESRH framework, alongside the Institutional AI concept, necessitates comprehensive empirical testing, theoretical development, and refinement. Subsequent endeavors will involve conducting controlled experiments to evaluate the defined indicators, examining coordination models, and devising practical methodologies for the application of these concepts within real-world AI systems. The transition from single-agent to multi-agent AI systems is currently underway. In the absence of innovative frameworks and methodologies for their governance, latent safety challenges may remain obscured until they escalate beyond manageable proportions. Ultimately, this paper aims to elucidate these challenges and propose a systematic approach to address them effectively.
