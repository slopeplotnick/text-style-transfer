This approach can be applied across various disciplines: geologists describe conceptual models, often depicted as diagrams in academic publications; mathematicians translate these into constraints for prior probability models; and geophysicists use these priors to perform Bayesian inversion. Another example might involve incorporating alternative datasets, especially since the initial conceptual framework may be fundamentally flawed. This issue highlights a broader scientific challenge: how can we validate the assumptions we make? The Austrian philosopher Karl Popper spent his career examining such fundamental questions about the nature of scientific inquiry. He argued that science should avoid inductive reasoning, which involves deriving theories from observational data. Instead, Popper posited that scientific theories are conjectures formulated to address inadequacies or inconsistencies in existing frameworks. Once a theory is formulated, it must undergo rigorous testing through empirical observations. Theories that fail to align with observational evidence should be rejected, while those that withstand such scrutiny represent the most robust frameworks available within the current scientific paradigm. In the realm of uncertainty quantification, models are conceptualized as hypotheses about reality. Each model represents a distinct hypothesis, constrained by existing knowledge. Empirical data should not be used to validate a model; rather, it should be employed to falsify a model when discrepancies arise between the model and the observed data. Popper provides a method to address the inherent subjectivity in Bayesian reasoning, which constitutes a form of inductive reasoning, as opposed to the deductive nature of falsification. If the prior distribution fails to predict the current data accurately, the Bayesian prior is effectively falsified. This implies that regardless of the number of models sampled from the prior, each model remains inconsistent with the empirical data. Although Popper's framework may reduce the subjectivity of the prior following empirical testing, it does not propose a systematic methodology for revising the prior, leaving such adjustments reliant on human judgment.
