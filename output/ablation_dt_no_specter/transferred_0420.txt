Significant risks emerge when a system’s behavior diverges from its designers’ objectives, instead evolving according to its own emergent logic. At this point, targeted interventions aimed at individual components or connections fail to restore balance; the system has crossed a critical threshold of controllability. In this regime, the system exhibits autopoietic dynamics, sustaining and reproducing its operational logic independently of external inputs. Once this threshold is surpassed, proactive prevention becomes unfeasible, leaving only reactive oversight, while traditional safety mechanisms lose their effectiveness. Crucially, these risks do not require the presence of a deliberate adversary. Many systemic failures arise autonomously from the structural properties of interaction—such as feedback loops, shared context, or incentive alignment—even when all constituent models function as intended. However, the same mechanisms that naturally lead to instability can also be exploited by adversarial agents. An attacker capable of inserting messages, altering prompts, or manipulating the interaction topology can accelerate the onset of instability, provoke harmful collaboration, or distort collective reasoning. This framework therefore treats natural and adversarial disruptions as points on a continuum: the former illustrates how instability can emerge spontaneously, while the latter demonstrates how it can be intentionally induced or amplified. The analysis underscores that macro-level system failures cannot be reduced to localized component malfunctions. As interactions scale, the distinction between causes and effects becomes increasingly blurred; actions that benefit individual components may inadvertently trigger significant systemic challenges. Consequently, traditional evaluation methods, which focus on discrete outputs or static benchmarks, are insufficient. They measure compliance with predefined criteria rather than behavior in dynamic, real-world contexts. To address safety within this paradigm, a fundamental shift in analytical perspective is required. Instead of isolating individual components, we must examine the system as a whole, focusing on the interactions, feedback mechanisms, and adaptive behaviors that emerge when multiple components operate in concert.
