Online videos have become a dominant communication medium, and their widespread availability has enabled the rapid dissemination of hateful content. Such content not only exacerbates discrimination and societal division but also has the potential to incite real-world violence. Current approaches predominantly rely on extracting and integrating features from video frames, audio signals, and transcribed text. However, these methods often fail to achieve effective multimodal semantic interaction. As illustrated in Figure 1, hateful video detection faces two primary challenges. First, there is the challenge of understanding nuanced context. Hateful videos often convey harmful intent through subtle contextual cues that span temporal and multimodal dimensions, such as the alignment of specific visuals with verbal expressions or the implicit associations between visual and auditory elements. Second, the fusion of multimodal semantic relations remains a significant obstacle. While existing methods attempt to integrate features from multiple modalities, they frequently fall short in enabling robust semantic interaction across these modalities. Addressing both challenges requires advanced contextual reasoning capabilities, which conventional approaches struggle to provide. Recent efforts have sought to improve multimodal understanding by incorporating reasoning frameworks into visual-language models (VLMs). These methods, as shown in Figure 1, often include direct reasoning or Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning as additional steps. However, direct reasoning tends to produce superficial descriptions that fail to capture the nuanced semantic associations indicative of hateful content. Moreover, CoT reasoning has been shown to generate explanations that may misrepresent the underlying decision-making processes of the model, raising concerns about its reliability in sensitive detection tasks. Beyond reasoning quality, recent studies of hateful video datasets have revealed that hate cues exhibit diverse temporal patterns, appearing either as brief, isolated segments or as dispersed occurrences throughout the video timeline. Furthermore, inference signals often carry complex meanings that require integration with foundational multimodal features. These challenges highlight a critical gap: existing systems lack both reliable semantic reasoning and effective mechanisms for fusing diverse information modalities, which are essential for the accurate detection of hateful videos.
