Large language models (LLMs) demonstrate proficiency in explicit reasoning; however, their implicit computational strategies remain largely unexplored. Decades of research in psychophysics have established that humans intuitively process and integrate noisy signals using near-optimal Bayesian strategies in perceptual tasks. This study investigates whether LLMs exhibit similar behavior and can perform optimal multimodal integration without explicit training or instruction. By adopting methodologies derived from psychology, we infer the cognitive processes of these models through systematic behavioral analysis. We introduce BayesBench, a behavioral benchmark comprising four tasks designed to evaluate dimensions such as magnitude and temporal estimation through linguistic and visual stimuli, grounded in established psychological research. This framework enables the comparison of nine models against human judgments to establish a benchmark. By systematically varying parameters such as noise levels, contextual information, and instructional directives, we assess the efficacy of these models and their capacity to integrate diverse informational cues. Beyond evaluating accuracy and efficiency, we propose a metric termed the Bayesian Consistency Score, which assesses the extent to which models exhibit Bayes-consistent behavioral adaptations, even when accuracy has reached a plateau. Our findings indicate that while certain models demonstrate the capacity for Bayes-consistent adaptation, accuracy alone does not necessarily equate to robustness. For instance, GPT-5 Mini achieves exemplary performance in textual tasks but exhibits inefficiencies in integrating visual information. This reveals a critical dissociation between capability and strategy, suggesting that accuracy-focused evaluations may overlook essential aspects of uncertainty management. The results elucidate emergent strategies for principled uncertainty handling and reveal a strong association between predictive accuracy and Bayesian reasoning tendencies. To advance the development of multimodal architectures, we introduce a novel psychophysics benchmark and a consistency metric, which serve both as evaluation tools and as resources to guide future system design. The evaluation of magnitudes, such as size, duration, or distance, constitutes a fundamental capability for both humans and machines. Humans achieve this by adeptly integrating noisy sensory inputs, assigning greater weight to more reliable signals and incorporating prior expectations to mitigate errors. This cognitive strategy emerges naturally across diverse cultural and developmental contexts, indicating it as a fundamental mechanism for processing information under conditions of uncertainty.
