Language comprehension, or sensory language processing, follows a stepwise progression in which auditory input is perceived, phonemes are recognized, words are formed from these phonemes, and the meanings of these words are integrated according to grammatical structures to derive the overall meaning of a sentence. In this discussion, we focus specifically on the cognitive mechanisms underlying language comprehension after word recognition—a sophisticated and uniquely human cognitive function—to draw comparisons with the language processing capabilities of advanced artificial intelligence systems. Evidence suggests that neocortical language processing operates hierarchically, with lower layers responsible for basic tasks such as word prediction and higher layers engaged in more abstract operations, such as understanding complex concepts. This hierarchical organization enables the brain to refine its language processing through prediction-error learning, a mechanism that allows for the continuous adaptation and improvement of its internal language model. Notably, this principle of predictive coding is shared between the human brain and advanced AI systems, forming a common computational foundation for language processing. For instance, the Google Neural Machine Translation (GNMT) system exemplifies a sophisticated translation mechanism consisting of three core components: an encoder, an attention mechanism, and a decoder. The encoder, a stacked-LSTM architecture, processes each word in a sentence sequentially, ultimately generating a signal that represents the semantic content of the entire sentence. This process is facilitated by eight hierarchical layers, with the final layer synthesizing the information into a unified representation. The attention mechanism then determines the relative importance of individual words in the source sentence for translation, passing this information to the decoder. The decoder, also structured as a multi-layered LSTM, predicts the next word in the translation sequence. Through supervised learning, the system refines its parameters by comparing its outputs to human-generated translations and adjusting based on error signals. In contrast, GPT-2, another advanced AI model, employs a distinct architecture. Unlike GNMT, GPT-2 lacks discrete encoder-decoder components and instead generates predictions for subsequent words in a sentence by leveraging the preceding lexical context. During training, GPT-2 is presented with sentences containing masked words and learns to infer the missing words. After training, it generates sentences by predicting one word at a time based on the context of previously generated words. Its architecture comprises repeating blocks of attention mechanisms and two-layer feedforward neural networks, stacked multiple times—12 in GPT-1 and 48 in GPT-2. This design enables GPT-2 to process input sequences of up to 2048 words and produce probabilistic predictions for the next word, a capability acquired through iterative training on next-word prediction tasks without the need for manually curated examples.
