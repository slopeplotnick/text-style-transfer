We examined three interrelated domains of research: methodologies incorporating external feedback, strategies to minimize reliance on human annotations, and theoretical frameworks concerning the impact of representational structures on the quality of generated outputs. Typically, to align large language models with human preferences, Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) is employed. This approach leverages human preference data to train a reward model, which subsequently guides policy optimization to achieve optimal performance while maintaining proximity to a reference model. Such methodologies underpin systems like ChatGPT and Llama; however, they face challenges, including the high cost and potential inaccuracies of preference data, as well as vulnerabilities in reward models, which may be susceptible to exploitation or fail to generalize across diverse scenarios. To address these limitations, alternative techniques such as Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), KTO, Bayesian RLHF, and MaxMin-RLHF aim to refine policies directly through pairwise comparisons without relying on a dedicated value network. Nevertheless, these methods still require meticulously curated preference datasets to ensure effectiveness. Models that provide evaluative scores and feedback for reasoning and assessment maintain supervisory processes external to the model's primary operations. Alternatively, leveraging automatic supervision or signals intrinsically generated by the model can diminish dependence on human-generated labels. For instance, approaches like DeepSeek-R1 and TÜLU 3 utilize programmatic feedback mechanisms in lieu of human-provided input.
