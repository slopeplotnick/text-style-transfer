Engineering practice has increasingly adopted hierarchical systems in which supervisory agents act as intermediaries between human operators and worker agents. These supervisors evaluate outputs, identify anomalies, and possess the authority to halt operations when predefined thresholds are exceeded. This concept aligns with the "Institutional AI" framework, which will be explored in greater detail later. However, the mere implementation of supervisory mechanisms is not sufficient. Because supervisory models share the same representational substrate as the agents they oversee, they are prone to inheriting similar biases or becoming entangled in comparable feedback loops. Furthermore, excessive alignment among supervisory agents can lead to correlated failures or even deliberate collusion, undermining the integrity of the oversight process. Therefore, effective governance demands not only the addition of hierarchical layers but also the integration of diverse oversight mechanisms, redundant observation pathways, and a clear separation of authority among supervisory roles. As systems evolve toward multi-agent autonomy, the challenge of ensuring safety becomes increasingly complex. Centralized control and static regulations are no longer adequate; instead, resilient networks must be designed to independently detect, absorb, and rectify deviations through their intrinsic communication patterns. In such interconnected systems, the failure of a single model does not occur in isolation but propagates, influencing the behavior of others. Consequently, understanding the origins of collective instability and developing methods to measure it are essential for addressing AI safety in distributed ecosystems. The growing prevalence of interactions among language models further underscores the inadequacy of existing risk frameworks—such as those addressing bias, misuse, or robustness—in capturing the intricate vulnerabilities that emerge within these systems. While individual models can be audited and evaluated in isolation, their interactions create new dimensions of failure that evade current taxonomies. To address this, we propose the concept of the Emergent Systemic Risk Horizon (ESRH), an analytical framework that identifies the threshold at which the collective dynamics of interacting models become unstable, even when each individual model remains aligned.
