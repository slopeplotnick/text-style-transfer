We propose a methodology to extract meaningful information from images using advanced multimodal language models, aligning them with domain-specific ontologies to construct interpretable knowledge graphs. Moreover, we introduce a graph-based analysis pipeline that facilitates the comparison of model-generated images with their training counterparts, thereby enhancing human comprehension of their semantic similarity. This approach is instrumental in applications such as ownership attribution, interpretability enhancement, and data provenance transparency, demonstrating its potential to refine tracking mechanisms in generative models. We evaluated our method on locally trained models as well as on large-scale models through a style-specific experiment centered on the increasingly popular Ghibli-style images. In this context, our approach aims to assess the efficacy of our method in detecting stylistic influences, even in scenarios where the model's internal parameters are inaccessible. The structure of the paper is as follows: Section 2 reviews related work on interpretability in generative models and the construction of knowledge graphs using advanced language models. Section 3 delineates our proposed methodology. Section 4 presents the experimental methodology and results obtained using both localized and large-scale models. Section 5 concludes by summarizing the key findings and outlining directions for future research. As generative models continue to advance, the necessity of comprehensively understanding their implications across technical, ethical, and policy dimensions has become increasingly critical. General approaches such as t-SNE, LIME, and attention visualization provide insights into model behavior; however, they do not directly address the attribution of training data—a critical issue in domains such as authorship and copyright. To analyze the influence of training data on model outputs, various attribution methodologies have been proposed. Initial efforts in this area include influence functions, retraining-based attribution, and Shapley value approximations. Despite their utility and effectiveness in well-understood domains such as classification tasks, these methods are often impractical for large-scale models due to their dependence on gradients, retraining access, or substantial computational resources. Consequently, they are not employed in contemporary deep learning frameworks, particularly in the context of generative models where internal parameter access is restricted.
