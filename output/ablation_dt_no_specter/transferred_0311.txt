This study examines whether a computational simulation of language acquisition can autonomously identify grammatical structures and lexical categories through interaction with a language model, Delilah, which provides samples of adult language usage. Following this acquisition, the daughter model, as the simulation is termed, can utilize the learned grammatical framework to engage in interactions with another model, referred to as the mother model. Specifically, the daughter model applies this knowledge to generate novel sentences, which are then shared with the mother model during their exchanges. If the configuration allows, the mother model can offer feedback on the grammatical adequacy of these sentences, enabling the daughter model to refine its performance. Additionally, the daughter model employs its acquired grammatical knowledge to interpret new sentences produced by the mother model, such as by identifying the lexical categories used. Notably, corpus linguistics has consistently shown that words fulfilling grammatical functions tend to occur with high frequency, while content words are generally less frequent. Many content words, in fact, appear as hapax legomena, occurring only once or a few times within a given text (Powers, 1998). This distribution aligns with Zipf’s law, which observes that the frequency of a word is inversely proportional to its rank in a frequency list (cf. Mandelbrot, 1954; Zipf, 1949). Moreover, a significant correlation has been observed between the statistical distribution of lexical items in natural languages and the grammatical categories identified by linguists. This study explores whether a similar relationship can be observed in language generated by a computational system.
