Legal and ethical concerns surrounding generative models are intensifying, particularly regarding the use of copyrighted material in model training. At the heart of these debates lies the issue of authorship and the associated moral rights. Legal disputes have emerged, asserting that the unlicensed use of copyrighted content constitutes a violation of intellectual property rights. For instance, the New York Times has initiated legal proceedings against OpenAI for the unauthorized use of its journalistic archives, while Getty Images has filed a lawsuit against Stability AI for generating content derived from its proprietary database. Beyond these legal actions, artists have also voiced their concerns. Notably, Elton John, Dua Lipa, and 200 other artists have signed an open letter urging technology companies to safeguard human creativity against AI technologies capable of replicating their voices and styles without consent. In response, various organizations have proposed new regulatory frameworks. The European Union's AI Act outlines requirements for transparency in AI systems, while the United Kingdom and UNESCO have established guidelines advocating for responsible AI practices that prioritize privacy and property rights. Recently, Denmark has proposed an amendment to its copyright legislation, aiming to confer legal rights over individuals' voice, facial features, and bodily likeness—marking a pioneering initiative within Europe. This legislative amendment seeks to address the legal complexities associated with AI replication of personal attributes. As the Danish culture minister emphasized, it ensures the right to one's own body, voice, and facial features, thereby underscoring the necessity for identity protection in the context of generative AI. Despite these regulatory advancements, the opacity of most generative models remains a significant challenge, making it difficult to ascertain the influence of training data on their outputs. Even open-source models, despite their accessibility, remain prohibitively large to replicate in their entirety. Contemporary approaches, including influence functions and retraining-based attribution, necessitate direct access to the internal mechanisms of these models.
