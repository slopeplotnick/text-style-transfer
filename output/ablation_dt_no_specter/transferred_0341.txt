Except for Study 1, all simulated datasets successfully replicated the factor structure of their corresponding scale (H1). Similarly, M-CFA indicates that both simulated and real-world participants exhibit comparable response patterns to the scales (H2). These findings align with prior research on group-level simulated data (Argyle et al., 2023; Hewitt et al., 2024). Notably, Studies 3 and 4 demonstrate high levels of measurement invariance between datasets compared to Studies 1 and 2, suggesting that scales developed from simulated subjects may exhibit greater generalizability to human samples than the reverse. One possible explanation is that, when responding, large language models (LLMs) tend to interpret scale items using a more standardized semantic framework, which is less influenced by randomness and biases stemming from study conditions and population characteristics. Consequently, factor structures derived from simulated samples may possess greater semantic generalizability to external samples than those extracted from real-world data. Further research is needed to explore the factors that influence how LLMs and human participants interpret and respond to survey items. Although confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) consistently produces favorable outcomes, simulated samples fail to replicate the pairwise correlations (H3), distribution (H4), and variance (H5) observed in real-world samples. Collectively, these findings suggest that, while both datasets exhibit identical factorial structures, their score distributions differ significantly across all measured dimensions. Therefore, group-level data and their dimensional structures are sufficiently reliable for use in pilot testing, whereas individual-level simulated data are not suitable for this purpose. These limitations are consistent with the findings of Petrov and colleagues (2024). It is worth noting, however, that while Studies 2, 3, and 4 yielded comparable results, Study 1 exhibited distinct differences. These discrepancies may be attributable to several factors: first, the dimensional structure of this scale was determined using a different rotation method (minres) compared to other scales, which may have influenced the responses (Sass & Schmitt, 2010). Second, this scale employs a 5-point Likert scale for responses, whereas the others use a 6-point (Study 2) or 7-point scale.
