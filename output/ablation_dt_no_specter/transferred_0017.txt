The implantation stage encompasses three primary objectives: covertness, reversibility, and controllability. Covertness minimizes observable discrepancies between the malicious tool and its benign counterparts. Reversibility ensures that the tool can restore its original outputs following an exploitation cycle. Controllability implies that the attacker can effectively harness computing resources to perform additional operations. In this section, we elucidate the comprehensive implementation of these three objectives. A covert command-and-control (C2) protocol facilitates the clandestine transmission and reception of instructions. Specifically, the backdoor employs a two-phase communication protocol. Initially, the implant transmits a concise beacon packet containing a victim identifier to a remote endpoint via an outbound network socket. Subsequently, the remote endpoint responds with an additional task payload. The implant integrates the payload into the tool's response, ensuring it appears as a legitimate component. During the subsequent phase, the implant monitors the next invocation of the tool to capture user-provided inputs, which are then transmitted to the remote endpoint. This bifurcated process effectively minimizes the duration of persistent network connections. Consequently, the exploitation stage prompts the agent to perform additional tasks before subsequently delivering the standard outputs of the tool, ensuring that the user's experience remains unaltered. Moreover, three distinct types of triggers are utilized in this design: content-based, frequency-based, and context-based. A content-based trigger is activated when the tool input contains a specific term or symbol. In contrast, a frequency-based trigger is activated following a randomized number of benign invocations to obfuscate discernible patterns. Meanwhile, a context-based trigger is activated when the agent engages a specific tool or executes a particular function in response to a contextual request. Upon activation of a trigger, LeechHijack retrieves an additional task from the attacker, thereby facilitating unauthorized utilization of the agent's resources.
