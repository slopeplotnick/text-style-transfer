A journal entry, in accounting terms, represents the fundamental record of a financial transaction, typically involving a debit and a credit entry across one or more accounts. Each entry is enriched with attributes such as the transaction date, posting ID, currency, user responsible for the entry, applicable tax rates, monetary amounts, and, at times, descriptive annotations. These heterogeneous attributes—spanning numerical, categorical, and textual data—make journal entries an ideal testbed for evaluating anomaly detection systems designed to handle diverse data types. To support the development and validation of such models, synthetic and anonymized datasets that preserve the essential characteristics of real-world data have proven invaluable. Consequently, researchers have increasingly turned to machine learning (ML) methodologies to enhance anomaly detection in accounting datasets. Unsupervised algorithms, such as clustering techniques and the Isolation Forest, have shown promise in identifying outliers that deviate from established patterns within journal entries (Herreros-Martinez et al., 2025). Moreover, recent studies using synthetic general ledger datasets have demonstrated that these approaches can outperform traditional rule-based systems in detecting irregularities (Gronewald et al., 2024). However, traditional ML models often rely on structured data and may lack the contextual understanding required to distinguish genuinely risky anomalies from benign outliers. In contrast, the emergence of Large Language Models (LLMs), such as GPT-4, has introduced new opportunities for auditing processes. These foundation models exhibit advanced natural language understanding and reasoning capabilities, and preliminary research suggests their potential as AI auditors or co-pilots in financial data analysis (Gu et al., 2023). Furthermore, recent advancements highlight the versatility of LLMs in anomaly detection across various data modalities. For instance, SigLLM has been successfully applied to time-series data (Alnegheimish et al., 2024), while AnoLLM has demonstrated efficacy in identifying irregularities within tabular datasets (Tsai et al., 2025), both achieving competitive performance with minimal data preprocessing. Nevertheless, the deployment of LLMs in high-stakes applications such as auditing requires careful consideration. By their nature, LLMs can occasionally produce inconsistent responses, exhibit biases, or even fabricate information. In response, the research community has developed frameworks like AuditLLM (Amirizaniani et al., 2024a) and LLMAuditor (Amirizaniani et al., 2024b) to systematically evaluate and monitor LLM outputs. These efforts underscore that while LLMs hold significant potential, robust governance and oversight are essential when employing them as decision-support tools in critical domains like auditing.
