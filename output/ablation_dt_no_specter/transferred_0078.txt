Faulty code not being executed. Our approach requires that erroneous statements are executed by at least one failing test case; statements that are never executed cannot be diagnosed. Consequently, insufficient test coverage may lead to undetected issues within the codebase. To partially address this limitation, we employ multiple failing test cases in conjunction with lightweight static analysis. Bounded model checking (BMC). Our methodology utilizes bounded encodings of programs to facilitate analysis. However, loop unrolling and depth bounds can exclude feasible behaviours, potentially rendering otherwise valid diagnoses unreachable under the imposed constraints. Moreover, when iterations are truncated, diagnoses that apply to the bounded model may fail to generalise to the original program. To mitigate this, we choose bounds conservatively and report them alongside our results, though incompleteness due to bounding remains an inherent limitation. Pointers, arrays, and undefined behaviour. As detailed in Section 5.1, CFAULTS employs static analysis tools, such as CPPCHECK and CLANG-TIDY, to identify issues including uninitialized variables and potential runtime errors (e.g., division by zero). If any such issues are detected, CFAULTS promptly terminates and reports them to the user. Additionally, CFAULTS leverages CBMC's memory-safety checks (e.g., --bounds-check, --pointer-check) to detect invalid memory operations, such as out-of-bounds accesses and unsafe pointer arithmetic. However, CFAULTS adheres to CBMC's specifications for ANSI C, treating behaviours deemed acceptable by CBMC as valid, while violations not flagged by CBMC may remain undetected. Enhancing the alignment between CBMC's configuration and the intended C semantics for pointers and arrays is left for future work. External validity. We evaluated this approach on benchmark and student programs that compile successfully and include well-defined specifications, such as assertions or expected outputs. Extending this approach to larger programs, those with extensive input/output operations, concurrency, or complex libraries, or to specifications expressed in richer formalisms, may require additional engineering efforts and could potentially alter the findings of this study.
