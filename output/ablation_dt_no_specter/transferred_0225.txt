Recent advancements in reasoning techniques have significantly enhanced the capabilities of large language models (LLMs), elevating expectations for their ability to deliver accurate, truthful, and reliable information. Nevertheless, emerging evidence suggests that iterative reasoning processes may foster belief entrenchment rather than promote truth-seeking behavior. In this study, we propose a systematic framework for evaluating belief entrenchment in LLM reasoning by leveraging the Martingale property from Bayesian statistics. This property implies that, under rational belief updating, the expected value of future beliefs should remain equal to the current belief, meaning belief updates cannot be predicted solely from the current belief. To measure violations of this principle, we introduce the unsupervised, regression-based Martingale Score, which signals deviations from the Bayesian ability to update on new evidence. In open-ended problem domains, including event forecasting, value-laden questions, and academic paper review, we found such violations to be widespread across models, reasoning paradigms, problem domains, and system prompts, where future beliefs are consistently predictable from the model's current belief—a phenomenon we term belief entrenchment. Through comprehensive experiments, we identify the models (e.g., GPT-4o), reasoning techniques (e.g., chain of thought), and domains (e.g., forecasting) that are more prone to belief entrenchment. Furthermore, we validate the Martingale Score by demonstrating that it predicts ground-truth accuracy in problem domains where ground truth labels are available. This indicates that, while designed as an unsupervised metric that operates even in domains without access to ground truth, the Martingale Score serves as a useful proxy for the truth-seeking ability of the LLM reasoning process. Consider a stubborn person who refuses to be shown wrong or a king surrounded by sycophants. Human beings, in their efforts to seek true beliefs, often end up entrenching their pre-existing beliefs, whether due to their own confirmation bias [Klayman, 1995] or due to external influences.
