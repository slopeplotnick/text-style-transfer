In recent years, brain-computer interfaces (BCIs) have garnered significant attention as mechanisms for enabling direct communication between the brain and external devices or digital systems, both in the scientific and medical communities as well as in mainstream media. These interfaces can be categorized as either invasive, requiring surgical intervention, or non-invasive, not necessitating such procedures. BCIs are multidisciplinary systems that enable individuals with severe motor disabilities, such as those resulting from locked-in syndrome, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), spinal cord injuries, or severe movement or speech disorders following stroke, to communicate and sometimes even regain mobility when traditional modalities are no longer viable. Consequently, the imperative to enhance the efficacy of non-invasive BCIs has intensified, necessitating a comprehensive analysis of an expanded array of EEG-derived features. Feature extraction techniques are highly diverse and play a critical role in EEG analysis, ranging from straightforward approaches like time-domain or frequency-domain analyses to more sophisticated multi-dimensional techniques. Transforming raw EEG signals into meaningful representations, such as time-domain statistics, frequency-domain features like power spectral density, and time-frequency methods like wavelet transforms, is crucial for accurate signal classification and robust performance. Moreover, developing features that exhibit robustness and generalizability across individuals is critical, given the inherent variability and non-stationarity of EEG signals across different users. Challenges such as temporal variability and inter-dataset discrepancies necessitate the implementation of methodologies like transfer learning and domain generalization to ensure the reliability and robustness of extracted features. An open and critical question remains: which combinations of feature-extraction and classification techniques yield truly robust and generalizable performance, especially given the substantial inter-individual variability inherent in EEG signals? Addressing this issue requires the adoption of rigorous evaluation protocols designed to mitigate overly optimistic biases. In practice, this means using participant-independent validation strategies, such as leave-one-subject-out cross-validation, employing performance metrics robust to class imbalances like balanced accuracy and AUROC, assessing statistical significance via nonparametric or permutation testing, and rigorously guarding against data leakage. This is particularly important by ensuring that operations like filter estimation and feature normalization are confined strictly to the training folds and never informed by the test data.
