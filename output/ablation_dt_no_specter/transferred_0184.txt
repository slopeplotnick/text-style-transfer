As illustrated in Figure 9 and detailed in Table 10, there is a notable reduction in the overall frequency of reasoning components following optimization. This reduction is largely attributable to the streamlined nature of optimized prompts, which are typically shorter and more focused, avoiding redundant instructions such as "answer like Shakespeare." Such instructions often inflate the length and diversity of reasoning chains. Consequently, the proportion of the "Others" category drops significantly, suggesting that optimized prompts result in more structured and interpretable model behavior. Moreover, the frequency of reasoning components like Logical reasoning and Reframing undergoes substantial changes, underscoring the sensitivity of these behaviors to prompt formulation. **Task-Dependent Effects of Optimization:** The effects of optimization are highly contingent on the specific task. For mathematical problem-solving, optimized prompts lead to a clear increase in both Calculation and Logical reasoning, aligning well with the domain's requirements. However, these same prompts fail to elicit similar reasoning components in other tasks. In moral reasoning, for instance, we observe a sharp decline in the absolute frequency of most reasoning components, although Logical reasoning remains the dominant behavior in relative terms. **Rare Components Across Scenarios:** Certain reasoning components, such as Backtracking and Backward chaining, remain consistently rare across all evaluated tasks, likely due to the absence of reflective capabilities akin to reinforcement learning (RL) systems. **Reasoning Features as Predictors of Performance:** Finally, we investigate whether the structural composition of reasoning chains alone can predict prompt performance.
