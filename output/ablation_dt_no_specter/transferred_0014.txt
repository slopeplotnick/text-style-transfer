To effectively comprehend user inquiries and the intricacies of items, the system must first possess foundational knowledge of the subject matter. With this knowledge, it can accurately discern users' genuine intentions, extract salient item attributes, and align them with users' requirements. Moreover, visual representations of items often convey attributes, such as aesthetic or tactile qualities, that are challenging to articulate verbally. To address this, the system integrates item image data, synthesizing insights from both textual and visual modalities. By leveraging cross-modal information integration, the system constructs a more comprehensive and precise representation of the item. Following the identification of user intent and the extraction of item attributes, the system adheres to objective rules for attribute matching and verification. At this stage, these rules ensure logical consistency and operational functionality. 

Step 1: Knowledge and Reasoning. As outlined in Section 2.3, knowledge is categorized into two types: general world knowledge and domain-specific e-commerce knowledge. Large language models possess extensive general knowledge acquired during training, enabling them to understand common concepts and relationships. However, within the e-commerce domain, numerous phrases and terms present challenges that cannot be resolved solely through literal meanings. These challenges fall into two primary categories. First, Rare Queries encompass highly specific inquiries about atypical items, for which models often lack the necessary background knowledge to accurately associate terms with their intended meanings. For example, the query "strange things are also loved by someone," presented alongside the item "novel and interesting toys," actually pertains to the title of a book. Without prior knowledge, the system struggles to align the query with the item based on literal semantics alone. Additional examples include brand-specific jargon, product nicknames, and marketing terminology that are comprehensible only within the e-commerce context. Second, Ambiguous Items refer to items so rare or obscure that the system lacks sufficient prior knowledge to interpret or categorize them effectively. For instance, an item labeled "lying gate" cannot be understood or classified solely by its nominal designation, as deeper contextual understanding of its attributes is required. Addressing such cases necessitates the integration of supplementary domain-specific e-commerce knowledge to enable accurate interpretation and categorization.
