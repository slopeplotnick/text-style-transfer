While machine learning and generative techniques are popular and widespread, the application of AI in decision-making remains relatively uncommon. This is because decision-making involves the irrevocable allocation of resources, a practice that falls outside the typical scope of academic research. To date, the only significant and impactful use of decision-making AI in mineral exploration has been in the characterization of the high-grade portion of the Mingomba deposit in Zambia (Dempsey, 2024). In this instance, historical drilling data was re-analyzed using advanced analytical tools, leading to the generation of new hypotheses that suggested the presence of a higher-grade mineralization zone than previously identified. AI, formulated as a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP), was employed to systematically plan drilling operations. The primary objective was to reduce uncertainties surrounding geological hypotheses about the spatial extent of high-grade mineralization. This approach proved to be significantly more efficient than traditional grid-based drilling methods (Mern and Caers, 2023). The efficiency stemmed from prioritizing the falsification of geological hypotheses rather than merely attempting to locate mineralization. In Part III, I will explore how AI can accelerate the discovery and characterization of critical mineral deposits. I will begin by focusing on data analysis and prediction, before transitioning to AI-based decision-making. The initial deployment of AI was, in some quarters, met with defensiveness. There was a perception that AI might replace or diminish the importance of domain experts. This concern, rooted in fears of job displacement, remains prevalent today. However, the idea that AI could independently make groundbreaking discoveries—such as pinpointing a specific location for resource extraction and directing robotic drilling—is unfounded. Rather, AI should be seen as a tool to enhance the capabilities of domain experts by automating tasks that are either beyond their expertise or unnecessary for their direct involvement. This allows experts to focus on the creative and process-driven aspects of their work, such as those inherent in geology and geochemistry.
