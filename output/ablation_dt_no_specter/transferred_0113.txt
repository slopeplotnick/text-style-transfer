We evaluate our knowledge graph-based system through two complementary experimental setups to ascertain the impact of data attribution in generative models. First, we conduct controlled data removal experiments on a locally trained model to determine if our method can identify influential training samples. Second, we explore the influence of stylistic elements by employing Ghibli-style images in a black-box setting, where the internal workings of the model remain inaccessible. We construct a "Ghibli World" knowledge graph derived from the original films and conduct a comparative analysis with stylized images. These setups facilitate the examination of both direct data influence and the traceability of stylistic elements within generative models. To ensure a fair and interpretable comparison, we replicate the experimental setup from a recent influence framework that operates independently of access to training parameters. This method functions in two stages: initially, it retrieves training samples whose textual descriptions closely align with the user's prompt. Subsequently, it ranks these samples based on their cosine similarity to the generated output. The most analogous training images are then excised, and the model undergoes retraining to empirically assess their impact. We implemented our graph-based method under analogous conditions, utilizing the same dataset, prompts, and generative model. Instead of relying on textual and latent-space similarity measures, we identified significant examples by evaluating the structural similarity of the corresponding knowledge graphs. This approach allowed for a direct comparison between the two methods in terms of their capacity to identify and interpret influential instances. For the generative model, we employed a compact implementation based on the open-source dalle-pytorch package, trained on a dataset comprising 44,000 fashion images paired with descriptive captions. We maintained the original setup and parameters, thereby ensuring complete control over the training data. This approach facilitated experimentation with data removal and subsequent retraining.
