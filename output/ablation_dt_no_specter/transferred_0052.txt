When the target is far away, the character transitions to a fast running behavior to close the distance. These nuanced behaviors emerge solely from the integration of a stationary score-matching motion prior with a simple target-distance task objective, eliminating the need for an explicit motion planner to determine the character's movement strategy. SMP provides the flexibility to adapt behaviors from the dataset, enabling the creation of new skills for diverse tasks. For instance, in the challenging Dodgeball task, agents trained with SMP autonomously develop agile jumping and dodging skills, as illustrated in Figure 5(c). These emergent behaviors, although absent from the original dataset, closely resemble real human strategies in dodgeball. In contrast, AMP struggles with this task, likely due to the instability of the adversarial objective when the required dodging skills significantly deviate from the motions in the reference dataset, which primarily consists of basic locomotion. Furthermore, policies trained using AMP with a static discriminator fail to generate natural behaviors or achieve strong task performance, highlighting the limitations of adversarial priors in being effectively reused, even for similar tasks. On the other hand, SMP enables a single pretrained motion prior to be reused across different tasks and policies. As shown in the task return curves in Figure 7, the stationary score-matching motion prior also offers superior sample efficiency, consistently providing stable and informative guidance throughout training. Developing the ability to recover from arbitrary fallen states is a critical skill for both humans and humanoid agents. However, this task is particularly challenging, as many existing methods rely on auxiliary rewards to suppress overly dynamic or erratic motions. While approaches like AMP adaptively update the motion prior during training to learn natural get-up behaviors, our method demonstrates that even with a stationary motion prior, SMP can effectively train a robust get-up policy. As shown in Figure 5(e), the character, when knocked down at a significant distance, successfully executes a sequence of coordinated actions, including rolling over, pushing itself upright with its hands, and ultimately returning to a standing position.
