The task is framed as unsupervised fraud detection in multi-type journal entry data, where labels for fraudulent activity are scarce and often unavailable in practice, making supervised learning infeasible at scale. Therefore, we aim to detect anomalous entries by modeling irregular patterns across structured and unstructured fields to reflect real-world auditing conditions. To evaluate this approach, we utilize a synthetic journal entry dataset introduced by Gronewald et al. [2024], designed to emulate the day-to-day bookkeeping practices of a medium-sized enterprise. Each entry features a unique posting ID, date and time of posting and transaction, Credit/Debit (CD) flags, tax rate, and Account ID, among other elements. Moreover, we benchmark our method on real-world anonymized accounting data from our industry partner. Unlike typical double-entry bookkeeping systems where posting IDs link balanced debit-credit cycles, this dataset lacks such identifiers due to anonymization, breaking the usual transactional structure. Consequently, we treat it as a transaction-level fraud detection problem. To compensate for missing context, we enrich the LLM prompt with bookkeeping heuristics, guidelines, and summary statistics, which proves essential to outperform traditional ML baselines. Privacy is a critical aspect when dealing with confidential information such as accounting data; hence, preference was given to open-weight LLMs (LLaMa AI [2025], Gemma Team et al. [2024]) over proprietary LLM API endpoints like GPT-4 by OpenAI [2024] when benchmarking our method. Our method demonstrates improved performance relative to baseline models on both the synthetic dataset by Gronewald et al. [2024] and the real-world anonymized dataset. We use Isolation Forest, introduced by Liu et al. [2008], as our baseline due to its theoretical advantages in modeling tabular data, as noted by Grinsztajn et al. [2022], and its alignment with the unsupervised anomaly detection task proposed in Section 3.
