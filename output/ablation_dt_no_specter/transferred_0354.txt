Developing virtual characters capable of replicating human-like movement is essential for enhancing the realism of games and virtual reality environments. This capability is equally significant in robotics, where naturalistic movements can improve safety, energy efficiency, and the acquisition of broadly applicable skills from human data. Given the complexity and expressiveness of human motion, researchers have increasingly adopted data-driven approaches to synthesize realistic character animations by leveraging motion capture datasets to learn patterns of human movement. Kinematics-based methods, such as autoregressive models, variational autoencoders (VAEs), and diffusion models, have demonstrated efficacy in generating plausible animations. However, these methods often fail to explicitly incorporate physical constraints, leading to kinematic artifacts and physically implausible behaviors, particularly in novel scenarios or tasks. In contrast, physics-based methods aim to generate motion that adheres to real-world physical principles by employing control policies and dynamic equations. These approaches, which often utilize trajectory optimization or reinforcement learning, are designed to produce motion that is both functional and physically realistic. Earlier heuristic-based methods, while capable of generating robotic movements, often resulted in unnatural and rigid behaviors, as manually designing heuristics that capture the complexity of human motion is inherently challenging. To address these limitations, contemporary approaches integrate data-driven techniques, such as model predictive control and trajectory optimization, to better emulate naturalistic human movements. More recently, reinforcement learning-based motion tracking methods have further advanced the realism and agility of robotic motion by effectively imitating a diverse range of reference motion clips. However, these tracking-based methods constrain controllers to closely follow specific motion clips, which can hinder their ability to generalize to novel tasks. To overcome this limitation, many systems now incorporate task-specific motion planners or high-level policies, which enable controllers to select optimal movements or latent-space skills tailored to the requirements of the task at hand.
