4.5.1 Fact Verification. Our findings indicate that the efficacy of FV methods is heavily dependent on the performance of the retrieval module and the availability of relevant external information. As shown in Table 3, this dependency creates a significant challenge when the retrieval component fails to provide the necessary evidence. Specifically, when the retriever is unable to locate pertinent information—potentially due to keyword mismatches in the LLM-Q configuration—the verification process is forced to proceed with insufficient context. This lack of corroborative evidence leads to erratic model behavior: the model may either exhibit unwarranted skepticism (resulting in false alarms, as seen in Case 1) or accept erroneous claims without verification (causing missed errors, as illustrated in Case 2). However, when the retrieval process is enhanced with the generated answer (LLM-QA), the system is more likely to retrieve relevant supporting documents, enabling the FV model to make more accurate and reliable predictions. 4.5.2 Hallucination Detection. In contrast, HD methods often struggle with the inherent semantic flexibility of language generation, which can lead to false alarms. Table 4 highlights a clear dichotomy: while HD methods perform well on straightforward responses (Group A), their effectiveness diminishes significantly when dealing with more expressive or creative articulations (Group B). For example, a model may be factually confident about an event, such as the formation year of a band, but lexically uncertain in its phrasing. Current HD methods are unable to reliably distinguish between legitimate semantic diversity and actual falsehoods. Consequently, the high entropy caused by diverse phrasing is frequently misinterpreted as hallucination, leading to the erroneous flagging of correct generations simply because of their linguistic variability. 4.5.3 Implications for Hybrid Design. Our analysis underscores the complementary nature of HD and FV, as they exhibit distinct error boundaries that justify their integration. FV serves as a precision-oriented anchor: its judgments are highly reliable when relevant evidence is successfully retrieved, but its performance deteriorates in the absence of such evidence. On the other hand, HD acts as a recall-oriented safety net: it can identify potential errors even without external evidence, though it remains vulnerable to misinterpretation due to semantic flexibility. Therefore, an optimal hybrid strategy should not treat both paradigms equally. Instead, it should prioritize FV’s decisions when relevant evidence is available, while leveraging HD’s intrinsic signals when the retrieval process fails to yield pertinent information. This conditional complementarity forms the foundation for the hierarchical design of our hybrid framework, as discussed in RQ3.
