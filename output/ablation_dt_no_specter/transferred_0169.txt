This paper addresses a critical issue by reconceptualizing safety as a property of interaction architectures rather than a characteristic of isolated models. To advance this perspective, we introduce the Emergent Systemic Risk Horizon (ESRH) framework, which provides a structured approach to identifying the conditions under which collective instability emerges and the mechanisms driving such dynamics. The progression of this concept unfolds systematically: beginning with dyadic interactions, expanding to multi-agent configurations, developing a taxonomy of systemic risks, and culminating in a proposal for Institutional AI—a design framework aimed at mitigating these risks within complex systems. Currently, the safety architecture of language models is centered on the human-AI dyad, relying on prompts and filters to enforce compliance by blocking harmful content or adhering to predefined protocols. While this approach is valuable, it presumes that model behavior is both observable and stable. However, language models are inherently probabilistic systems whose outputs depend on latent states, conversational histories, and deployment contexts. Even minor contextual shifts can redirect reasoning trajectories without breaching surface-level constraints. The limitations of this paradigm become increasingly apparent when models interact with one another. In multi-agent systems, the output of one model serves as the input for another, creating feedback loops that amplify subtle deviations. For instance, benchmarks like JAILJUDGE reveal that multi-model interactions can significantly alter jailbreak success rates, as reasoning chains between judge, critic, and generator models produce outcomes that single-model tests fail to predict. Similarly, experiments in simulated market environments show that independently aligned agents can spontaneously coordinate to form inequitable equilibria, exposing strategic behaviors that arise solely through interaction. These findings underscore that alignment at the component level does not guarantee alignment at the system level. A network of individually compliant models can still generate unsafe global dynamics when reciprocal influences, incentives, and network structures interact. Therefore, the focus must shift from evaluating isolated prompts or actions to analyzing the protocols and communication architectures that govern system-wide behavior.
