Multimodal large language models (MLLMs) have made significant strides in recent years, yet they continue to struggle with accurately capturing and analyzing fine-grained details within high-resolution images. To address this challenge, recent research has explored segmenting images into smaller regions and using specialized models to evaluate the relevance of each segment in relation to a given query. This method prioritizes the most salient regions corresponding to the primary object of interest while disregarding less relevant information. However, fragmenting the image can disrupt the congruence between the segments and the inquiry. Empirical evaluations have shown that segments of varying dimensions are more effective at different levels of detail. Therefore, we devised a method to comprehend intricate images without requiring additional training. To mitigate the issue of fragmentation of objects across different image segments, we employ a multi-resolution semantic fusion method that integrates semantic similarity maps obtained at varying levels of detail, enhancing semantic accuracy and maintaining the integrity of target objects. Moreover, to facilitate the direct localization of objects on a global scale, we implement an open-vocabulary object detection model that identifies object regions through a sliding-window approach. Empirical evaluations on high-resolution image understanding benchmarks demonstrate the efficacy of our proposed method. While large language models have shown significant proficiency in integrating and interpreting visual and linguistic information, thereby facilitating tasks that necessitate the synthesis of both modalities, a prevalent approach among most models is to process input images at fixed and predetermined resolutions. This simplifies the process but introduces substantial limitations. The reduction of high-resolution images to lower detail levels can result in the distortion of shapes and the blurring of features, compromising the fidelity of intricate details. Empirical studies indicate that existing methodologies are insufficient for tasks requiring detailed image analysis, as evidenced by their suboptimal performance on benchmarks specifically designed to assess the comprehension of detailed imagery.
