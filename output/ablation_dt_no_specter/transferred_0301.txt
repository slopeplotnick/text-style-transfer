Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models have demonstrated remarkable efficacy in robotic manipulation tasks, benefiting from large-scale, multimodal datasets collected via human teleoperation or scripted policies. During pretraining, these models employ methodologies such as flow-matching or diffusion-based objectives to effectively learn multimodal behavioral policies that encompass diverse manipulation modes, environments, and tasks. Subsequently, the pretrained VLA model is adapted to specific downstream tasks through supervised fine-tuning on smaller, task-specific datasets. Leveraging a pretrained VLA model for novel tasks typically yields superior outcomes compared to training from scratch, as the model possesses substantial pre-existing knowledge in visual perception, language comprehension, and object and spatial recognition. However, despite their generally robust performance, a notable limitation emerges when VLA models are deployed in practical applications. This limitation arises from the persistence of extraneous actions that do not contribute to task completion, even after fine-tuning. Specifically, during training, VLA models acquire a multitude of actions from diverse datasets, complicating the task of concentrating solely on the requisite actions for a specific task. Consequently, even after fine-tuning, these models retain extraneous actions that are not pertinent to the task at hand. Moreover, the datasets employed for fine-tuning may exhibit stylistic variations due to their collection from disparate sources or methodologies, some of which may not be optimal. This presence of extraneous actions introduces a discrepancy between the operational behavior of the VLA model and the optimal task-specific actions it is expected to execute, a discrepancy that becomes evident upon deployment.
