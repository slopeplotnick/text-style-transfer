Despite the evolution of optimization approaches, traditional methods for construction coordination exhibit fundamental limitations. The necessity for comprehensive problem specification at the outset conflicts with the inherently dynamic nature of construction projects, where task requirements, environmental conditions, and resource availability continuously change throughout execution. Moreover, computational complexity often becomes prohibitive, especially in large-scale projects characterized by numerous robots and intricate task dependencies. These persistent limitations have motivated researchers to explore learning-based methodologies that can adapt to dynamic environments without requiring predefined plans. Reinforcement Learning (RL) has emerged as a transformative approach, enabling robotic systems to acquire cooperative behaviors through iterative interactions with their environment, thus mitigating the reliance on comprehensive pre-planning. However, RL necessitates extensive training through repeated environmental interaction and trial-and-error exploration, which introduces significant risks in construction settings due to potential coordination errors that may jeopardize worker safety or damage costly equipment. Furthermore, the prolonged duration required for policy training renders RL impractical for projects constrained by stringent timelines. These safety and efficiency concerns have prompted the development of RL methodologies tailored specifically to construction applications. In 2022, Lee et al. developed a digital twin-driven Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) methodology for adaptive task allocation in robotic construction. This approach integrated Building Information Modeling (BIM) with DRL to facilitate context-aware coordination decisions that account for spatial constraints, temporal dependencies, and safety requirements. The digital twin framework enabled safer policy learning within simulations prior to real-world implementation. Nevertheless, RL continues to encounter challenges, as models trained in simulated environments may fail to perform reliably in real-world applications, posing potential safety risks. Moreover, the direct application of learned strategies from simulations to actual construction sites, which often present varying conditions, remains problematic. These sim-to-real transfer challenges have necessitated the development of more sophisticated RL methodologies.
