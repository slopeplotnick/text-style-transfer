To ensure that each (chunk + summary) pair adhered to the 512-token limit, each article was divided into smaller chunks. The summary was then compared against each chunk, and the final SummaC score was calculated by averaging the entailment scores across all chunks, resulting in a value within the range of [0,1]. Heatmaps and a one-way ANOVA were utilized to examine whether the position of the input significantly influenced the generated summaries. Figure 3 displays the mean values of three evaluation metrics across six input orders and three stance categories. For each condition, the mean was derived by averaging the metric scores across all article clusters with the same input order and stance. The observed variation in shading and metric values across input orders provides clear evidence that the positioning of input documents affects the similarity patterns in summaries generated by large language models (LLMs). Specifically, summaries achieve higher ROUGE-L and BERTScore values when aligned with the document placed first in the sequence. Notably, BERTScore values for Position 1 surpass those for Positions 2 and 3, indicating that the first document in the sequence has a stronger influence on the generated summaries. Addressing RQ1, our findings consistently demonstrate that the generated summaries exhibit greater similarity to the initial article in the input sequence. However, the absolute metric values observed in this study differ from those reported in prior summarization research. This discrepancy arises because, unlike previous studies that compare model outputs to human-written reference summaries [G et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2023b], our evaluation directly compares the generated summaries to the full original articles. Given that the generated summaries average approximately 220 words, while the source articles range from 300 to 1600 words, this significant length disparity naturally reduces token-overlap and embedding-based similarity scores. Therefore, our analysis focuses on relative performance across different input conditions rather than absolute metric values. Moreover, the evaluation process is constrained by the limited availability of metrics designed to assess alignment between summaries and their source documents. Existing evaluation frameworks are predominantly reference-based, which restricts their ability to measure source alignment. SummaC, a sentence-level entailment metric, consistently produces low scores and demonstrates limited sensitivity to positional variations. Since abstract summaries often omit specific details from the source text, entailment-based scoring mechanisms tend to penalize unsupported or implied statements. Consequently, even when the model prioritizes information from the first article, it is unlikely to reuse its exact phrasing. As a result, SummaC is unable to account for subtle content biases unless explicit supporting sentences are preserved.
