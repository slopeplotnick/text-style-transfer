In the fast-moving world of Natural Language Processing (NLP) today, large language models (LLMs) such as GPT and BERT have become the principal agents of change. They can generate human-like text, answer multifaceted questions, and engage in conversation with remarkable fluency. If a method produces an output that makes sense ex-ante, we can think of it as being near north on a compass. However, while an answer generated by LLMs may be related to the input and follow logical arguments, it often lacks truth value since it is not based on known facts. This plausible yet misleading output is akin to a mirage that cannot be debunked from ex-ante knowledge. This phenomenon, known as hallucination, is a major obstacle to their widespread use. In other words, it is like an experienced storyteller who, in the flow of narration, occasionally includes details outside of reality while attempting to convey the truth. Similarly, LLMs may produce content that seems accurate but lacks grounding in the actual data they were trained on. A simpler analogy would be a GPS giving directions confidently but occasionally leading you down a nonexistent road. More technically, hallucinations in LLMs manifest as generated content that does not match factual or expected outputs relative to their training data. These inaccuracies may appear as false statements, fabricated references, or incorrect reasoning paths. Just like the misguided GPS, the LLM seems to know where it's going but ends up in the wrong place. Addressing these challenges remains a focal point of ongoing efforts within the AI research community. Hallucinations can occur even in advanced models. Although LLMs are remarkable, they are not consistently reliable, especially when it comes to producing factual data. In contexts like healthcare, legal matters, and academic research, the stakes are significant.
