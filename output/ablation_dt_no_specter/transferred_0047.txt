However, robustness and generalization remain critical challenges. Despite the use of large and diverse training datasets, these models often fail to generalize effectively to out-of-distribution scenarios, exhibiting pronounced degradation when exposed to unseen camera viewpoints, lighting changes, or scene perturbations. Such brittleness significantly limits their applicability in real-world environments, where visual conditions are inherently dynamic and unpredictable. To address these challenges, researchers typically employ two primary strategies: data-centric and representation-centric approaches. The data-centric strategy seeks to enhance robustness by increasing visual diversity, typically through large-scale, multi-view datasets like Libero-Plus. Although this approach exposes models to a wider range of visual conditions, scaling data collection to real-world settings remains costly and labor-intensive, making continual adaptation impractical. Conversely, representation-centric methodologies aim to achieve robustness through geometric consistency, learning geometry-aware or 3D-consistent representations. By leveraging multi-view supervision or specialized 3D-aware architectures, these methods improve viewpoint invariance. However, despite being geometry-aware, they remain sensitive to task-irrelevant visual factors such as background clutter and illumination changes, highlighting a persistent gap between viewpoint robustness and general visual adaptability. It is commonly presumed that augmenting data or employing more sophisticated 3D-focused designs is imperative for enhancing model robustness. Nevertheless, there is a paucity of research examining whether the underlying issue resides in the representation of spatial information itself. To investigate this, we adopt a paradigm that conceptually decomposes the model into two components: Spatial Modeling, executed by the visual encoder, constructs spatial relationships between objects—such as positions, orientations, interactions, and occlusions—from the image, producing the spatial representations required for downstream tasks; Physical Modeling, integrates the task instructions, spatial representations, and historical actions to perform high-level reasoning and generate actionable strategies. A viewpoint shift primarily alters the spatial configuration of the observed scene, rather than the underlying task semantics or action dynamics. Therefore, the degradation of performance under viewpoint changes is largely due to misalignment in Spatial Modeling, while the Physical Modeling components remain functionally competent. In other words, the high-level policy still retains its reasoning and control capability, but receives spatially distorted visual embeddings, leading to failures in downstream coordination.
