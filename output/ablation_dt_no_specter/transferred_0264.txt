Challenges facing humanity, such as climate change and the transition to alternative energy sources, far exceed the scope of any single scientific discipline, yet we still lack academic programs specifically dedicated to Mineral Exploration. Moreover, the production of information has become nearly instantaneous, with its analytical capabilities continuously advancing. The traditional practice of consulting physical journals in libraries has been supplanted by algorithms capable of searching and synthesizing vast quantities of documents in real time. Meanwhile, there is a declining interest in pursuing degrees in economic geology and mining engineering within the Western world. Therefore, it is imperative to preserve the longstanding knowledge concerning the formation of ore deposits, much of which remains archived in antiquated geological reports dispersed globally. The establishment of high-quality databases, akin to those developed in Canada and Australia, is essential on a worldwide scale. In Africa, only a single nation has established such a database (gsd.gov.zm). Facilitating open and accessible online platforms for such information is critical to fostering increased investment. In this essay, I advocate for greater collaboration between the scientific and industrial communities in developing a novel methodological framework for mineral exploration. As exploration progresses "under cover," the traditional methodology of initiating with surface outcrops and employing deterministic geophysical inversion has proven ineffective, as evidenced by the declining rate of discoveries. Consequently, a paradigm shift towards a decision-focused approach that explicitly evaluates risk through uncertainty quantification is imperative to mitigate erroneous drilling outcomes. This necessitates an acknowledgment of the fundamental lack of comprehensive understanding of orebodies, as evidenced by the quantification of epistemic uncertainty. Furthermore, it is imperative to develop advanced methodologies for the appropriate handling of data at their respective scales through geostatistical techniques, which are currently insufficient, to mitigate the occurrence of false positives.
