The Area Under the Curve (AUC) metric is particularly emphasized to evaluate the model's ranking capability independent of specific decision thresholds, thereby addressing the challenges posed by class imbalance within evaluation datasets. In this section, we introduce our novel evaluation framework, specifically designed to unify the historically isolated domains of Hallucination Detection and Fact Verification. We begin by examining the underlying factors contributing to the separation of these two research areas and critically analyze the limitations of existing evaluation methodologies that hinder comprehensive assessment within a unified context. Subsequently, we elucidate the foundational concepts underpinning our system and delineate its operational mechanisms. Before delving into the specifics, it is crucial to comprehend the rationale for the historical separation of these two domains. The primary challenge lies in the fundamental incompatibility between the text-centric design of traditional Fact Verification datasets and the model-centric requirements of Hallucination Detection methodologies. Fact Verification frameworks, such as FEVER, typically employ static pairs of claims and corresponding labels, without accounting for the provenance of these claims. This configuration proves inadequate for Hallucination Detection approaches, which necessitate an examination of generative processes, including the assessment of internal states or cross-sample consistency. The absence of these critical signals in the fixed datasets utilized for Fact Verification poses significant challenges for the concurrent evaluation of both paradigms. An alternative approach involves constructing evaluation tests using pre-generated outputs from large language models, such as those provided by HELM. While this methodology offers utility, it is constrained by two significant limitations within the rapidly evolving domain of artificial intelligence: Model Obsolescence and the Inability to Evaluate New Models. The accelerated pace of large language model (LLM) development implies that datasets derived from current state-of-the-art models, such as LLaMA-3, risk becoming mere historical artifacts as newer architectures are introduced. Moreover, established testing protocols are inadequate for assessing hallucination detection methodologies across diverse or emergent models. Given that these methodologies frequently rely on particular model characteristics, a system that fails to accommodate new models impedes advancement and fails to demonstrate genuine adaptability.
