To address this challenge, it is essential to develop tools that capture the topology, propagation, and co-evolution of reasoning processes over time, rather than merely evaluating the correctness of isolated instances. The concept of the Emergent Systemic Risk Horizon serves as a critical marker, signaling the point at which the assumptions of separability and reversibility no longer apply. Beyond this threshold, safety must be understood as a property of the entire interconnected system—its ability to sustain coherence, interpretability, and responsiveness amid continuous interaction. Achieving this perspective will require the creation of innovative experimental paradigms, datasets, and metrics specifically designed to observe the temporal dynamics of collective behavior. Moreover, it will necessitate governance frameworks that operate at the same systemic scale as these networks, a topic explored further in the following section, where we introduce the concept of Institutional AI. As language model systems grow in complexity, governance approaches must evolve in parallel. When these models begin to function autonomously—engaging in reasoning, negotiation, and collaboration without ongoing human oversight—traditional methods such as centralized control, static safety protocols, or periodic evaluations become insufficient. These approaches assume stability, transparency, and clear accountability, characteristics that are increasingly absent in dynamic, multi-agent systems. Therefore, we propose Institutional AI as a framework for embedding rules, checks, and evaluative mechanisms directly within the architecture of these systems, rather than appending them externally. The goal is not to replicate human institutions but to integrate their advantageous features—such as distributed authority, procedural transparency, and adaptive rule modification—into networks of interacting models. While constitutional AI, which relies on predefined normative rules or behavioral principles established during training or fine-tuning, can enhance alignment and consistency in single-agent interactions, it falls short of addressing the systemic risks inherent in complex, multi-agent environments.
