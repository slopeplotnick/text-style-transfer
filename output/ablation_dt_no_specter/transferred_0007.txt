While model innovation remains active, practical deployments often hinge on choices that are frequently treated as secondary in academic reports: how we define virality, what signals are feasible to collect (textual vs. numeric/metadata), and which evaluation protocols best reflect real-world constraints, such as access limits, time censoring, and class imbalance. This paper adopts an evaluation-first stance on these design decisions. We demonstrate across two recent datasets that label design and input views (text-only vs. text+signals) can rival architectural changes, releasing reproducible baselines that make these trade-offs explicit. Although our focus is on evaluation design rather than model novelty, we show that lightweight architectures can reach or exceed the performance of recent multimodal and graph-based systems on standard benchmarks such as FAKENewsNET. Specifically, we examine two tasks: misinformation ("fake news") detection and early virality prediction. The question becomes: What signals and definitions matter most to achieve stable performance under realistic constraints? To address this, we compare textual representations, such as sentence-level transformer embeddings, with lightweight numeric signals, including user/account statistics, early engagement counts, and timing features. These tasks are evaluated on datasets with distinct structures: one based on self-contained news items, the other on temporally ordered sequences of social media posts. We address two supervised classification tasks across two datasets, yielding four experimental conditions: (i) fake-news detection and (ii) virality prediction on EvONS and FAKENewsNET. For the fake-news detection task, given an item (article or tweet series), we predict a binary veracity label (fake vs. true). This task is run on both EvONS articles and FakeNewsNet tweet series, with results reported using standard classification metrics. In the FAKENewsNET dataset, we use the tweet-series setting built from the repository. The data are anonymized via IDs and require API hydration; obtaining the dataset's Politifact split was possible through prior academic sharing, while GossipCop could not be recovered under current constraints. Each instance corresponds to a series of tweets referring to the same news article, illustrating the propagation of information.
