A different dataset, AI2-THOR, was employed instead of the TEACH dataset used in the benchmarking phase. This methodological transition was necessary as it enabled direct replication for feasibility assessment and provided a common ground for fair algorithmic comparison, while also allowing for indirect performance comparison with tasks of a similar nature. However, when benchmarking LTAA against traditional MRTA approaches, the same dataset was utilized for consistency. Additionally, the authors are conducting evaluations of SMART-LLM using the TEACH dataset to facilitate a more balanced comparison. Secondly, the study was conducted on a relatively small scale, which allowed for a comprehensive examination of fundamental aspects and a thorough analysis of capabilities. Nevertheless, real-world construction tasks involve substantially more complexity, characterized by intricate temporal and spatial challenges not fully captured in the evaluation dataset. To enhance generalizability, researchers opted to utilize abstract features such as elemental/compound and heavy/dexterous to represent construction tasks, rather than focusing on specific tasks. This approach was necessitated by the absence of an extensive database of real-world construction operations. The authors posit that the existence of such a database would facilitate the evaluation of the LTAA framework across a broader spectrum of scenarios involving dynamic replanning and resource conflicts, thereby reinforcing the framework's robustness. Moreover, the inherent variability in LLM outputs raises reliability concerns for safety-critical construction operations. Despite rigorous validation efforts to mitigate potential failures, the stochastic nature of these outputs, while enabling comprehensive reasoning, may result in divergent allocation outcomes. Construction sites requiring high precision may necessitate additional verification measures beyond current practices. Furthermore, regarding computational resource requirements, the stage-specific costs of SMART-LLM could not be directly quantified due to its reliance on a single concatenated prompt encompassing all stages. To facilitate a comparative analysis with LTAA, which focuses exclusively on allocation, an approximation of SMART-LLM's stage-level costs was derived by dividing its total token usage and runtime evenly across the three stages. While this estimation is acceptable, it may not precisely reflect the actual costs associated with allocation, and future research incorporating comprehensive evaluations is necessary to obtain a more accurate understanding.
