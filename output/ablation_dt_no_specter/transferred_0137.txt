Predictions can be modeled using machine learning methods, such as neural networks or generative AI algorithms. Offline and online sequential planning under uncertainty can be effectively addressed using Monte Carlo Tree search algorithms. Having identified these two major components of AI—predicting and deciding—I will review how they are currently applied in exploration. Making predictions requires datasets, and the most important challenge in mineral exploration is the creation and curation of these datasets, rather than the type of machine learning method used. These datasets often present several challenges, as they can be multivariate, three-dimensional, compositional, and extremal. Such challenging prediction or learning problems are rarely encountered in other domains. I will consider two crucial and often overlooked components of data curation: not all data is on the same scale, and not all data should be pooled into a single dataset. Moreover, learning in one area may not apply to another, and upscaling ground truth data, such as core samples, to the geophysical scale can introduce inaccuracies. Additionally, deterministic interpolation of geophysical and geochemical datasets and merging data from different zones without geological explanation can compromise analysis integrity. Applying machine learning models from one area to another without validating their applicability further exacerbates these issues. All these common errors lead to an increase in false positives, giving AI a bad name in the industry. However, the actual problem lies not in AI but in data curation practices. A significant error in the current paradigm, long recognized in mining geostatistics (Journel and Huijbregts, 1978), is the significant variance difference in data at various scales. For example, comparing interpolated geophysical data with outcrop sampling or core measurement shows that deterministic interpolation will inevitably reduce variance, resulting in interpolated geophysics having smaller variance than the actual data. Furthermore, label data from drilling at a centimeter scale is often upscaled—or worse, moved—to enable comparison with geophysical data, artificially increasing the correlation between label and predictor. Consequently, current mineral potential or prospective mapping often contains a significant number of false positives.
