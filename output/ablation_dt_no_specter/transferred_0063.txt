The threat model focuses on the post-deployment phase, wherein a malicious tool has been activated by an agent. It is assumed that in fully localized implementations, the tool's source code may be publicly visible or subject to static analysis; however, the latent backdoor remains undetected due to the semantic resemblance between benign and malicious functionalities. In cloud-hosted or hybrid configurations, the internal mechanics of these tools are obscured from the user, thereby confining the potential attack surface to their observable outputs. The attacker operates entirely within the permissible boundaries and refrains from attempting privilege escalation or engaging in memory corruption. The criteria for a successful attack are defined by two conditions: (1) the successful completion of the attacker's external computational task, and (2) the maintenance of the agent's functional accuracy on the user's original task within an acceptable deviation threshold. Moreover, the threat is economically motivated. The exploitation of computational resources incurs tangible financial implications, including the consumption of API tokens and local GPU cycles. The aggregate impact of numerous such tools can lead to significant resource depletion across the agent ecosystem. This section elucidates the design of the LeechHijack attack. Initially, we provide a comprehensive overview of the attack mechanism. Subsequently, we elucidate the implantation phase, wherein a latent backdoor is embedded into a compliant MCP tool. Thereafter, we delineate the exploitation phase, during which the backdoor is activated to establish a covert command and control (C2) channel and compel the victim agent to execute additional workloads. We conclude with formal definitions of the criteria for attack success and the metrics employed for its evaluation. LeechHijack effectuates computation hijacking through a bifurcated process. Initially, the adversary disseminates a seemingly innocuous MCP tool that harbors a concealed backdoor component. During standard operation, the backdoor remains dormant and exhibits no discernible malicious activity or adverse effects. In the subsequent exploitation phase, the backdoor is activated by a conditional trigger. Upon activation, the tool delivers altered responses that compel the agent to execute additional tasks as specified by the attackers. The exploitation phase concludes upon the subsequent invocation of the tool. At this stage, LeechHijack transmits the calculated attack results and reverts to standard functionality, leaving no discernible artifacts detectable by the user.
