We will begin by discussing the early foundational work in Bayesian and n-gram-based language models, which were instrumental in building probabilistic frameworks for speech recognition tasks. These models laid the groundwork for current systems by effectively modeling word sequences and handling uncertainties inherent in speech inputs. Next, we will explore the rise of advanced end-to-end speech models that integrate contextual information directly into the modeling process. These models capture not only linguistic content but also paralinguistic features such as prosody, emotion, and speaker characteristics, thereby allowing for more robust and nuanced speech understanding and generation. Furthermore, we will examine the role of large language models (LLMs) in post-ASR error correction, where these models are employed to refine and rectify the outputs of traditional ASR systems. Special attention will be given to issues of fairness and bias, particularly concerning speaker variability, including accent, dialect, and sociolect, and their impact on speech processing. We will discuss how recent advancements are addressing these challenges and the ethical implications of ensuring equitable performance across diverse speech populations. Additionally, we will review some theoretical foundations of LLM adaptation and their integration with speech input, encompassing topics such as population risk measurement and model transferability estimation from speech models to LLM adaptation. This exploration motivates distinct design pipelines for spoken agents. Building on this, we will investigate methodologies for joint text-speech pre-training, which have pushed the boundaries of multimodal understanding by combining speech and text learning objectives. Finally, the application of LLMs for voice quality estimation will be discussed, demonstrating how these models can assess and dynamically adapt to different speaker characteristics in real-time.
