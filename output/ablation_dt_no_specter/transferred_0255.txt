In this context, language models surpass traditional algorithmic models due to their superior predictive capabilities and comprehensibility, alongside their proficiency in addressing multiple tasks concurrently. However, the application of large language models (LLMs) faces significant challenges: they are computationally expensive to train and deploy, and they raise privacy concerns in terms of processing sensitive data on remote servers. Their large size also makes them impractical for resource-constrained on-device settings, underscoring the need for more efficient alternatives. By contrast, Small Language Models (SLMs) present a viable alternative to LLMs, designed for operational efficiency through techniques such as quantization, knowledge distillation, and lightweight architectures. SLMs are adept at performing tasks such as text classification, information summarization, and question answering on devices with constrained resources, including mobile phones and embedded systems. Moreover, SLMs require significantly lower computational resources while maintaining competitive performance in specific tasks. Empirical evidence indicates that in data-scarce scenarios, smaller models can achieve comparable or even superior outcomes to their larger counterparts, particularly in specialized domains such as text classification and healthcare applications. This renders SLMs a viable and efficient solution for the identification of mental health conditions from social media data. Previous studies have predominantly concentrated on isolated tasks or domains, without integrating various mental health issues such as depression, stress, and suicidality. In contrast to earlier LLMs, which are often constrained by their extensive parameter requirements, rendering them impractical for deployment in resource-limited settings or where privacy is a concern, SLMs prioritize usability, characterized by rapid response times, reduced memory consumption, and cost-effectiveness. Prior research has undertaken comparative analyses of several SLMs against LLMs across diverse mental health tasks utilizing social media datasets, revealing that few-shot prompting significantly enhances the performance of SLMs. For instance, Kim et al. introduced a specialized language model (SLM) named mhGPT, which demonstrates superior performance on mental health-related tasks compared to larger models such as MentaLLaMA and Gemma, despite utilizing significantly fewer parameters and less training data. Furthermore, recent research highlights the potential of SLMs for application in clinical settings, particularly for structured tasks that extend beyond the mere detection of mental health conditions. However, these studies often exhibit limitations in task balancing and optimization strategies, as they predominantly rely on either minimal prompting or full-scale fine-tuning, thereby neglecting more efficient fine-tuning methodologies.
