The model's training framework includes both single-speaker inputs and multi-user interactions. In this case, the post aggregates interleaved content from multiple speakers without clear boundaries, thereby increasing interpretive complexity and deviating from standard training paradigms. This structural incongruity, combined with the extended input length, can lead the model to disproportionately focus on initial salient elements while neglecting the broader contextual intent due to instability in processing long-context inputs. Nevertheless, through task-specific supervision and systematic fine-tuning, our Menta model demonstrates robust performance across both binary and multi-level mental health classification tasks, such as stress detection, depression severity, and suicidal ideation and risk assessment. It effectively manages a diverse range of mental health outcomes within a cohesive and efficient framework. Moreover, the Menta model performs competitively with significantly larger language models, particularly excelling in depression and stress tasks, where it occasionally surpasses these larger models. These findings underscore the practical applicability of well-optimized smaller language models (SLMs) for continuous mental health monitoring, offering a cost-effective yet high-performing solution for early detection in digital mental health applications. In this section, we address three key questions regarding the use of SLMs and our fine-tuned Menta model in predicting mental health outcomes, while also discussing existing limitations and potential directions for future research. Can SLMs effectively predict mental health status? Our findings indicate that SLMs exhibit substantial proficiency in predicting mental health conditions from text-based social media data. Despite their smaller parameter sizes, the well-trained Menta model successfully manages complex classification tasks, highlighting its potential to deliver accurate predictions in resource-constrained or deployment-limited environments. While a prior study by Jia et al. (2025) explored SLMs in the mental health domain, focusing on models within the 2-7 billion parameter range, our research specifically examines models strictly below 7 billion parameters. Notably, we find that models with fewer than approximately 3.8 billion parameters (e.g., those around 1 billion) exhibit inadequate performance, often generating blank or arbitrary outputs in zero- or few-shot scenarios. This suggests that reducing language models below a certain parameter threshold results in a substantial decline in task performance rather than a gradual degradation, thereby revealing a distinct lower-bound capacity limit. Our study explicitly identifies this threshold within the domain of mental health prediction.
