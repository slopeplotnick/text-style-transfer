Quantitative results are summarized in Table 4, with learning curves provided in Figure 8. While AMP demonstrates strong imitation capabilities, it requires retaining reference motion data throughout the training process. In contrast, SMP effectively reproduces a diverse range of skills, including highly dynamic and challenging motions, achieving imitation accuracy and sample efficiency comparable to AMP, all without relying on reference data during training. Moreover, SMP consistently outperforms SMILING and AMP-Frozen across all evaluated skills. AMP-Frozen, which attempts to eliminate AMP's dependency on reference motion data by substituting a pre-trained discriminator, proves inadequate for motion imitation and results in severely degraded behaviors. DeepMimic, on the other hand, benefits from explicit phase synchronization, which reduces tracking errors for certain motions. However, its performance diminishes on more complex motions, such as spinkicks and cartwheels, particularly when pose termination is disabled, leading some training runs to converge to suboptimal local solutions. In contrast, our framework introduces one of the first approaches to construct a reusable motion prior using score-matching, achieving motion fidelity comparable to state-of-the-art adversarial imitation learning methods. In this section, we highlight the key role of ensemble score-matching in enhancing training stability and improving performance outcomes. Specifically, we compare our ensemble score-matching approach, which averages multiple SDS evaluations over a fixed set of diffusion timesteps, against the conventional method employed in prior work, which relies on a single SDS evaluation at a randomly sampled timestep. This comparison is conducted on single-clip imitation tasks of varying difficulty, including Run, Cartwheel, and Backflip. As shown in Table 5, the single-timestep SDS objective performs adequately on simpler motions but faces significant challenges with more complex behaviors. For example, when imitating a backflip, policies trained with a single SDS evaluation often collapse to stationary postures or fall backward instead of completing the maneuver. In contrast, averaging multiple SDS evaluations across a fixed set of diffusion timesteps provides a more robust and informative reward signal, enabling effective policy training for intricate motions, such as acrobatics.
