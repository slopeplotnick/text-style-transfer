However, these methods exhibit notable deficiencies in two principal aspects. Regarding data types, the majority of studies predominantly rely on numerical time series data, with textual information utilization limited to superficial feature extraction. This limitation hinders the comprehensive integration of diverse information sources, such as market sentiment and policy interpretations. In terms of model mechanisms, while neural networks enhance pattern recognition capabilities, they fall short in comprehending the deeper semantic logic of scenarios. They are unable to establish causal relationships between events and price fluctuations, thereby limiting their efficacy in navigating complex market environments. Nevertheless, the emergence of Large Language Models (LLMs) has introduced a paradigm shift from merely identifying historical patterns to incorporating memory mechanisms. For instance, FinMem implemented a financial memory framework designed to store and retrieve condensed representations of news and events, while MagicNet developed a hierarchical memory architecture to temporally organize market data. However, these methodologies continue to exhibit significant limitations in the design and implementation of their memory systems. Memory units predominantly consist of discrete textual or data fragments without synthesizing them into a comprehensive narrative of event progression. Furthermore, retrieval mechanisms rely on identifying analogous meanings, which poses challenges in addressing more intricate scenarios. Overall, while these methods are transitioning from historical pattern analysis to memory incorporation, substantial advancements remain necessary in the comprehension and application of pertinent information. This paper focuses on elucidating the intricacies of event-driven stock prediction, which endeavors to discern the primary causal factors influencing stock price fluctuations. Prior to the advent of LLMs, this domain progressed from examining the impact of events on stock returns to the automatic extraction of event signals from textual data, initially employing rudimentary models such as Bag-of-Words and topic models, and subsequently advancing to sophisticated deep learning representations like BERT. However, a common fundamental limitation persists across these methods: they essentially remain within a numerically-driven paradigm. Even when event labels or sentiment scores are extracted, they are ultimately transformed into features for temporal models such as LSTM or Transformer. Consequently, these models primarily capture statistical patterns within the data rather than the underlying event logic, leading to a significant loss of critical semantic information and impeding their ability to perform deep reasoning. Significant details are often overlooked during this process, hindering the ability to engage in thorough and reflective analysis.
