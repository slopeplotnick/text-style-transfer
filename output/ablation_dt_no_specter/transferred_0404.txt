Hate speech recognition in low-resource languages remains a challenging issue due to insufficient datasets, orthographic diversity, and linguistic variability. Bangla, spoken by over 230 million people in Bangladesh and the Indian state of West Bengal, is significantly underrepresented in computational resources. Despite the growing demand for automated moderation on social media platforms, Bangla lacks adequate computational support. In this study, we explore Subtask 1A and Subtask 1B of the BLP 2025 Shared Task on hate speech detection. We reproduce the official baselines, including Majority, Random, and Support Vector Machine, and additionally consider Logistic Regression, Random Forest, and Decision Tree as baseline methods. Furthermore, we employ transformer-based models such as DistilBERT, BanglaBERT, m-BERT, and XLM-RoBERTa for hate speech classification. Notably, all transformer-based models outperformed the baseline methods for the subtasks, except for DistilBERT. Among the transformer-based models, BanglaBERT demonstrated the best performance across both subtasks. Despite its relatively smaller size, BanglaBERT surpassed both m-BERT and XLM-RoBERTa, highlighting the importance of language-specific pre-training. Our findings underscore the potential and necessity for pretrained language models tailored to the low-resource Bangla language. Over recent decades, the internet has experienced significant growth in popularity, and social media platforms have seen increased global usage. Improved internet accessibility has facilitated the online expression and dissemination of thoughts and information. The utilization of social media is rising rapidly and shows no signs of slowing down. It has become a significant component of public discourse; however, it also facilitates the dissemination of hatred. The swift proliferation of toxic and harmful content, such as hate speech, raises substantial safety concerns on these online platforms. Hate speech can be characterized as harmful, offensive, or derogatory language directed at individuals or groups. Consequently, the automatic detection of hate speech has emerged as a critical task within the field of Natural Language Processing (NLP). While significant advancements have been made in the detection of hate speech in high-resource languages such as English, progress remains limited for low-resource languages like Bangla. This limitation arises from the scarcity of high-quality annotated datasets and the intricate linguistic characteristics of the language.
