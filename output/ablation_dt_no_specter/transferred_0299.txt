OmniFusion is adapted to leverage lecture slide screenshots during live translation by extracting relevant slides from segmented audio. Moreover, we introduce an open-source image-to-image translation pipeline with modular components, enabling future research on full-image or slide translation and rendering. The inclusion of images is demonstrated to enhance performance in language processing tasks across various language models, illustrating improvements in different language pairs. We evaluate several optical character recognition (OCR) models and assess the translation quality of unimodal and multimodal approaches for image translation. To fully localize lecture content, including audio and slides, across multiple modalities and languages, and to support accessibility tasks such as summarization and question answering, we develop multimodal translation systems. Our approach performs multimodal speech translation and leverages the resulting transcripts for downstream language processing tasks. Additionally, we translate slides by converting text and images into the target language while preserving their layout and visual coherence. To map visual context to each audio segment and improve usability, we built a PDF viewer that displays slides with overlaid captions synchronized to the presenter's selected slide. This interface enables participants to follow translations while viewing slides and allows the system to automatically identify which slide corresponds to each audio segment, providing essential context for multimodal speech translation. In this section, we first describe the multimodal speech translation pipeline, including how slide images are extracted and associated with audio segments. Next, we outline how the resulting translations are used for downstream tasks such as summarization and question answering. Finally, we present the slide-translation process, detailing how text embedded within slide images is detected, translated, and re-rendered. Several speech translation systems support translation across multiple languages; however, they are not directly suitable for live lectures. Most are trained for offline tasks with fixed segmentation, which is incompatible with streaming audio, and require simultaneous translation policies to determine when enough audio has been received. Existing systems are either unimodal, ignoring slides, or multimodal but lack multilingual support. Lecture scenarios demand both multimodality and multilinguality.
