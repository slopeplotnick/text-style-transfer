Despite these evaluation limitations, the replication study confirmed three critical findings: (1) LLM-driven coordination is feasible for multi-robot task allocation, (2) structured validation mechanisms significantly improve reasoning reliability, and (3) hierarchical retry systems effectively handle LLM output inconsistencies. With the feasibility of LLM-MRTA established, the authors developed the LTAA framework to enhance its performance. The LTAA framework was evaluated using the TEACH dataset, which is the standard benchmark for direct comparison with Q-learning, DQN, and DP methodologies in MRTA research. This dataset shift from AI2-THOR household tasks to construction robotics scenarios enables direct performance comparison with established algorithmic baselines under identical evaluation conditions. The LTAA framework specializes in the allocation component from the Self Corrective-Agent Architecture, accepting pre-decomposed tasks, as traditional algorithms do, and focusing exclusively on robot-task assignment decisions. This scope alignment ensures that both LLM-based and algorithm-based approaches are evaluated on equivalent problem formulations. Building on the self-corrective principles established above, the LTAA framework implements a nine-node workflow. Unlike the Self Corrective-Agent Architecture, which handled full-pipeline coordination, the LTAA framework focuses solely on the allocation decision process, accepting pre-decomposed tasks and producing robot-task assignments comparable to traditional algorithmic outputs. The LTAA framework introduces three core technical innovations that enable competitive performance with traditional optimization algorithms while maintaining reasoning transparency: (1) phase-adaptive allocation strategies that dynamically adjust decision priorities throughout project progression, (2) multi-stage validation with hierarchical retry and self-correction mechanisms ensuring LLM reasoning quality, and (3) context-aware reasoning integration that structures allocation decisions with quantitative capability priors. These contributions are implemented through a nine-node LangGraph workflow that processes each task through structured reasoning stages.
