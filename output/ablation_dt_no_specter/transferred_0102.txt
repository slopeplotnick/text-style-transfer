Attempts to bridge this gap by developing static benchmarks of LLM outputs, such as HELM, remain limited, as these datasets cannot evaluate novel or out-of-distribution models, nor can they capture dynamic generative signals. This divergence has led to the emergence of two parallel research trajectories, resulting in redundant efforts and leaving fundamental questions unanswered. It remains unclear whether one paradigm is inherently superior, how their respective strengths differ, or whether they can be effectively integrated. Furthermore, the continued separation of these paradigms prevents the exploitation of their complementary attributes: HD's internal model signals could enhance FV's evidence-based reasoning, while FV's contextual grounding could support HD's uncertainty signals. To address this longstanding divide, we take a decisive step forward. We present the first large-scale, systematic empirical study to directly compare Hallucination Detection and Fact Verification within a unified framework. Far from being a mere benchmarking exercise, this work provides a comprehensive investigation into the intrinsic connections between these two approaches. For the first time, this unified framework enables us to tackle three pivotal research questions (RQs) that have remained unexplored due to the historical separation of the fields: (1) Overall Performance (RQ1): How do Hallucination Detection and Fact Verification methods compare in identifying factual inaccuracies within the same LLM outputs? (2) Complementary Strengths (RQ2): Do HD and FV focus on distinct types of errors, thereby demonstrating complementary strengths, or do they exhibit overlapping failure patterns? (3) Hybrid Performance (RQ3): Can the signals from both paradigms be effectively combined to create a hybrid system that outperforms each individual approach? Previously, such a comparison was deemed unfeasible due to the fundamental incompatibility between the two paradigms. However, our work resolves this challenge with UniFact, a unified evaluation framework specifically designed to overcome this divide. Unlike static benchmarks that rely on pre-generated outputs from LLMs, UniFact employs a dynamic evaluation process. It prompts any LLM to generate responses to factual inquiries in real time. Subsequently, an automated labeling system determines the accuracy of these responses by comparing them against ground-truth answers and pre-labeled reference knowledge. This dynamic configuration is pivotal, as it simultaneously produces the textual outputs required for FV methods and the internal model signals essential for HD methods. This design enables a direct, instance-level comparison of both paradigms on identical content.
