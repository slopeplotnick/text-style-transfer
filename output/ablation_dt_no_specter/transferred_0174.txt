Improving the ability of machines to process high-resolution images has become an emerging research focus. A common "zoom-in" strategy is widely adopted to enhance detail perception in models. Although training-based methods such as Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) and Reinforcement Learning (RL) can effectively identify relevant regions, they are hampered by critical limitations, including high computational costs, long training cycles, and poor cross-architecture transferability, which curtails their scalability and practical application. In contrast, training-free methodologies leverage attention mechanisms or hierarchical tree-based search strategies to autonomously identify salient regions within an image, without requiring additional training. Despite employing a top-down search strategy from high to low resolution, these methods face significant limitations. A primary issue is the model's inadequate perception of small-scale features during the initial search stage, which frequently leads to suboptimal search paths and erroneous localization outcomes. Recently, inspired by the success of Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) for enabling long-context understanding in general LLMs, a novel paradigm termed Retrieval-Augmented Perception (RAP) has been proposed. RAP aims to enhance the capacity of models to process high-resolution images without necessitating additional training, thereby offering a promising direction for improving detail perception in machine learning systems. The RAP framework consists of three key components: First, the Visual Retrieval module utilizes a pre-trained vision RAG model to ascertain semantic similarity between the query and various image regions, thereby retrieving the most pertinent segments to mitigate extraneous information. Next, the Spatial-Awareness Layout module preserves the intrinsic spatial relationships of the retrieved segments, ensuring spatial coherence in the model input. Finally, the Adaptive Retrieval-Exploration Search (RE-Search) module dynamically determines the optimal quantity of retrieved segments by constructing a Retrieval-Exploration Tree (RE-Tree), thereby achieving a balance between information sufficiency and computational efficiency.
