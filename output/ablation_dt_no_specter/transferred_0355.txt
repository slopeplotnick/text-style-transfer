We conducted extensive experiments on a diverse set of benchmarks, including machine translation evaluation (WMT23) (Song et al., 2025) and reward model assessment (Reward Bench 2) (Malik et al., 2025). Our findings indicate that our distribution-calibrated approach significantly surpasses conventional self-consistency methodologies. By meticulously analyzing the entire vote distribution, our method transforms disordered individual model judgments into more reliable ratings, achieving performance comparable to or exceeding that of human raters when assessed against a human-consensus gold standard. Our contributions are threefold: First, we identified that conventional aggregation methods employed for combining outputs from LLM judges during inference are suboptimal, and that the development of a more robust aggregation strategy is essential. Second, we introduced a novel aggregation framework based on Expected Risk Minimization (ERM) utilizing a Bradley-Terry-Davidson model, calibrated on a small validation dataset. This approach demonstrated consistent superiority over existing aggregation methods across a range of tasks, including reward benchmarks and machine translation evaluation. Third, in the realm of machine translation evaluation, we employed a consensus-based methodology to enhance the reliability of ground truth labels amidst noisy data conditions. This approach facilitated a fair comparison with human raters, demonstrating that large language model (LLM) judges can occasionally surpass human performance. Recently, the utilization of LLMs as evaluative agents for generated text has proven highly effective, providing a scalable alternative to traditional quality assessment metrics. These models have demonstrated a commendable alignment with human judgments across various domains. Certain methodologies employ general-purpose LLMs directly, exemplified by G-Eval or JudgeLM, whereas others involve the modification of models tailored specifically for evaluative tasks, such as Prometheus or Auto-J. Despite the robustness of these approaches, they encounter challenges, including sensitivity to prompt design and inherent biases, such as positional bias, which favors specific candidate orders, or morbidity bias, which prefers longer outputs. Moreover, the decision-making processes of LLM judges exhibit variability, with certain models demonstrating greater stringency in distinguishing subtle differences or resolving ties. Therefore, this study aims to attenuate such variability and enhance the consistency of judgments through the implementation of a principled aggregation methodology.
