Multi-agent autonomy emerges through the integration of large language models with orchestration layers, shared memories, and external tools. Frameworks such as AutoGen [11] and CAMEL [7] establish role-based dialogues—planner, critic, executor—within a unified environment. More recent systems, including SWE-agent [12] and Voyager [10], advance this paradigm by incorporating continuous learning loops where agents autonomously generate tasks, evaluate outcomes, and iteratively refine their strategies. Consequently, the process becomes an adaptive ecology rather than a static sequence: each model's output modifies the context for subsequent models, generating feedback that accumulates over time. Although humans remain nominally "in the loop," their authority is deferred, indirect, and constrained. Traditional safety evaluations presuppose that behavior is both observable and interpretable; however, in extensive multi-agent systems, these assumptions do not hold. Communication frequently lacks structure, manifesting as free-form natural language exchanges, JSON payloads, or API calls devoid of semantic clarity. When meaning becomes endogenous to the network, traceability diminishes not due to the absence of data, but owing to the instability of interpretation. Therefore, technical interventions endeavor to address this deficiency in visibility. Multi-agent sandboxes now log interaction graphs and compute metrics such as divergence, semantic coherence, influence centrality, and consensus rate to characterize system-level behavior. The Inspect framework of the UK AI Security Institute adopts a similar methodology by incorporating provenance metadata into each message, thereby enabling the reconstruction of causal chains post hoc. These developments signify a paradigm shift from merely evaluating outputs to analyzing the propagation of information through the network. As systems expand in scale, the conceptualization of autonomy undergoes a transformation. In traditional supervised frameworks, autonomy was defined as the capacity to operate independently of direct human directives. However, within multi-agent systems, autonomy is characterized by relational dynamics: agents engage in coordination, competition, and inference of each other's intentions. Even when agents are initialized with identical objectives, minor divergences in reward structures or contextual parameters can precipitate polarization, collusion, or unchecked escalation. Thus, autonomy ceases to be an intrinsic attribute of individual components and instead emerges as a systemic property of the network as a whole.
