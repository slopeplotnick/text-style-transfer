The fine-tuned model Menta demonstrates superior performance in detecting depression and stress compared to suicidal risk. Similarly, both fine-tuned and standard language models, including even larger ones, tend to show enhanced effectiveness in these tasks relative to suicide risk classification. For instance, a systematic review by Bauer et al. (2024) highlights that language models frequently misclassify nuanced expressions of suicidality due to issues like class imbalance and a reliance on contextual or temporal factors, which are not adequately captured in isolated posts. These findings are consistent with Lamichhane (2023), who reported an F1-score of 0.86 for depression detection and a markedly lower score of 0.37 for suicidality detection based on social media datasets. These outcomes indicate that depression and stress often manifest with clearer linguistic patterns and a richer dataset, whereas suicidality is rarer, more context-dependent, and linguistically subtler, making it more challenging for models to interpret reliably. Consequently, language models may exhibit superior efficacy in general screening tasks, such as identifying depression or stress. However, their capability for the nuanced stratification of suicide risk is constrained unless supplemented with multimodal data or temporal tracking. Despite these insights, several limitations were identified in our study. Although models like Qwen-3 (4B) show proficiency in predicting mental health concerns, factors such as the sole reliance on textual social media posts limit our findings. This approach excludes multimodal features such as temporal variations in writing patterns, user profile information, and online interaction behaviors, which are crucial for a comprehensive understanding of mental health phenomena. Therefore, incorporating these elements could enhance the robustness and ecological validity of future research efforts. Additionally, our study's narrow focus on depression, stress, and suicidal ideation, representing a limited subset of mental health disorders, restricts its breadth. Real-world mental health challenges encompass a broader spectrum, including anxiety, bipolar disorder, and emotional disturbances, which were not addressed in this investigation. Moreover, our study utilized publicly accessible, anonymized social media data instead of data from actual clinical environments. While this ensures privacy and reproducibility, it limits our ability to draw conclusions regarding clinical safety, ethical compliance, and longitudinal reliability in genuine healthcare settings.
