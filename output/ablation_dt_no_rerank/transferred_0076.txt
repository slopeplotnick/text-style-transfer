Empirical observations suggest that predictions often stabilize well before reaching the final diffusion step (Pengxiang et al., 2025). To manage the balance between exploration and acceleration across the chain, dynamic policies are utilized. These policies leverage signals like historical logits or local determinism to diminish redundant steps (Wei et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025b; Or-El et al., 2025). Meanwhile, other research avenues concentrate on enhancing error correction and refinement by allowing models to autonomously revise their outputs through re-masking of low-confidence tokens (Zhang et al., 2025a; Su et al., 2025). Furthermore, expedited methods tackle this challenge by incorporating concepts from continuous processes, such as phased learning, to uphold consistency and align with flow dynamics (Sahoo et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025; Meister et al., 2024). In parallel, acceleration-focused strategies employ data caching for prompt retrieval and implement mechanisms for concurrent verification and validation (Yuan et al., 2025b; Wang et al., 2025a; Sohoni et al., 2025; Yuan et al., 2025a). However, despite their effectiveness, these strategies often require additional training, novel models, or complex heuristics. This highlights the need for a straightforward approach to determine termination criteria without extra training or specific configurations. Thus, we explore the decision-making process pertinent to determining cessation points and introduce SchED, a schedule-based method for early termination that bypasses additional training and specific model requirements. Essentially, the method involves assessing the confidence for each output component and comparing it to a dynamically adjustable threshold that becomes increasingly lenient throughout the sequence. By separating confidence assessment from iterative progression and gradually altering the threshold, predictions can be halted as soon as stabilization is achieved, preventing abrupt changes or fixed constraints. SchED integrates seamlessly with conventional transfer schedules without necessitating modifications to the model training process (Nie et al., 2025; Ye et al., 2025).
