To address this pivotal issue, the research community has developed two primary approaches: Fact Verification (FV) and Hallucination Detection (HD). Fact Verification is a well-established, text-centric research area focused on assessing the factual accuracy of statements using external knowledge sources such as web documents or databases. A critical aspect of FV is its origin-agnostic nature, as it evaluates the veracity of statements regardless of whether they are human-authored or machine-generated by examining the evidence procured. Conversely, Hallucination Detection has emerged more recently alongside the rise of large language models (LLMs). Unlike FV, which concentrates on evaluating text in isolation, HD specifically targets content generated by LLMs and incorporates the generative process itself. Although HD can use external information similar to FV, its main concentration is on intrinsic cues from the model, such as token-level uncertainty, activation dynamics, and consistency across sampled generations. Therefore, HD is inherently model-centric, enabling it to identify fabricated content even without external validation. While both HD and FV share the goal of identifying factual inaccuracies, they have evolved separately, as evidenced by their distinct evaluation metrics, methodologies for assessing efficacy, and scholarly dissemination venues. This divergence stems from the fundamentally different evaluation methodologies employed by each paradigm. Specifically, FV is inherently text-centric, with benchmarks like FEVER assessing claims as independent textual entities.
