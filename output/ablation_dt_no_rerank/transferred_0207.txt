In today's fast-paced world of Natural Language Processing (NLP), large language models (LLMs) like GPT and BERT have emerged as the primary drivers of innovation. These models can generate human-like text, adeptly respond to intricate queries, and engage fluently in conversations. If a method delivers an output that intuitively makes sense, it can be likened to heading north on a compass. However, while an answer produced by LLMs may logically relate to the input, it lacks empirical truth as it is not rooted in factual knowledge, rendering it a type of mirage that defies verification from prior understanding. This phenomenon, known as hallucination, represents the primary barrier to the widespread adoption of LLMs. Imagine an experienced storyteller who, while aiming to convey truth, occasionally embellishes their narrative with unreal details. Similarly, LLMs can produce content that appears accurate but lacks a foundation in their training data. A simpler analogy is a GPS that confidently offers directions yet sometimes leads you to nonexistent roads. More technically, hallucinations occur when the generated content fails to align with factual data or expected outcomes based on training corpus. This might involve false statements, fabricated references, or flawed logic pathways. Like the errant GPS, these models appear trustworthy yet arrive at incorrect conclusions. This challenge is actively being tackled by the AI community. Even the most sophisticated models are not immune to hallucinations. Despite their remarkable capabilities, LLMs are not always dependable, particularly in generating factual information, which is crucial in high-stakes fields like healthcare, law, and academia.
