Training effective reward models is challenging because they are subjective and context-dependent, making it difficult to develop robust reward models. Moreover, learned proxies are susceptible to reward hacking, further complicating the process. The scarcity of annotations also limits the ability to fine-tune the model for capturing nuanced behaviors. Consequently, these obstacles have prompted efforts to reduce dependency on annotations. Direct Preference Optimization circumvents explicit reward modeling; however, it still necessitates preference datasets. Furthermore, verifiable rewards rely on ground-truth outcomes but are only applicable where automatic verification is feasible. Self-evaluation approaches also face difficulties due to prompt sensitivity and inherent biases. Importantly, while these methodologies continue to evaluate quality through external signals, they often overlook the information contained within a model's internal representations. Ideally, the validity of a generation should be mirrored in its computational processes. For instance, when a model states, "The capital of France is Paris," its hidden states encode activated factual knowledge; yet, when it generates incorrect information, different patterns emerge. This leads to a critical question: Can we assess text quality directly through internal signals, thus enabling LLM alignment without external supervision? We demonstrate that a simple geometric property of LLM hidden states, called effective dimensionality, reliably indicates LLM response quality. We advocate using stable rank, a matrix-theoretic measure, as an unsupervised proxy for generation quality. Stable rank evaluates the degree of independent semantic directions utilized in a response by assessing the ratio of total variance to dominant-direction variance within the hidden-state representation. It balances representational richness with coherence: a high stable rank indicates information dispersion across multiple dimensions rather than concentration in a few. The theoretical foundation of this concept is supported by two lines of research. Initially, softmax bottleneck analysis suggests that precise language modeling requires traversing a high-dimensional semantic space due to the complex contextual probability distribution inherent in natural language.
