While enhanced taxonomic frameworks recognize that practical deployment scenarios involve robots with specialized capabilities executing complex tasks, construction environments present unique MRTA challenges. These challenges set construction apart from controlled settings where traditional approaches have flourished. Construction sites entail unstructured layouts, dynamic material flows, weather dependencies, and complex interdependencies between trades. Moreover, these environments demand allocation frameworks adept at handling spatial constraints, temporal dependencies, and safety-critical operations. Furthermore, construction requires human oversight mechanisms that cannot be predetermined or readily encoded into mathematical formulations. Therefore, these construction-specific challenges necessitate adaptive coordination approaches capable of managing dynamic constraints and unstructured environments effectively. Traditional optimization methods have sought to address these MRTA challenges through various algorithmic strategies. Classical optimization techniques form the backbone of established multi-robot coordination approaches, delivering mathematical rigor and performance guarantees through diverse computational methods. Kuhn (1955) laid the theoretical foundation for optimal assignment problems with the seminal Hungarian method, which efficiently addressed bipartite matching tasks to resources via linear programming with polynomial time complexity guarantees. However, the Hungarian method's scope was limited to simple one-to-one assignments, insufficient for managing heterogeneous robot capabilities or complex task dependencies. Within construction environments, robots exhibit specialized capabilities, such as heavy lifting or precision assembly, and workflows demand coordinated task sequences with explicit dependencies. Hence, these limitations sparked the development of more sophisticated optimization formulations. Atay and Bayazit (2006) pioneered mixed-integer linear programming (MILP) formulations for multi-robot task allocation, incorporating constraints related to robot capabilities, task precedence, and communication limitations. Furthermore, military and aerospace applications have refined MILP-based coordination through sophisticated methodologies addressing heterogeneous capabilities and timing constraints. MILP offered notable advantages in modeling complex multi-robot scenarios with explicit constraint satisfaction. Nevertheless, MILP approaches rely on centralized optimization architectures, encountering scalability challenges when coordinating large robot teams. Given that construction projects involve numerous robots distributed across vast, unstructured sites with limited communication infrastructure, centralized coordination becomes impractical. These scalability limitations have prompted the development of distributed coordination strategies.
