Recent datasets have significantly advanced the field, starting with Mozilla's Common Voice, which offers crowd-sourced, scripted speech in six South African languages under a permissive CC-0 license, encouraging broad reuse. Meanwhile, FLEURS provides approximately 12 hours of data per language in four South African languages, primarily intended for benchmarking and evaluation. On another front, Vuk'uzenzele (VixSD), a community-led ASR initiative, is released under the Esethu licensing framework, stressing community ownership and ethical commercialization. Although smaller in scale, it exemplifies the value of local participation in resource creation. Moreover, AfriSpeech-200 includes English speech recordings featuring South African accents, focusing on healthcare topics, which are beneficial for accent-aware ASR modeling. However, despite these resources, there remains a scarcity of spontaneous speech data and focused domain coverage. This points to the necessity for more diverse datasets like Swivuriso, which integrate both scripted and unscripted speech, offer broader domain representation, and enhance speaker diversity. Furthermore, collecting speech data in underrepresented language communities necessitates sensitivity regarding consent, privacy, cultural norms, and long-term impact. Previous efforts, such as Common Voice and ALFFA, have underscored the need to integrate ethical and participatory practices throughout the data lifecycle. A recent case study on the Catalan edition of Common Voice highlights the importance of clear consent procedures, privacy safeguards, and inclusive participation. Similarly, the ALFFA project emphasized community involvement by collaborating with local experts and field linguists during data collection. Swivuriso builds upon these foundational efforts by spotlighting ethical practices and inclusive representation. Local coordinators played a pivotal role in managing recordings, providing participant support, and ensuring comprehensive community representation. The initiative adhered to institutionally approved ethical protocols, including metadata anonymization, removal of personal identifiers, and implementation of protective measures against misuse such as voice cloning and biometric profiling. Accessibility and licensing are vital to ensuring datasets are broadly usable, particularly for low-resource languages. Open licenses, such as Creative Commons, have been effective in enabling extensive reuse, especially when data collection poses challenges or is financially prohibitive. Projects like Common Voice exemplify how permissive licensing can foster equitable access and accelerate language technology development for languages with limited support.
