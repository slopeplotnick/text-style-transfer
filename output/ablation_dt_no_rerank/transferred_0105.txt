Psychometric scales and questionnaires are crucial tools of quantitative research in various fields, such as psychology, medicine and health sciences, social sciences, education, and human-computer interaction studies. After a novel construct or theoretical framework is proposed and becomes the subject of empirical research, the development of corresponding psychometric tools typically becomes a necessity. However, this process is resource-intensive, requiring careful planning, time, materials, and substantial funding (Stefana et al., 2025). This article examines the potential for simplifying the scale development process by employing in silico simulations using data generated by Large Language Models (LLMs). First, we elaborate on the theoretical rationale and discuss four studies designed to empirically test this method's feasibility. Finally, we will discuss our results and provide recommendations for future research endeavors. Obtaining a representative sample is costly and time-consuming, often leading researchers to rely on convenience samples of university students, a practice that significantly limits the external validity of findings (Hanel & Vione, 2016; Wild et al., 2022). Recently, participant recruitment for validation studies has increasingly utilized online platforms such as Prolific.co and Amazon MTurk (Buhrmester et al., 2018; Douglas et al., 2023; Huang et al., 2015). These platforms, depending on available funds for participant payments, have markedly reduced time expenses and may enhance external validity by enabling the recruitment of representative samples for certain populations, notably from the UK and US. Nonetheless, ethical concerns persist regarding the participation of individuals from disadvantaged backgrounds (Huang et al., 2015; Huff & Tingley, 2015; Moss et al., 2023). When researchers explore constructs for which no validated measurement scales exist or when conducting a validation study is impractical, they often resort to developing ad hoc questionnaires grounded solely in theoretical constructs. While this approach may be cost-effective and time-efficient, it risks compromising the findings' external validity, as they may not generalize beyond the specific sample studied (Lee et al., 2020).
