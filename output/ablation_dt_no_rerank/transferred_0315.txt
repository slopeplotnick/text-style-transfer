Legal and ethical concerns surrounding generative models are increasing, particularly regarding the use of copyrighted content in model training. At the heart of these concerns lies the question of authorship and moral rights. The right to be recognized as the author of one's work, often described as "the most moral of rights," is central to ongoing debates. Consequently, a number of lawsuits argue that using copyrighted content without consent violates intellectual property rights. For example, the New York Times has filed suit against OpenAI for the unauthorized use of its journalistic archives, while Getty Images has taken legal action against Stability AI for generating content derived from its proprietary database. Beyond these legal challenges, artists and performers have expressed apprehensions. Notably, Elton John and Dua Lipa, along with 200 other artists, signed an open letter urging tech companies to safeguard human creativity from AI tools that could mimic their voices and styles without consent. In response to such concerns, policy bodies and international organizations have proposed new governance frameworks. The European Union's AI Act outlines rules for transparency in AI systems, while the UK's National AI Strategy and UNESCO's ethical guidelines advocate for responsible AI, grounded in privacy and property protections. Denmark has recently proposed an amendment to its copyright law that seeks to grant individuals legal rights over their voice, face, and bodily likeness. Described as the first initiative of its kind in Europe, this amendment aims to address legal gaps concerning AI-generated imitations of personal characteristics. As the Danish culture minister noted, it ensures "the right to their own body, voice, and facial features," underscoring the necessity for identity protections in the context of generative AI. However, despite these legislative efforts, most generative models continue to function as black boxes, limiting visibility into how training data influence their outputs. Even open-source models, while accessible, are too large to replicate precisely. Current methods, such as influence functions or retraining-based attribution, require access to the internal mechanisms of these models.
