Despite the development of optimization techniques, traditional methods consistently exhibit fundamental limitations when applied to construction coordination. The demand for a comprehensive problem specification at the planning stage is at odds with the dynamic nature of construction projects, where task requirements, environmental conditions, and resource availability fluctuate throughout execution. Additionally, computational complexity often becomes prohibitive for large-scale projects that involve many robots and complex task dependencies. These enduring limitations have driven researchers to explore learning-based coordination approaches capable of adapting to dynamic environments without the need for complete problem specification. Reinforcement Learning (RL) has emerged as a transformative approach to multi-robot task allocation, with the ability to learn optimal coordination policies through environmental interaction without requiring full problem specification. RL frameworks empower robots to develop coordination skills through trial-and-error learning, allowing them to adapt to environmental changes and evolving task requirements. However, RL necessitates extensive training through repeated environmental interaction and trial-and-error exploration. In construction environments, this exploration process poses significant safety risks, where coordination errors could jeopardize workers or damage equipment. Furthermore, the lengthy period required for training coordination policies is impractical for construction projects with tight schedules. Consequently, these safety and efficiency concerns inspired construction-specific RL approaches. Lee et al. (2022) developed a digital twin-driven Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) method specifically for adaptive task allocation in robotic construction. This approach integrates Building Information Modeling (BIM) with DRL to make context-aware coordination decisions that incorporate spatial constraints, temporal dependencies, and safety requirements. The digital twin framework enables safer policy learning in simulation before real-world deployment. Nevertheless, RL’s exploration requirements still conflict with construction safety needs, as even simulation-trained policies can lead to hazardous coordination failures during real-world implementation. Moreover, transferring learned policies from simulations to actual construction sites with differing environmental conditions remains problematic, necessitating advanced RL techniques to address these sim-to-real transfer challenges.
