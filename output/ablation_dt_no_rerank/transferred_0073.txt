This paper examines why safety mechanisms initially designed for human-model interactions do not scale to environments where large language models (LLMs) interact with each other. Most current governance practices concentrate on single-agent safety measures, including prompts, fine-tuning, and moderation layers, which constrain individual model behavior but leave multi-model interactions ungoverned. These mechanisms assume a setting where a single model responds to an individual user under consistent oversight. However, both academic research and industry advancements are rapidly moving toward LLM-to-LLM ecosystems, where outputs are recursively utilized as inputs across chains of agents. In such systems, local compliance can aggregate into collective failure even when each model is individually aligned. Therefore, we propose a conceptual transition from model-level safety to system-level safety, introducing the framework of the Emergent Systemic Risk Horizon (ESRH) to formalize how instability arises from the interaction structure rather than isolated misbehavior. The paper contributes (i) a theoretical account of collective risk in interacting LLMs, (ii) a taxonomy connecting micro, meso, and macro-level failure modes, and (iii) a design proposal for Institutional AI, an architecture for embedding adaptive oversight within multi-agent systems. Current safety approaches presuppose a single model operating under direct human supervision, wherein undesired behavior can be curtailed through prompt adjustments, reinforcement learning from human feedback, or output moderation. While these techniques have improved model reliability and decorum, they remain pointwise: governing how one instance responds to a solitary input, rather than coordinating interactions among multiple models. As large models are increasingly deployed in tool-using, agentic, and multi-agent settings, the single-agent safety paradigm begins to deteriorate. Models that are individually well-aligned can collectively generate outcomes that no single instance was designed to prevent, through feedback amplification, imitation, or emergent coordination. Consequently, a gap emerges between local alignment and systemic safety, where ensuring compliance at the component level does not necessarily guarantee control at the network level.
