While innovation in model design continues, the practical deployment often relies on considerations that are frequently regarded as secondary in academic discussions: defining virality, determining feasible data signals (whether textual or numeric/metadata), and selecting evaluation protocols that accurately mirror real-world constraints such as access limitations, temporal censorship, and class imbalance. In this paper, we adopt an evaluation-first perspective on these critical design decisions. Our contribution focuses on demonstrating, through two recent datasets, that both label design and the choice of input data views (text-only versus text augmented with signals) can rival the impact of architectural changes. We release reproducible baselines to make these trade-offs clear. Although our focus is on evaluation methodology rather than novel model development, we show that streamlined architectures can match or surpass the performance of recent multimodal and graph-based systems on standard benchmarks like FAKENewsNET (refer to Section 5). Specifically, we explore two essential tasks: the detection of misinformation ("fake news") and the prediction of early virality. Thus, we investigate which signals and definitions are most crucial for achieving stable performance under realistic conditions. We compare textual representations, such as sentence-level transformer embeddings, with lightweight numeric signals like user statistics, early engagement metrics, and timing features. The two tasks are evaluated on datasets with distinct structures: one consisting of self-contained news items and the other comprising temporally ordered sequences of social media posts. We address two supervised classification tasks across these datasets, resulting in four experimental conditions: fake-news detection and virality prediction on EvONS (Krstovski et al., 2022) and FAKENewsNET (Shu et al., 2020). For fake-news detection, the task involves predicting a binary veracity label (fake versus true) for an item, whether an article or a series of tweets. This task is conducted on both EvONS articles and FakeNewsNet tweet series, with results reported using standard classification metrics. Specifically for FAKENewsNET, we use the tweet-series format derived from the repository. The data are anonymized via IDs and require API hydration; the Politifact split was accessible through previous academic sharing, while GossipCop remains unavailable under current constraints. Each instance corresponds to a series of tweets related to the same news article, illustrating its propagation.
