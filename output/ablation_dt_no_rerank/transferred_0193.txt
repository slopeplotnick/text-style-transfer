This issue cannot be observed in evaluations of individual models, as it arises from the structure and dynamics of interactions, consequently reducing safety, reliability, or accountability at the system level. We propose three preliminary dimensions to elucidate where this problem may manifest, acknowledging that further dimensions may warrant consideration as testing progresses. Firstly, interaction topology focuses on the structure of communication and influence among agents. Secondly, cognitive opacity denotes the degree to which reasoning and message semantics become inaccessible or uninterpretable. Lastly, objective divergence addresses the extent to which local optimization goals begin to drift apart across agents. Collectively, these dimensions help predict the likelihood and form of emergent collective risks. Each dimension can be operationalized through measurable indicators and scrutinized in controlled multi-agent environments. The topology of information flow is crucial; highly interconnected systems promote rapid coordination yet enable swift error contagion. Conversely, sparsely connected systems impede error propagation while also hindering collective correction. Therefore, topology predominantly determines how a single deviation, such as a biased summary, can transform into a shared belief. Metrics like contagion velocity and misalignment diffusion are essential for capturing these dynamics. Hence, benchmarks for multi-agent safety should include topology-sensitive evaluations. Moreover, cognitive opacity can arise unintentionally or strategically, masking agents' internal reasoning processes. In such contexts, uncritical acceptance of explanations by one model from another reinforces misleading rationales. The intent-opacity rate, indicating the fraction of outputs with untraceable rationales, serves as a preliminary measure for this transparency loss.
