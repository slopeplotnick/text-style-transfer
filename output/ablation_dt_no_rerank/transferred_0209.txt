In all three models, the total causal influence of the attribute 'young' on both the image and the predictive outcome remains consistent, illustrating its reliability as a measure independent of abstraction. However, when an explanation model disregards the causal architecture by treating variables as independent, it captures only the direct effect, which may coincide with the total effect in the first model but becomes null in the final model. Thus, direct influence is not a consistent measure across all models. Section 5.3.1 addresses the challenges faced when model assumptions are violated, thereby suggesting that model choices should be made carefully. Whenever biases are introduced by assumptions in explanations, the model should either be modified or the explainer should be informed about the biased context within which the explanations remain valid. Importantly, this is not to suggest that more complex models with fewer assumptions are always preferable to simpler models. To ensure that explanations align with their intended interpretation, the causal model must reflect the level of abstraction consistent with the explainee's understanding of the world. Therefore, prioritizing comprehensibility necessitates omitting certain detailed singular causal mechanisms, condensing pertinent information into a more accessible format. Moreover, a notable advantage of the causal explanation model discussed in Section 5.3.1 is its reliance on expert knowledge, which enhances its ability to identify and eliminate spurious correlations compared to data-driven models. Thus, the explanation model can identify spurious relationships learned by the predictor, even when using the same dataset for its derivation. With expert knowledge becoming more accessible through LLMs, as demonstrated by the illustrative example provided, this feature is advantageous for improving models by detecting unintended behaviors.
