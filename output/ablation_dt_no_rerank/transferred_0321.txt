To adhere to the 512-token limit, each article is split into multiple chunks, ensuring that every chunk combined with the summary fits within this boundary. The summary is then compared against each chunk, and the overall SummaC score is derived by averaging their entailment scores, yielding a value between [0,1]. Using heatmaps and conducting a one-way ANOVA, we investigated whether the positioning of input significantly influences the summaries generated. Figure 3 displays the average values of three evaluation metrics across six distinct input orders and three stance categories. The mean for each condition is calculated by averaging the metric scores across all article triplets with identical input orders and stances. The differences in shading and evaluation scores provide empirical evidence that input positioning affects the similarity patterns seen in language model-generated summaries. Notably, summaries show higher ROUGE-L and BERTScore values when compared to the document appearing first in the sequence. Indeed, BERTScore values surpass those associated with Positions 2 and 3, indicating that documents presented first have a greater influence on the generated summaries. In addressing RQ1, our results consistently show that the generated summaries exhibit a closer resemblance to the first article in the input sequence. However, the absolute metric values diverge from those commonly reported in previous summarization research. This is due to our methodological approach, which contrasts past studies by directly comparing the generated summary with the full original articles rather than human-written reference summaries. As the model-produced summaries average only 220 words while the source articles range from 300 to 1600 words, this substantial length mismatch naturally reduces token overlap and embedding-based similarity scores. Therefore, we focus on the relative scores across different input conditions instead of the absolute values. Additionally, a methodological limitation exists due to the scarcity of metrics designed to evaluate the similarity between a summary and its source documents, as most frameworks are reference-based, posing challenges for source alignment assessment. SummaC, capturing sentence-level entailment, yields low scores and shows minimal sensitivity to positional variation. Since abstract summaries often omit specific details from the source content, entailment-based scoring mechanisms may penalize unsupported or inferred statements. Consequently, even if the model favors information from the first article, it is unlikely to replicate its surface forms verbatim, leading SummaC to overlook nuanced content biases unless explicit supporting sentences are preserved.
