Language Model Systems (LMS), which include Large Language Models (LLMs) [Brown et al. 2020; Gemini Team 2024; Liu et al. 2023, 2024; Touvron et al. 2023] and Visual Language Models (VLMs) [Liu et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2024; Yu et al. 2023], as well as agentic systems, demonstrate versatile applicability across a range of domains. Examples include domain-specific chatbots like medical chatbots, autonomous vehicle navigation assistants, and coding assistance tools [Liang and Tong 2025; Zhang et al. 2024; Zheng et al. 2024]. Because these systems are integrated into safety-critical [Nie et al. 2024; Wang et al. 2025b] and user-facing applications, ensuring their correctness and alignment with human standards of appropriate behavior is crucial. Indeed, numerous existing studies [Anwar et al. 2024; Huang et al. 2023] have explored the integration of desirable safety properties within LMS. However, the current methodologies for both evaluating and enforcing desirable behaviors are insufficient, as they fail to offer generalizable methods with formal guarantees of effectiveness. Formal assurances are essential to fostering user safety and trust [Russell et al. 2016; Singh and Chawla 2025; United Nations Scientific Advisory Board 2025]. Focusing on LMS safety evaluation, recent academic efforts have pioneered approaches to formally certify LMS for specific desirable behaviors such as fairness, correct question answering, and safety, encoded as mathematical specifications. These specifications effectively constrain LMS input scenarios, ensuring outputs meet desirable behavior criteria. For instance, [Chaudhary et al. 2024] have introduced specifications for counterfactual fairness in LLM responses, while [Chaudhary et al. 2025] provide specifications for reading comprehension in general knowledge and medical domains. Moreover, [Wang et al. 2025a] specify protocols for managing catastrophic risk in multi-turn LLM conversations, and [Yeon et al. 2025] outline specifications for precise tool selection by agentic systems. Nevertheless, these tailor-made certification methods pose challenges for adaptation to diverse and domain-specific desirable properties for LLMs. This is primarily because their specification of desirable behaviors is deeply influenced by the property of interest, often described informally or algorithmically, with a predominant focus on conversational settings that lack clear extensions to other modalities. Consequently, even minor specification changes demand substantial reformulation. Therefore, current formal certification approaches in LMS are bespoke for each property, domain, and LMS type, a methodology we aim to refine.
