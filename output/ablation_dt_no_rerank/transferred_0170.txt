However, these methods present substantial challenges in two primary domains. Firstly, regarding data types, most studies predominantly rely on quantitative time series, with textual information utilization limited to superficial feature extraction. This limitation makes it difficult to fully integrate diverse data sources, such as market sentiment and policy interpretations. Secondly, concerning model mechanisms, despite the proficiency of neural networks in pattern recognition, they lack the capacity to comprehend the deeper semantic logic underlying scenarios. Consequently, they are unable to discern causal relationships between event logic and price fluctuations, thereby limiting their adaptability in navigating complex market environments. The advent of Large Language Models (LLMs) has facilitated a paradigm shift from traditional techniques focused on historical pattern analysis to advanced memory mechanisms. For instance, FinMem has constructed a specialized financial memory framework, achieving rudimentary memory functionalities by storing news summaries and event embeddings. Similarly, MagicNet has developed a hierarchical memory architecture, organizing market information into layers according to temporal granularity. Nevertheless, these methodologies encounter significant limitations in the design of their memory systems; they predominantly consist of discrete data fragments, failing to synthesize them into comprehensive narratives that elucidate event progression. Moreover, these approaches primarily depend on semantic similarity identification, which poses challenges in managing more intricate scenarios. Therefore, while there is a noticeable transition from retrospective pattern analysis to the incorporation of memory systems, substantial progress is still necessary to effectively discern and apply pertinent information. This paper focuses on addressing the intricacies of event-driven stock prediction, aiming to identify the primary causal mechanisms driving stock price fluctuations. Prior to the advent of LLMs, this domain advanced from event study methodologies examining cumulative abnormal returns to the automatic extraction of event signals from textual data utilizing basic models such as Bag-of-Words, topic models, and sophisticated deep learning representations like BERT. Nonetheless, these methodologies uniformly exhibit a fundamental issue: they predominantly concentrate on numerical data. Event labels and sentiment scores are ultimately transformed into features for incorporation into temporal models like LSTM or Transformer. Consequently, these models are inclined to learn statistical patterns instead of the intrinsic logic of events, resulting in the significant loss of critical semantic details and hindering the comprehensive understanding of complex issues.
