The examination included both CNNs and ViTs, all trained using uniform adversarial training protocols to accurately assess the impact of such training on attack transferability. Contrary to the intuitive expectation that robustly trained models should produce less transferable attacks, our findings reveal an unexpected pattern. Specifically, adversarially trained models generate adversarial examples that transfer significantly better than those originating from standardly trained models. This suggests that the robust features developed during adversarial training may inadvertently introduce universal vulnerabilities that transcend different architectures, as depicted in Figure 1. Our contributions are notably significant. First, we performed extensive adversarial training on both CNN and ViT architectures, covering 36 models in total. To support reproducibility and further research, all trained models and the associated code will be publicly accessible via a GitHub repository. Moreover, we demonstrate that adversarial training models result in more transferable attacks, highlighting a security paradox that challenges existing assumptions about model robustness. Furthermore, we have developed a novel attack strategy called Momentum Integrated Gradient (MIG), based on the foundational principles of integrated gradient (IG). The MIG method iteratively refines perturbations using a momentum strategy guided by IG, thereby increasing the likelihood of misclassification by focusing on the most influential components identified by IG. A critical feature of IG, known as implementation invariance, facilitates the effective transfer of MIG-crafted attacks between different architectures such as CNNs and ViTs, or even across both types. This level of transferability was previously unattainable in attacks like Projected Gradient Descent (PGD), as illustrated in Figure 2. IG serves as an axiomatic interpretability method aimed at identifying the most influential features that contribute to a model's predictive outputs, computed by accumulating gradients along a direct path from a baseline input to the actual input, where the baseline serves as a reference point.
