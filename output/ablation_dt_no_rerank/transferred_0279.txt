An alternative dataset, AI2-THOR, was utilized instead of the TEACH dataset initially used during the benchmarking phase. Although this methodological transition was essential for enabling direct replication in feasibility assessments and providing a common ground for fair algorithmic comparisons, it also facilitated indirect performance comparisons with tasks of similar nature. Conversely, the LTAA framework was benchmarked against conventional MRTA approaches using the same dataset to maintain consistency in performance evaluation. In addition, the authors are pursuing an evaluation of SMART-LLM within the TEACH dataset to establish a more balanced benchmark system. Secondly, the study was conducted on a relatively small evaluation scale, which allowed for systematic baseline comparisons and detailed capability analyses. However, it should be noted that real-world construction involves significantly more tasks with complex spatial and temporal constraints that were not fully captured in the evaluation dataset. The study employed abstract descriptors such as elemental/compound and heavy/dexterous to characterize construction tasks, facilitating better representation and generalization, given the absence of a large-scale real-world construction operation database. The authors hypothesize that having such a database would enable the evaluation of LTAA across a broader range of scenarios involving dynamic replanning and resource conflicts, thereby strengthening confidence in the robustness of the framework. Moreover, the inherent variability in LLM outputs raises reliability concerns for safety-critical construction operations. Although multi-stage validation processes were implemented to avert execution failures, controlled stochasticity provides nuanced reasoning but can result in varying allocation outcomes. Construction environments that demand absolute precision may require additional verification mechanisms beyond current validation methods. Furthermore, in evaluating the computational resources required, it was challenging to measure SMART-LLM's token and time costs per stage, due to the use of a single concatenated prompt across all stages. Therefore, to facilitate comparison with LTAA, which focuses only on allocation, the estimated costs for SMART-LLM were inferred by dividing the total number of tokens and runtime by three. While this estimation is reasonable, it may not fully capture the true allocation-specific overhead, and future work involving stage-level instrumentation would allow for more precise analysis.
