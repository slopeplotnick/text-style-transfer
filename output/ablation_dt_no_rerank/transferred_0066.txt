Upon further examination, it became evident that the outcomes were primarily driven by individual attitudes toward AI rather than the experimental manipulation itself. When such attitudes were incorporated as covariates in the analysis, the experimental conditions no longer significantly predicted participants' preferences or emotional responses to the music; however, attitudes toward AI remained a consistently significant predictor. This finding aligns with previous research by Hong et al. (2022), which highlights the impact of attitudes toward autonomous machines on evaluation, and offers new insights into the work of Shank et al. (2023), suggesting that composer bias may be contingent upon the musical context and characteristics, such as the song's popularity and perceived human origin. Although several personality traits and music-related factors initially correlated with liking and emotional engagement, their influence was notably reduced once attitudes toward AI were controlled. Notable exceptions to this trend were the traits of openness to experience and neuroticism, which have been previously identified as influential on emotional responses to music, especially concerning emotions like joy and vitality (Gerstgrasser et al., 2023; Juslin et al., 2011; Liljeström et al., 2013). These emotions were markedly pronounced in the musical stimuli employed in this study. Most notably, attitudes toward AI emerged as the sole listener characteristic significantly correlated with both the appreciation of AI-generated music and the resulting emotional responses. In terms of variance in listener characteristics, the results were comparable to those from previous studies (Gerstgrasser et al., 2023; Strauss et al., 2024), underscoring the importance of attitudes toward AI as a crucial factor in the assessment of AI-generated music across diverse audiences. Contrary to the findings of Ansani et al. (2024), our study did not identify a moderating effect of attitudes toward AI on experimental groups, a discrepancy possibly attributed to differences in attitude scales, our focus on listening to tracks instead of live performances, and the use of three experimental conditions rather than two.
