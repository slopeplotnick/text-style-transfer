We will begin by discussing the early foundational research in Bayesian and n-gram-based language models, instrumental in establishing probabilistic frameworks for speech recognition systems. These models laid the groundwork for contemporary systems by modeling word sequences and managing uncertainties inherent in spoken language inputs. Subsequently, we will explore the evolution towards contextual end-to-end speech models that directly integrate contextual information into the modeling process. These models encompass not only linguistic content but also paralinguistic features such as prosody, emotional nuances, and speaker characteristics, thereby enabling more robust and nuanced speech understanding and generation. Furthermore, we will examine the role of large language models (LLMs) in post-ASR error correction, where these models are employed to refine and rectify outputs generated by automatic speech recognition systems. Particular attention will be given to fairness and bias considerations, especially concerning variations in speaker characteristics like accent, dialect, and sociolect, and their impact on speech processing. We will review recent developments in addressing these challenges and discuss the ethical implications of ensuring equitable system performance across diverse speech populations. Additionally, we will delve into theoretical frameworks for LLM adaptation and their integration with speech input, including topics such as population risk measurement and model transferability estimation from speech models to LLM adaptation. These analyses motivate various design pipelines for spoken agents. Building on this, we will explore methodologies for joint text-speech pre-training that enhance the comprehension of integrated learning objectives for speech and textual representations. Finally, we will discuss the application of LLMs to perform voice quality estimation and their capacity to adapt dynamically to diverse speaker characteristics in real-time.
