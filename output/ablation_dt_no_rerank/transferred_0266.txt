Advanced reinforcement learning (RL) techniques have incorporated domain randomization and reward shaping to address the disparities between simulations and real-world applications. Tobin et al. (2017) demonstrated that domain randomization facilitates successful sim-to-real transfer for robotic tasks, while Grzes and Kudenko (2010) developed reward shaping strategies that enhance learning efficiency in multi-agent scenarios. These methods improve robotic adaptability to environmental variability, particularly in construction settings. However, they demand substantial computational resources and careful hyperparameter tuning, which become impractical for real-time coordination in dynamic construction environments where conditions change frequently. Additionally, interpreting and validating the decision-making processes of these learned policies remains a significant challenge. Consequently, game-theoretic approaches have emerged as sophisticated mathematical frameworks for modeling strategic interactions among autonomous agents. In 2023, Martin et al. developed a multi-robot task allocation approach based on game theory, employing Shapley values to quantify individual robot contributions. Their framework exhibited enhanced performance, providing formal analysis of equilibrium solutions and stability properties. Nonetheless, game-theoretic methods still struggle with interpretability in decision-making processes and assume rational agent behavior that may not consistently appear within uncertain construction contexts. Moreover, they require precise specifications of utility functions, which are difficult to delineate for complex construction tasks. Despite these advancements, all learning-based and AI-driven approaches face critical limitations when applied to construction coordination scenarios necessitating explainable decision-making. The black-box nature of deep learning and game-theoretic models impedes transparent understanding of coordination decision processes. Accordingly, construction environments necessitate human supervisors who can understand and validate coordination rationales to ensure safety and effective project management. Therefore, there has been exploration into leveraging Large Language Models (LLMs) for enhanced coordination due to their capabilities in natural language understanding, contextual reasoning, and adaptive decision-making. LLMs represent a transformative paradigm for multi-robot coordination through their ability to articulate reasoning in natural language and elucidate decision-making processes. Unlike traditional optimization methods requiring exhaustive problem specification, LLM-based approaches enable intuitive human-robot interaction, addressing the explainability limitations of conventional methods by providing transparency in decision-making processes through natural language reasoning.
