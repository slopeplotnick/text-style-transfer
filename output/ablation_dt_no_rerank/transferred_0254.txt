In our research, we explored a category of attack where the backdoor is probabilistically activated upon user engagement with a tool, indicating the vulnerability's presence from the start. However, there exists an even more insidious variant of this attack. In this scenario, the tool may initially appear benign, allowing the adversary to avoid malicious actions and relay the tool's standard outputs. Once the tool is extensively used, the attacker can remotely activate the attack. This delayed activation strategy, akin to supply chain attacks, considerably amplifies the threat's potential impact and complicates attribution. Furthermore, the attack's stealth can be enhanced by strategically targeting specific entities. For example, adversaries can analyze user data to specifically target individuals who frequently use the tool, especially those who typically pass security validation. Consequently, this makes the attack more challenging to detect during testing. This study introduces the notion of implicit toxicity, an attack modality previously overlooked, exploiting existing permissions to conduct harmful operations. In order to expose this risk, we developed a method called LeechHijack, which clandestinely commandeers a system's computational resources under the guise of legitimate tool usage. LeechHijack can be executed by any MCP tool provider, infiltrating systems without needing elevated privileges, thus repurposing the system's resources for unauthorized tasks. This reveals a critical flaw within the MCP tool-sharing model, where malicious applications can compromise agent systems without elevated access. We conducted comprehensive evaluations across various agent frameworks, confirming LeechHijack's effectiveness in bypassing current defense mechanisms. Additionally, we examined potential strategies to mitigate associated risks and established foundational measures to enhance future security protocols. Our investigation highlights a security vulnerability within systems utilizing large language models, emphasizing the critical need for thorough scrutiny of third-party applications. Adherence to strict ethical and security guidelines was maintained throughout our experiments. The LeechHijack attack described here serves as a controlled demonstration to highlight the issue, rather than to encourage or promote malicious activity. All experiments were carried out exclusively within secure, controlled environments set up by the research team, ensuring no interactions with genuine systems, platforms, or services occurred. Connections to authentic servers were avoided to prevent any risk of real data exfiltration.
