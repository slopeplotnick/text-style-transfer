Since the base model sometimes struggles to adhere to instructions when the prompt becomes lengthy, we incorporated a quick warm-up stage via supervised fine-tuning (SFT) to help ensure it reliably follows the required format. In the R-Few framework, the Challenger role involves few-shot grounded generation, where it computes a corresponding reward and updates itself accordingly. Meanwhile, the Solver role undertakes online curriculum learning, computing its reward and updating itself. This strategy guarantees that the Solver concentrates on problems that are both challenging and solvable, aligning with principles of optimal learning theory. The Solver's reward combines synthetic and human data, weighted by the curriculum's level of difficulty. R-FeW sustains the self-evolving loop characteristic of R-ZERO while incorporating a grounded and curriculum-aware data pipeline. Within this framework, the Challenger creates synthetic tasks with few-shot demonstrations based on human inputs. Then, the Solver strives to resolve these tasks, carefully estimating success rates. An online filtering mechanism selects tasks of moderate difficulty from both synthetic and authentic datasets. Our study employs Qwen3-4B-Base and Qwen3-8B-Base as the core backbone models. For comparative analysis, we assess against several baselines: (1) Base Model — the pretrained model checkpoint without further training, readily available for download; (2) R-Zero — the inaugural ungrounded self-play approach, enabling the model to autonomously generate its own questions and answers; (3) Absolute Zero — a constrained self-play framework focused on code generation tasks, verified through Python execution; (4) SPICE — a grounded self-play framework using large document corpuses to create diverse reasoning tasks with document-grounded answers. We reconstructed baselines (1) and (2) using the available configurations and consistent training setup, while the results for baselines (3) and (4) were sourced from Liu et al.'s findings.
