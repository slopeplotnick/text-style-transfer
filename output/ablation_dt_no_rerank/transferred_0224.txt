Proper software to support a rational decision-making approach is scarce. Software companies aim to develop products that people want to purchase, and consumers prefer software that aligns with their needs according to current paradigms. However, rational decision-making based on rigorous uncertainty quantification often requires high-performance computing, like that used in Monte Carlo sampling methods, which are costly. Small exploration companies, therefore, are unlikely to invest significant funds in cloud computing systems or hire personnel with the necessary expertise. Most exploration is conducted by junior mining companies, which are small entities owning limited prospects and employing a few geoscientists. Funding for these operations often depends on demonstrating success, such as mineral discovery from a single drill hole. Hence, obtaining further capital for drilling is prioritized over a rigorous scientific decision-making approach. Disproving a geological hypothesis demands long-term data acquisition, yet these companies focus on short-term results. It is clear, then, that new financial strategies are needed. Investing in a single borehole is akin to wagering on a single stock. The typical investment strategy in stocks involves a diversified portfolio that balances risk and return. Therefore, the mining industry should adopt investment strategies that involve a diversified array of prospects, understanding the trade-offs between risk (uncertainty) and potential profit (estimated present value). How this can be effectively implemented remains uncertain. Meanwhile, academia has undergone significant transformation, especially following the introduction of large language models in recent years. Professors have had to swiftly adapt their teaching methods and revise their syllabi, which, arguably, was a change long overdue.
