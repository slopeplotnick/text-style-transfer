The Area Under the Curve (AUC) is particularly emphasized for evaluating the model's ranking capability independently of specific decision thresholds, addressing potential class imbalance in evaluation datasets. In this section, we introduce our unified evaluation framework, designed to bridge the long-standing divide between Hallucination Detection and Fact Verification. We begin by examining why these two closely related paradigms have historically evolved in isolation, and we analyze the incompatibilities in existing benchmarks that have impeded cross-paradigm evaluation. Subsequently, we present the core design principles underlying our framework, followed by a detailed description of its architecture. Before detailing our framework design, it is crucial to analyze the technical root causes that have historically segregated these two closely related paradigms. The divergence stems from the fundamental incompatibility between the text-centric design of traditional Fact Verification datasets and the model-centric requirements of Hallucination Detection methods. Traditional Fact Verification benchmarks, such as those employed in the FEVER context, comprise static claim-label pairs agnostic to the origin of the claim. This structure renders them unsuitable for Hallucination Detection methods, which fundamentally rely on analyzing the generation process, such as internal states or cross-sample consistency. These generative signals are absent in static Fact Verification datasets, creating an impassable barrier for unified evaluation. A seemingly intuitive solution to bridge this gap is to construct static benchmarks comprised of pre-generated language model outputs, such as those from HELM. However, this strategy encounters two significant limitations in the dynamically evolving sphere of artificial intelligence: Model Obsolescence and Inability to Evaluate New Models. The rapid advancement in large language model development implies that datasets created using outputs from current leading models, like LLaMA-3, risk becoming merely archival snapshots as new models are introduced. Crucially, static benchmarks cannot evaluate hallucination detection methods on new or out-of-distribution models. Since the performance of these methodologies is often tightly coupled to specific model characteristics, a framework that cannot test methods on arbitrary new language models fundamentally restricts progress and fails to measure true generalizability.
