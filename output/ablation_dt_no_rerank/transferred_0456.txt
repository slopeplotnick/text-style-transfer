Question Construction. As per the classification described in Section 2.1, eight graduate students specializing in domain-specific areas selected specific categories and crafted 20 prompts each. Importantly, each prompt was meticulously designed to involve reasoning across at least two scientific concepts, thus ensuring a sufficient level of difficulty. The design of these experiments drew inspiration from public educational examples to guarantee scientific validity, making sure they could be effectively represented within videos of ten seconds or less. Moreover, two of these students curated a set of 40 Image-to-Video (I2V) questions, each paired with a reference video depicting the experiment as the ground truth. The reference videos were sourced from public datasets and online resources, with key frames extracted from the initial segment serving as conditional input images illustrating the complete experimental setup. Review and Verification Process. Subsequently, upon completing the test suite, all the graduate students assembled as a collaborative review panel, rigorously cross-verifying each set of 20 prompts to ensure specificity, procedural accuracy, and clarity of the intended outcomes. In the final phase of quality assurance, sophisticated video generation models, namely Sora-2 and Wan-2.5-T2V-Preview, were employed to synthesize the initial seconds of each prompt, thus confirming that the core elements of the experimental setups were adequately represented. Recent scholarly work has demonstrated that advanced Vision-Language Models (VLMs) can yield judgments closely aligned with human perspectives, contingent upon precise prompting and calibration. Thus, in VideoScience-Bench, we implement a VLM-as-a-Judge framework to systematically compare performance disparities between reasoning-oriented VLMs and models not primarily designed for reasoning, such as GPT-4o. Our observations show that direct prompting of VLMs, especially those not centered on reasoning, often results in inadequately justified assessments and false-positive evaluations, as detailed in Appendix E. Therefore, this insight fuels the development of a robust quantitative auto-evaluation mechanism incorporating a checklist-based grading approach, where each rating is substantiated through key video frames and an evidence table synthesized from comprehensive computer-vision analyses of the videos.
