With the increasing deployment of video language models in real-world applications, such as AI glasses and household robots, these models face the challenge of processing and reasoning over extremely long videos, which can span several hours or even days. Recent methodologies have introduced memory-based approaches that construct external memories from abstracted video representations. These techniques enable the models to focus on essential information by retrieving a select set of relevant memories, thus eliminating the need to analyze each individual frame. This is a more cost-effective and efficient method compared to examining every video frame, which demands significant computational resources. As illustrated in Fig. 1(a), current methodologies largely rely on text-based representations, converting segments of video content into captions or summaries to aid information retrieval and comprehension. However, while some approaches incorporate visual information, they primarily emphasize object recognition rather than deeper analytical functions. Additionally, these models typically select a predetermined number of video segments for analysis, often comprising three clips each lasting 30 seconds. This design presents two major drawbacks. Firstly, it fails to effectively integrate visual data from the video with textual memories during retrieval and information generation. Visual details are crucial for tasks requiring feature recognition, spatial reasoning, or accurate scene interpretation, and textual descriptions alone cannot fully convey these complexities. Moreover, the simultaneous use of both captions and frames during response generation may inadvertently lead to cognitive overload, diluting the efficacy of visual information processing. Therefore, it is imperative to develop strategies that strategically integrate textual and visual data to optimize information retrieval for specific queries—a domain that remains largely unexplored.
