Macro-level risks emerge when an entire system begins to operate independently from the designers' objectives, governed by its internal logic. At this point, interventions targeting individual components or connections fail to restore balance; the system has surpassed the Emergent Systemic Risk Horizon. In this regime, the system's behavior becomes autopoietic, maintaining and reproducing its operational logic without external intervention. Consequently, oversight shifts from preventive to reactive, and traditional safety measures lose their effectiveness. Importantly, the risks outlined do not require malicious intent. Many systemic failures arise spontaneously due to coordination, imitation, or recursive use of outcomes, even when all adherents follow prescribed protocols. This emergent behavior is rooted in structural interactional properties, such as feedback loops, shared contexts, or aligned objectives, rather than intentional interference. However, adversarial agents pose a threat by exploiting these pathways that naturally lead to systemic divergence. They can insert messages, alter directives, or disrupt connectivity, thus hastening instability, fostering maladaptive coordination, or skewing collective perceptions. The Emergent Systemic Risk Horizon framework, therefore, considers both adversarial and non-adversarial dynamics on a continuum: one shows how instability can arise on its own, while the other demonstrates how it can be deliberately induced or intensified. The phenomena discussed indicate that systemic malfunctions cannot merely be attributed to component-level deficiencies. As interactions increase, the distinction between causes and effects blurs; actions beneficial to individual elements may, via interaction, become catalysts for widespread disruptions. Therefore, traditional evaluation methods, which focus on isolated outputs or static benchmarks, fall short. They evaluate compliance rather than behavior in contextual interaction. Hence, understanding safety within this new paradigm requires reevaluating our analytical approach. Rather than assessing individual components in isolation, we must explore interactions, feedback mechanisms, and adaptive dynamics as multiple elements engage collectively.
