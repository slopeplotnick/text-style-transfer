Demonstrating the feasibility of LLMs for complex optimization problems involves assessing their computational efficiency in allocation using dynamic prompting and validating their performance against algorithmic solutions like DQN and Q learning. The framework achieves these goals by systematically progressing from task decomposition to balanced allocation optimization. Initially, long-horizon construction tasks are decomposed into manageable subtasks using object-centric and skill-centric strategies, adapted from the SMART-LLM framework (Kannan et al. 2024). The object-centric approach identifies task components based on physical objects and required manipulations, while the skill-centric strategy organizes subtasks according to robot capability needs, such as precision handling or force application. These subtasks are then allocated to heterogeneous robots through capability-aware matching, which accounts for robot specializations and task necessities. However, initial testing revealed a significant imbalance in workload distribution across robot teams; pure success-probability maximization led to overutilization of high-capability robots while some specialized units were underutilized. Consequently, phase-adaptive allocation strategies were developed, dynamically adjusting decision priorities throughout project progression—from success-rate emphasis in early phases to workload equity prioritization later—thus balancing overall mission success with fair resource utilization. The methodology development proceeded through three stages: first, the SMART-LLM implementation assessed LLM feasibility for MRTA but highlighted validation inconsistencies and output reliability concerns. Second, these challenges were addressed by the Self Corrective-Agent Architecture through multi-stage validation and hierarchical retry mechanisms. Finally, the LTAA framework integrated these principles with phase-adaptive allocation strategies, enabling systematic benchmarking against traditional optimization methods. The authors detail the classic LLM-based MRTA framework development, known as SMART-LLM (Kannan et al. 2024), and the encountered challenges—primarily validation inconsistencies, output reliability, and coordination limitations. These challenges sparked the creation of a phase-adaptive and self-corrective architecture using LangGraph, implementing multi-stage validation and retry mechanisms to rectify deficiencies. These advancements contributed to the development of the LTAA framework. For evaluation, the LTAA framework utilizes the TEACH dataset as a standard benchmark for direct comparison with Q-learning, DQN, and DP baselines under identical task scenarios. Evaluation criteria encompass workload balance distribution, feature-specific performance across task types, and reasoning quality assessment to ensure both allocation accuracy and performance efficiency.
