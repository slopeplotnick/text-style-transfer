We propose a novel approach to distinguish perceptual and cognitive functions in artificial intelligence models by integrating instances of incorrect reasoning into the training data, conditioning the model to discern between valid and invalid arguments. Moreover, our Structured Thinking Learning (STL) methodology enhances both perceptual and cognitive processes simultaneously, contrasting with earlier techniques that focused primarily on cognitive enhancement. Our methodology was rigorously evaluated across domains, including commonsense, language, and science. It demonstrated enhanced efficacy while circumventing reliance on human or proprietary supervision. Particularly within vision-language reasoning, integrating reasoning is pivotal for enhancing model performance. While recent advancements yield robust outcomes on standardized benchmarks, effectively incorporating visual information within the reasoning paradigm remains challenging, especially for open-source models. To facilitate sequential reasoning, several methodologies employ prompts. One strategy entails preliminarily examining context before generating high-level features, whereas another utilizes scene graphs to elucidate object-attribute relationships, guiding graph construction and reasoning. Additionally, some strategies fine-tune models with datasets comprising multimodal reasoning chains; however, constructing these datasets often requires sophisticated teacher models or extensive human annotations, posing scalability and cost challenges. Our approach allows models to autonomously improve their reasoning capabilities without relying on specialized datasets. Self-training serves as a semi-supervised approach, allowing models to refine their performance using their own outputs as training data. This technique advances reasoning skills in large language models by systematically generating and reusing reasoning steps, thus enabling autonomous capability enhancement without human intervention. Although certain studies have refined reasoning by selecting and optimizing appropriate examples, self-training methodologies within vision-language models remain relatively unexplored, providing a rich avenue for further investigation.
