Except for Study 1, all simulated datasets successfully replicated the factor structure of their corresponding scale. Similarly, M-CFA demonstrates that simulated participants and real-world ones respond to the scales in comparable ways. These results align with prior research on group-level simulated data. Remarkably, Studies 3 and 4 exhibit substantial measurement invariance across datasets compared to Studies 1 and 2, possibly indicating that scales derived from simulated subjects may be more generalizable to human populations than vice versa. One plausible interpretation is that, when responding, LLMs tend to perceive scale items through a standardized semantic framework, less influenced by randomness and biases stemming from study conditions and population characteristics. Consequently, factor structures obtained from simulated samples may possess greater semantic generalizability to external samples than those extracted from real-world data. Further research should explore the factors shaping differences in how LLMs and human subjects interpret and respond to questionnaire items. Despite the consistently positive outcomes yielded by CFA, simulated samples fail to replicate the pairwise correlations, distribution, and variance of real-world samples. Taken together, these findings suggest that although both datasets share an identical factor structure, their score distributions significantly differ across all evaluated dimensions. Therefore, group-level data and their dimensional structures are sufficiently reliable for use in pilot testing, whereas individual-level simulated data are unsuitable for this purpose. These limitations are consistent with the observations made by Petrov et al. Notably, while Studies 2, 3, and 4 yield similar results, Study 1 shows distinct differences. These discrepancies may be attributed to several factors: firstly, the dimensional structure of this scale was determined using a different rotation method (minres), which may affect response patterns. Secondly, this scale employs a 5-point Likert scale for responses, unlike the 6-point or 7-point scales used by others.
