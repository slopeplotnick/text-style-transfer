Ensuring that devices like smartphones, laptops, personal IoT assistants, autonomous drones, and automotive systems can manage malicious prompts and harmful responses autonomously, particularly in offline and on-device settings, is imperative. Such on-device guardrails are crucial for real-time safety enforcement on edge devices, eliminating dependence on cloud-based inference and improving latency, privacy, and deployability in constrained environments. However, although recent efforts have explored the development of lightweight multilingual safety models, these approaches often struggle to generalize effectively across a large number of languages, especially when scaled beyond high-resource languages. This marks a critical gap in the current AI safety research landscape. Therefore, fine-tuning a pretrained model on a high-resource language dataset and evaluating it on another language is a common strategy for zero-shot cross-lingual transfer, enabled by shared linguistic representations. As a result, high-resource or linguistically similar languages to the training set tend to benefit more, while low-resource languages still face challenges due to limited overlap and reduced exposure during pretraining. To address these challenges, we present CREST, a parameter-efficient, multilingual model for 100 languages, trained on only 13 high-resource languages with a modest 0.5B parameters. Moreover, we utilize the multilingual transformer model to cluster semantically and structurally similar languages. The representational closeness of languages within a cluster allows generalization of model performance, meaning fine-tuning on a high-resource language can effectively benefit low-resource languages in the same cluster. This approach is particularly valuable in safety-critical applications where annotated data is scarce or unavailable in many target languages. Despite its relatively small size, CREST consistently outperforms several baseline models, achieving strong and balanced performance across both high-resource and low-resource languages. Crucially, the model’s cross-lingual generalization is enabled by effectively exploiting shared vocabulary structures and script similarities.
