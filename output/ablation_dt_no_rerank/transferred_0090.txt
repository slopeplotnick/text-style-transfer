We evaluated a diverse set of recent language models spanning closed- and open-weight releases (see Appendix A.4 for details). Where possible, we disabled extended-thinking or reasoning controls to probe the models' natural, emergent behavior. This was feasible for all models except GPT-5 mini, which only allows adjusting reasoning depth; therefore, we set it to the lowest level. Moreover, we conducted a human baseline study for comparison on all our tasks under a limited number of ablations. See Appendix A.5 for details. Human results are included in the left panel of Figure 8. However, this experiment and analysis are used only as a reference point here, as our primary focus is on comparing language models against each other. Extensive human psychophysics studies, such as those by Jazayeri & Shadlen (2010) and Petzschner & Glasauer (2011), extensively document the magnitude estimation effects examined here. In general, most models perform better in the text modality than the image modality, except on the maze distance estimation task. The text input for this task involves a long, detailed path description, which is much more complex than the ASCII prompts used in other tasks (Fig. 2). Consequently, most models perform worse in the text modality for this task; however, GPT-5 Mini is an outlier here, achieving near-perfect text performance due to its residual reasoning capability, which can be attenuated but not fully disabled. This illustrates the task-dependent nature of behavior. Across tasks, the factor evidence for Bayesian behavior is consistently higher in the image modality than in text (Appendix A.11). Transitioning from unimodal to multimodal inputs does not uniformly improve performance. Nonetheless, some models are better able to leverage information from the additional modality: Llama-4 Maverick attains its best performance under multimodal conditions across all tasks, while Claude 3.7 Sonnet and GPT-4o improve on two of the three tasks.
