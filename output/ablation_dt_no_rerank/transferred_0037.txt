Self-Consistency (SC) entails aggregating multiple outputs using a majority voting mechanism. Soft Self-Consistency (Soft-SC) involves selecting the minimum, mean, or product of confidence scores within each category. Generative Self-Aggregation (GSA) engages the language model to synthesize a novel response derived from the context of multiple samples. Meanwhile, Universal Self-Consistency employs the language model to select the most appropriate response among various alternatives. In examining advanced language models, we consider gemini-2.5-flash, qwen3 next-80b, and gpt-oss-120b. Our analysis includes two machine translation tasks and six tasks from the Reward Bench 2 benchmark. This benchmark is crafted to evaluate reward models across six domains: factuality, strict adherence to instructions, mathematics, safety, focus, and instances of ties. For our evaluation, we constructed preference pairs by generating all possible pairwise combinations from each task's source dataset, incorporating both accepted and rejected responses. These pairs are categorized into 'non-tie' pairs (comprising one accepted and one rejected response) and 'tie' pairs (comprising either two accepted or two rejected responses). Subsequently, we selected a subset of 1000 examples for each of the six designated tasks to construct the final benchmark. Moreover, a comprehensive analysis of the vote distributions corresponding to each task is provided in Appendix B. The results obtained from various advanced language models, namely gemini-2.5-flash, gpt-oss-120b, and qwen3 next-80b, illustrate a consistent qualitative pattern. This consistency suggests that the efficacy of our approach is robust across these language model architectures. The structural characteristics of the problem involve the frequency of ambiguous instances, the propensity of the language model to produce directional versus tied votes, and the interaction of these characteristics with the MAE-aligned Davidson model. Notably, certain tasks exhibit smooth, well-behaved calibration landscapes that facilitate external application, while others present more complex landscapes, making their fitted parameters less amenable to generalization. Designing effective evaluations to anticipate conditions under which tasks may be transferable—and to assess robustness under significant distribution shifts or targets outside the typical range—remains an intriguing direction for future research.
