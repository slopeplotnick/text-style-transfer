The MODOMA system provides a laboratory simulation environment that enhances the investigation of language acquisition. It includes participants from both adult and child demographics, allowing for a comprehensive analysis. Researchers can modify the system's parameters while it transparently demonstrates the acquired grammar rules, facilitating straightforward examination and evaluation. This study exemplifies the potential of employing a computational framework with multiple agents for first language acquisition. Crucially, the implementation of Zipf's Law is a pivotal component in assessing the efficacy of the MODOMA system, thus establishing a robust foundation for subsequent research endeavors. Moreover, the experiments employ a wide array of methodologies to classify various word types, encompassing all elements necessary for more intricate experimental designs. Consequently, the study lays a strong groundwork for future investigations that might examine how altering parameters affects the grammar acquired by the child agent, its produced utterances, and its interactions with the adult agent. Furthermore, the exploration could include examining the frequency of the lexical acquisition process and whether pre-existing lexical categories should be modified based on newly processed information. Additionally, the influence of feedback from the adult agent on the child agent's language acquisition and verbal communication skills is a key focus of investigation. The positive outcomes of these experiments pave the way for exploring more sophisticated learning methodologies within the MODOMA framework, which also serves as a valuable tool for examining complex grammatical structures. For instance, research by Shakouri et al. (2025) demonstrates that grammatical categories like nouns and verbs can be autonomously acquired under conditions similar to those used for the acquisition of function and content words, employing sophisticated statistical methodologies. Ultimately, the results signify a significant advancement in language acquisition research, providing a crucial stepping stone for further scholarly inquiry in this domain.
