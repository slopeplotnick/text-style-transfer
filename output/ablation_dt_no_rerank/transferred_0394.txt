Despite its significance, the community still lacks a comprehensive, reproducible, and scalable methodology for benchmarking the power and energy requirements of LLM inference across varying model sizes, hardware generations, software stacks, and deployment configurations—from single-node to multi-node environments. Although some initiatives attempt to address this need, they do so only partially. For instance, the MLPerf Inference benchmark (Reddi et al. 2020) establishes performance benchmarking criteria across different machine learning (ML) tasks; however, it does not systematically incorporate cumulative power assessments or normalize energy utilization specific to LLM service metrics, such as Joules per generated token or per prompt. The MLPerf Power benchmark (Tschand et al. 2025) expands its measurement scope, yet its framework predominantly (i) emphasizes single-node configurations, (ii) examines relatively modest model sizes compared to the cutting-edge state-of-the-art (SOTA) LLMs, such as the Llama 3 405B-class deployments, and (iii) often relies on costly, high-precision metering equipment that is challenging to implement across diverse institutional testbeds. Moreover, prevailing benchmark suites frequently neglect configuration variables routinely adjusted in practice, such as batch sizing strategies, tensor/pipeline parallelism, context length, and quantization levels—all of which significantly affect both instantaneous power consumption and average energy per token. To address this gap, we propose TokenPowerBench, a novel, lightweight, and extensible benchmark specifically designed to assess the power consumption and energy expenditure of LLM inference. TokenPowerBench features a modular instrumentation system that integrates with vendor telemetry APIs, such as GPU/CPU/Memory power sensors, and performs node-level energy sampling, with the potential for additional measurements at the rack or facility level when available. A unified metrics pipeline aligns each power measurement with the primary inference phases—prefill and decode—enabling precise identification of energy expenditure points. We conduct extensive experiments to systematically explore the relationship between energy consumption and inference-relevant parameters, including batch size, maximum context length, parallelization strategy, and quantization.
