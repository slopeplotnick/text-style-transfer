Understanding high-resolution images presents a substantial challenge for multimodal large language models (MLLMs). A recent study addressed this issue by segmenting images into smaller sections and evaluating the semantic similarity of each section to a query using a specialized retrieval-augmented model. The approach selectively emphasizes the most pertinent sections to identify the primary object, effectively disregarding extraneous elements. However, segmenting the image may disrupt the alignment between the segments and the query, as whole objects can be fragmented across multiple sections. Empirical analysis indicates that segments of varying dimensions are more effective at different levels of detail. Consequently, we developed a training-free method called Multi-resolution Retrieval-Detection (MRD) to comprehend intricate images. To address the semantic similarity bias caused by section fragmentation, we employ a multi-resolution semantic fusion approach, integrating semantic similarity maps generated at varying levels of resolution to enhance the accuracy of semantic information and maintain the integrity of object structures. Additionally, for the direct localization of objects on a global scale, we introduce an open-vocabulary object detection (OVD) model that uses a sliding window technique to identify object regions. Empirical evaluations on high-resolution image analysis benchmarks demonstrate the efficacy of our proposed methodology. MLLMs have significantly advanced in integrating and interpreting both visual and linguistic information, enabling robust capabilities for complex cognitive tasks. By leveraging visual cues, these models decipher intricate images, effectively bridging the gap between raw data and semantic interpretation. Yet, the common practice of utilizing a fixed granularity for image analysis, while simplifying computational processes, introduces substantial limitations in accuracy and depth of understanding. Reducing the level of detail in high-resolution images can compromise the integrity of shapes and introduce blurring, degrading the quality of intricate details. Current methodologies have shown inadequacy for tasks requiring detailed image analysis, as evidenced by their suboptimal performance on high-resolution image comprehension benchmarks.
