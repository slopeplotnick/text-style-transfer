Within this framework, we examine the limitations of the recent approach [16]. As shown in Figure 1(a), the model initially attends to multimodal inputs, integrating both audio and visual cues. However, as reasoning progresses, its focus shifts to the text it has generated. This shift results in the model anchoring its reasoning on the emotional cues present in the initial segment of its generated text. The reasoning process typically starts with generating a visual description, reflecting the training data structure, with visual representations leading auditory elements and subsequent reasoning. Nevertheless, such a visual-first reasoning path may not accurately capture the primary emotional drivers, especially when emotion is conveyed through vocal tone or semantic content. If the initial visual-based reasoning proves irrelevant or inaccurate, the subsequent analytic process becomes increasingly misaligned, as shown in Figure 1(b). Consequently, the initial step becomes disproportionately significant. This issue also appears in other domains, where early cues divert reasoning from the foundational evidence [17]. To address this, we propose Modality-Importance-Guided Reasoning (MIGR), aiming to initiate reasoning from the modality most representative of the target emotion, ensuring consistency throughout analysis. We introduce Modality Importance (MI) estimation, designed to determine the critical modality for accurate emotion recognition. MI evaluates emotion prediction effectiveness under audio-only, visual-only, and combined audio-visual conditions. For example, if the model accurately identifies the emotion with audio alone but errs with visual or combined inputs, the visual modality may be irrelevant or misleading, necessitating prioritization of auditory cues. Utilizing this Modality Importance concept with recent methodologies for reasoning-based models [15, 16], we develop a two-stage learning framework: first, modality-aligned Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT), followed by modality-aligned reward optimization. During the SFT phase, we apply Mutual Information (MI) to restructure reasoning text into modality-specific segments, subsequently organizing these components accordingly.
