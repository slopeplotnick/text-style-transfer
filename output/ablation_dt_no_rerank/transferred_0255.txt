In contrast to traditional algorithmic models, language models not only boast strong predictive capabilities but also offer human-readable interpretations and the flexibility to support multiple tasks within a unified framework. Small Language Models (SLMs) have emerged as a practical alternative to Large Language Models (LLMs), optimized for efficiency through methods like quantization, knowledge distillation, and lightweight architectures. Designed to execute fundamental Natural Language Processing tasks such as text classification, summarization, and question-answering in resource-constrained settings—such as mobile devices and embedded systems—SLMs offer a compelling alternative to LLMs by reducing computational overhead while maintaining robust performance in specialized tasks. Recent research indicates that in classification settings with limited data, smaller models can match or even surpass larger models, especially in domain-specific areas like text classification and healthcare. This demonstrates that SLMs provide a scalable and efficient solution, making them a promising direction for detecting mental health issues on social media. Nonetheless, previous research focused predominantly on singular tasks or domains, neglecting multi-domain scenarios that integrate various mental health conditions such as depression, stress, and suicidality. Unlike prior LLMs with prohibitive parameter sizes that are impractical in resource-limited or privacy-sensitive environments, lightweight SLMs emphasize deployability with advantages in inference latency, memory footprint, and cost efficiency. Comparisons of multiple SLMs to LLM baselines across mental health tasks, trained on social media data, have revealed the increased efficacy of few-shot prompting for SLMs. Also noteworthy is the development by Kim et al. of mhGPT, a Small Language Model that excels in mental health tasks compared to larger models like MentaLLaMA and Gemma, despite its smaller size and reduced dataset. Additional studies suggest that SLMs could be integrated into clinical workflows for structured, meaningful tasks surpassing issue detection alone. However, these studies frequently lack systematic task balancing and often do not employ parameter-efficient fine-tuning, leading to either minimal prompting adaptation or full fine-tuning.
