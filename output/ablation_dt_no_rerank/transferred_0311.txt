The experiments in this study explore whether a computational simulation of language acquisition can independently identify grammatical structures and lexical categories through interaction with Delilah, a language model that provides exemplars of adult linguistic use. After acquiring this linguistic framework, the daughter language model can apply its newly gained grammatical knowledge to engage with the mother model. Notably, the daughter model employs these grammatical structures to generate new sentences, which are communicated to the mother during their dialogue. Importantly, when the experimental conditions allow, the mother model has the ability to offer evaluative feedback on the structural efficacy of the grammar, thereby facilitating the daughter model in refining its linguistic capabilities. Moreover, the daughter model can utilize this acquired knowledge to comprehend novel utterances from the mother, particularly by discerning their lexical categories. Empirical evidence reveals that lexemes serving syntactic functions appear more frequently than those with specific semantic content, with certain lexical items manifesting infrequently within a corpus. This distribution aligns with Zipf's law, which stipulates that high-frequency lexemes exhibit significantly greater usage than those of lower frequency, a pattern extensively analyzed by researchers like Mandelbrot and Zipf. Furthermore, there's an intriguing correlation between the lexical distribution observed in languages and the grammatical categories delineated by linguists. This study investigates whether a similar correlation can be identified in language produced by a computational system.
