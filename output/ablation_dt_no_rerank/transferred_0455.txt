Our large-scale experiments, conducted via the UniFact framework across diverse datasets, model families like LLaMA and Qwen, and detection methodologies, reveal several crucial insights that directly address our research questions. First, in response to RQ1, we find that no single paradigm achieves universal superiority; rather, performance is significantly influenced by the specific LLM and the task at hand. Second, and most importantly, regarding RQ2, our analysis offers compelling evidence of synergy. The HD and FV methods demonstrate success on different subsets of factual errors, confirming they capture unique yet complementary dimensions of factuality. Finally, with respect to RQ3, building on these findings, we illustrate that hybrid methods combining both paradigms consistently and significantly outperform individual approaches, setting a new benchmark in factual error detection. The implications are twofold: for researchers, they suggest a move from isolated studies to a unified approach that harnesses this complementarity; for practitioners, they highlight the necessity of integrating internal, model-based signals with external, evidence-based reasoning to ensure robust factuality assessment. In summary, this paper makes key contributions including: Analytical - we delineate and scrutinize the conceptual and methodological divide between Hallucination Detection and Fact Verification, emphasizing its implications for AI's advancement; Methodological - we introduce UniFact, a unified evaluation framework that enables fair and scalable comparison between HD and FV; Empirical - through extensive studies, we provide strong evidence that HD and FV exhibit distinct yet complementary strengths; Practical - we demonstrate that hybrid strategies combining both paradigms set new standards in performance. Furthermore, we establish formal definitions for Fact Verification and Hallucination Detection. Although often conflated recently, distinguishing these terms is crucial. As outlined in the introduction and shown in Figure 1, the main distinction concerns the object of evaluation: FV assesses the semantic truthfulness of text in an origin-agnostic way, whereas HD evaluates the factual correctness of text generated by an LLM. We provide formal definitions of HD and FV in Sections 2.1 and 2.2 and unify their evaluation metrics in Section 2.3.
