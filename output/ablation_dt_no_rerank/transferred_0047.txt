Robustness and generalization remain significant challenges for these models, even when they are trained with large and diverse datasets. Despite the extensive datasets, these models often fail to adapt to out-of-distribution scenarios, struggling with novel camera angles, lighting changes, or unexpected scene alterations. This intrinsic fragility limits their practical utility in real-world environments, where conditions are naturally dynamic and unpredictable. To address these issues, strategies generally fall into two categories: data-centric and representation-centric. Data-centric approaches aim to improve robustness by using diverse datasets, much like Libero-Plus, which expose models to a breadth of visual conditions. However, acquiring such comprehensive datasets is costly and labor-intensive, making long-term adaptation challenging. Representation-centric strategies focus on enhancing models' spatial representation capabilities by learning geometrically consistent or 3D-aware representations. By utilizing multi-view supervision and specialized 3D-aware architectures, these methods improve viewpoint invariance. Nonetheless, these models remain sensitive to irrelevant visual factors such as background clutter and lighting variations, indicating an incomplete adaptability to diverse visual scenarios. While previous methods often assume that increasing data or developing more sophisticated 3D architectures is essential for robust models, few have examined if the limitations originate from spatial representation itself. To explore this, we conceptualize the model as having two key components: Spatial Modeling, achieved by the visual encoder, which identifies spatial attributes like object locations and orientations, and Physical Modeling, which integrates spatial representations with task instructions for decision-making. Changes in camera perspective primarily disrupt the spatial configuration of the scene rather than the task's intrinsic semantics, leading to performance degradation largely due to misalignment in Spatial Modeling. Despite this, the Physical Modeling component remains operational, maintaining the model's reasoning and planning abilities but impairing execution due to distorted spatial information.
