Table 3 provides a comparative analysis of evaluation scores from various configurations of VideoScience-Judge against human annotations. Although the use of GPT-5 pro results in less pronounced score inflation, significant discrepancies remain in dimensions such as Dynamism, Coherence, and Immutability, where the video language model (VLM) tends to overestimate performance. However, integrating a checklist and requiring the model to substantiate each rating with evidence from key frames significantly reduces these overestimations. Moreover, the addition of computer vision tools like ByteTrack, which facilitates reliable identity association across frames and provides concrete evidence for penalizing frame jumps or unexpected object transformations, substantially narrows the scoring discrepancies by 0.07 in Coherence and 0.09 in Immutability, compared to the standard VideoScience-Judge without the checklist. This approach, however, leads to score quantization and reduces the ability to differentiate between models with similar features. Its rule-based approach for identifying explicit violations of discrete physical laws (e.g., "Does the ball fall downward?" "Does water flow correctly?") fails to capture the comprehensive nature of physical realism. This includes consistent motion trajectories, causal relationships across time, acceleration patterns, momentum conservation, and nuances of motion smoothness naturally evaluated by humans. These limitations are particularly evident in VideoScience-Bench, which contrasts with PhyGenBench's simplified, single-principle scenarios. VideoScience-Bench involves multiple scientific concepts, such as multi-object interactions and fluid dynamics, where outcomes result from combinations of laws rather than single principles, characterized by cascading effects where second-order dynamics are significant.
