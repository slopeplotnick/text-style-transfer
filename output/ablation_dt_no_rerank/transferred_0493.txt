However, current video model assessments predominantly emphasize rudimentary physical knowledge, confining evaluations to everyday scenarios that necessitate merely a basic understanding of physical phenomena. We introduce VideoScience-Bench, a benchmark aimed at evaluating the scientific understanding and reasoning capabilities of video models. Each instance encompasses multiple scientific concepts and demands undergraduate-level knowledge, spanning 103 concepts across physics and chemistry. We evaluate whether these video models can serve as zero-shot scientific reasoners, able to comprehend scientific setups and generate expected phenomena independently. The evaluation framework scrutinizes performance across five principal aspects: Prompt Consistency, Expected Phenomenon, Dynamism, Immutability, and Coherence. Moreover, we introduce VideoScience-Judge, an automated evaluation framework employing a checklist-based methodology to appraise these models effectively. VideoScience-Judge constructs a prompt-specific checklist to facilitate the evaluation process, subsequently selecting key frames to identify causally significant moments within each video. These components collectively underpin the VLM-based scoring methodology, and the scores are synthesized into a singular metric indicative of the model's comprehensive reasoning proficiency. For validation, we observed that the automated assessments generated by VideoScience-Judge exhibit a stronger correspondence with domain expert evaluations compared to other benchmarks focused on physical commonsense and visual fidelity. In summary, this study investigates whether video generation models can reason over multiple interacting scientific concepts simultaneously. To this end, we provide three primary contributions: First, we introduce VideoScience-Bench, a high-quality benchmark aimed at evaluating zero-shot scientific reasoning in video generation models, utilizing real-world phenomena across 103 physics and chemistry concepts. Second, we develop VideoScience-Judge, an evaluative framework grounded in a checklist-based methodology that utilizes Video Language Models (VLMs) as evaluators. This system incorporates key-frame selection and employs computer vision tools to detect physical cues, such as motion and object interactions, thereby providing quantitative assessments of both scientific validity and perceptual quality of the videos. Third, we conduct an extensive analysis utilizing VideoScience-Bench. Although contemporary video models are capable of generating high-quality, photorealistic, and temporally coherent videos, VideoScience-Bench continues to present significant challenges: numerous systems lack the proficiency to effectively model intricate scientific phenomena and frequently breach fundamental physical laws. Among the evaluated models, Sora-2 and Veo-3 demonstrated superior performance, exhibiting nascent capabilities in scientific reasoning.
