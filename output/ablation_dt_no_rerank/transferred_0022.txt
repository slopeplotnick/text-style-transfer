Our study covers three key areas of related research: using external feedback in methodologies, reducing reliance on human annotations, and exploring theoretical frameworks about how representational approaches affect output quality. Typically, to align large language models with human preferences, Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) is used. This approach employs human preference data to train a reward model, which then fine-tunes the policy to optimize outcomes while adhering to a reference model. This method underpins models such as ChatGPT and Llama. However, it faces challenges, including the high cost and potential inaccuracies of data, and reward models being susceptible to manipulation or ineffectiveness across various scenarios. Consequently, methods such as Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), KTO, Bayesian RLHF, and MaxMin-RLHF aim to refine the policy directly through pairwise comparisons, thus eliminating the need for an explicit value network. Nonetheless, these approaches still require curated preference datasets. Furthermore, certain models provide evaluative scores and feedback on reasoning and assessment, thereby maintaining supervision external to the model's core operations. An alternative strategy involves using automatic supervision or intrinsic signals from the model to reduce dependence on human-provided annotations. Programmatic feedback methods like DeepSeek-R1 and TÜLU 3 bypass the need for human input by utilizing automated supervisory signals.
