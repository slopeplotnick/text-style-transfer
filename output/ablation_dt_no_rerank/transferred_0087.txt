Examining the results for each sample, we discover that, in some cases, opting for a different resolution actually leads to more precise outcomes compared to when the resolution is set at 112, as depicted in Figure 3. The results obtained from image crops at various resolutions, alongside the visualization of the semantic similarity map, offer an intuitive rationale for this occurrence: When the complete object is divided into different portions, certain segments exhibit higher semantic similarity, as calculated by VisRAG, whereas other segments display lower similarity. After the filtering process, only segments with higher similarity are retained. However, this compromises the cohesion of the target object and interferes with the assessment by MLLM. In response to this challenge, we introduce a novel framework called Multi-resolution Retrieval Detection (MRD). The core design of MRD lies in employing multiple resolutions at different scales to better identify regions containing target objects. This simplifies the process of finding image crops associated with these objects, thereby eliminating irrelevant distractions and improving the perceptual understanding of high-resolution images by MLLMs. Based on insights from subsection 3.2, we propose that utilizing different resolutions for computing semantic similarity is more effective for objects of varying sizes and positions. Drawing from this insight, we first introduce an efficient method termed Multi-resolution Semantic Fusion, which calculates semantic similarity maps across various resolutions at a local scale and merges them to enhance the accuracy of similarity judgments. Furthermore, we employ an open-vocabulary object detection model that systematically analyzes the entire high-resolution image using a sliding-window approach, assigning confidence scores to regions likely containing target objects. By integrating these detection confidence scores with multi-resolution semantic similarity maps, our method not only improves localization of target regions but also discriminates fine-grained differences among crops within these regions, thereby aiding subsequent identification processes in accurately pinpointing crucial image areas.
