Engineering practice has initiated the adoption of hierarchical systems where supervisory agents bridge human operators and worker agents. These overseers evaluate peer outputs, flag anomalies, and can suspend execution when thresholds are exceeded. This layered control approach aligns with the "Institutional AI" paradigm, which will be discussed later. However, merely embedding oversight mechanisms is not sufficient. Supervisory models operate on the same representational framework as the agents they monitor, thereby inheriting similar biases and feedback loop issues. If multiple supervisors align in their judgments, this can lead to collusion or correlated failures at the oversight layer. Therefore, effective governance requires not only additional hierarchical layers but also a diversity of oversight signals, redundant observation paths, and a clear delineation of authority among supervisory roles. As systems transition to multi-agent autonomy, new safety complexities arise. Relying on centralized systems or static frameworks is inadequate; instead, networks must exhibit distributed resilience—the capacity to detect, absorb, and rectify deviations through internal communication patterns. Models in these ecosystems no longer fail in isolation; failure propagates collectively. Understanding how such collective instability originates and measuring it is essential for developing robust AI safety protocols in the era of interacting language models. The increasing interaction between language models reveals the inadequacies of current risk categories—such as bias, robustness, and misuse—in capturing vulnerabilities across distributed ecosystems. While it is possible to evaluate a single model, once models communicate, the potential for failure multiplies unpredictably. To address this, we propose the Emergent Systemic Risk Horizon (ESRH), a framework that defines the point where the collective behavior of interacting models becomes unstable—even when individual models appear aligned.
