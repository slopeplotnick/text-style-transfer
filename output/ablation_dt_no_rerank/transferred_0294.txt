We propose a method to extract semantic triples from images using multimodal LLMs, aligning them with domain-specific ontologies to build interpretable knowledge graphs (KGs). Moreover, we introduce a graph-based influence analysis pipeline that compares generated and training images via graph representations, enabling human-understandable explanations of similarity. This approach is particularly useful in settings involving copyright attribution, interpretability, and dataset transparency—highlighting its potential to enhance traceability in generative models. We evaluate our method on locally trained models and, furthermore, through a style-specific experiment on large-scale models, focusing on the recently popular Ghibli-style generations. This assessment aims to demonstrate our method’s ability to detect stylistic influence, even under black-box conditions. The structure of this paper is organized as follows: Section 2 reviews related work on interpretability in generative models and the construction of knowledge graphs using advanced language models. Section 3 elucidates our proposed method. Section 4 describes our experimental setups and results across both local and large-scale generative models. Finally, Section 5 synthesizes key findings and outlines future directions. As generative models evolve, the need for interpretability has grown across technical, ethical, and policy domains. General methods such as t-SNE, LIME, and attention visualization provide insights into model behavior; however, they do not directly address training data attribution, a critical concern in contexts involving authorship and copyright. To better understand how training data influences outputs, various attribution techniques have been proposed. Early methods include influence functions, retraining-based attribution, and Shapley value approximations. Despite their efficacy in established domains such as classification tasks, these approaches are often impractical for large-scale models due to their reliance on gradient calculations, retraining capabilities, or a high computational cost. Consequently, they are infrequently applied in modern deep learning pipelines, especially in generative models where internal parameters are inaccessible.
