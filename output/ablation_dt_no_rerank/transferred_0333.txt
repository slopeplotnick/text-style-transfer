In recent years, brain-computer interfaces (BCIs) have garnered significant attention as invasive and non-invasive tools for enabling direct communication between the brain and external connected devices and digital environments. This interest spans both the scientific and medical communities, as well as mainstream media. BCIs are multidisciplinary systems designed to enable individuals with severe motor disabilities, such as locked-in syndrome, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, spinal cord injury, or severe movement or speech disorders following strokes, to communicate and sometimes even regain movement, despite these conditions. Therefore, benchmarking a broader spectrum of EEG-derived features has become essential to improving the performance and accuracy of non-invasive BCIs. Feature extraction techniques are highly diverse and play a critical role in EEG analysis, with approaches ranging from one-dimensional methods, such as time-domain, frequency-domain, and decomposition-domain features, to multi-dimensional techniques. Moreover, transforming raw EEG signals into meaningful representations, including time-domain statistics, frequency-domain features like power spectral density, and time-frequency methods such as wavelet transforms, is crucial for accurate signal classification and robust system performance. Importantly, cross-user generalization relies on diverse and transferable features, underscoring that non-stationarity and dataset shifts in EEG-based emotion recognition require transfer learning and domain generalization strategies built upon robust feature representations. An open and critical question remains: Which combinations of feature-extraction and classification techniques yield truly robust and generalizable performance, especially given substantial individual variability? Answering this necessitates evaluation protocols that minimize optimistic bias. In practice, this means using participant-independent validation strategies, such as leave-one-subject-out (LOSO) cross-validation, employing performance metrics robust to class imbalances like balanced accuracy and AUROC, assessing statistical significance via nonparametric or permutation testing, and rigorously guarding against data leakage. This involves ensuring that operations like filter estimation and feature normalization are confined strictly to the training folds and never informed by the test data.
