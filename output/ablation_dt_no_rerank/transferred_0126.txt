Position Bias. Despite the advanced capabilities of language models in linguistic processing, recent research has highlighted their susceptibility to the structural arrangement of input data, commonly referred to as position bias. Position bias describes the tendency of models to disproportionately prioritize information that appears at particular positions within the input sequence, thereby neglecting critical details elsewhere. In the context of multi-document summarization (MDS), where models handle multiple long documents concatenated into a single context, this bias implies that the likelihood of information being included in the summary can depend significantly on its placement within the context window. Consequently, LLM-generated summaries may exhibit skewed perspectives or imbalanced content coverage depending on the input order. One illustration of this issue is the Lost in the Middle phenomenon. Studies have demonstrated that language models display a characteristic U-shaped performance curve in multi-document question answering and key-value retrieval tasks, where accuracy peaks when salient information is placed at the beginning (primacy) or end (recency) of the context, but substantially deteriorates when such information resides in the middle. While this analysis primarily focuses on tasks with short, limited-token outputs, related research indicates that analogous structural biases are evident in annotation settings. Additionally, when LLMs are employed as evaluators, a strong positional bias is observed, with a systematic inclination to favor the first or last option in a sequence, even when the best response is situated elsewhere. More specifically, this position bias is often linked to the lead bias, which emphasizes the initial segments of the input disproportionately. While some models, such as GPT-3.5-Turbo, exhibit relatively mild lead bias, others demonstrate considerable sensitivity to earlier-positioned content, particularly in extreme summarization tasks. Beyond degrading performance, such positional sensitivity raises critical fairness concerns. For example, if tweets are organized by dialect, summaries may disproportionately favor the group appearing first. This underscores the profound effect input ordering can have on the outcomes generated by these models.
