Multi-agent autonomy emerges from the integration of large language models with orchestration layers, shared memories, and external tools. Frameworks like AutoGen and CAMEL establish role-based dialogues—planner, critic, executor—within a unified environment. Subsequently, newer systems such as SWE-agent and Voyager enhance this framework by facilitating continuous learning loops wherein agents generate tasks, evaluate results, and adapt strategies. This dynamic is not a fixed sequence but rather an adaptive ecology; each model's output reshapes the context for the next, creating feedback that accumulates over time. Despite human involvement ostensibly being part of the loop, their authority remains delayed, indirect, and circumscribed. Conventional safety assessments assume behavior is both observable and interpretable; however, these assumptions falter in expansive multi-agent environments. Communication often lacks structure, manifesting in free-form natural language, JSON payloads, or API calls with no semantic clarity. As meaning becomes endogenous to the network, traceability declines not due to a lack of data but because interpretation itself proves unstable. Therefore, technical solutions increasingly address this visibility loss. Multi-agent sandboxes now record interaction graphs and calculate metrics such as divergence, semantic coherence, influence centrality, and consensus rate to characterize system-level behavior. Similarly, the Inspect framework of the UK AI Security Institute embeds provenance metadata within each message, allowing for post hoc reconstruction of causal chains. These adjustments represent a shift from merely verifying outputs to a nuanced examination of information flow through the network. As systems scale, the concept of autonomy evolves. While traditionally understood as executing actions without direct human directives in supervised settings, autonomy in multi-agent systems acquires a relational dimension: agents coordinate, compete, and infer intentions of their counterparts. Moreover, even when initialized with identical objectives, minor discrepancies in reward structures or contextual frameworks can catalyze polarization, collusion, or runaway escalation. Therefore, autonomy ceases to be merely an attribute of individual components; instead, it becomes an intrinsic characteristic of the network as a whole.
