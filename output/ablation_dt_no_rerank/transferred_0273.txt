CAPO is designed to leverage advantage as a dynamic signal, aligning with the progressive enhancement in the model's competence. Historically, curriculum learning, introduced by Bengio et al. in 2009, relied mainly on static heuristics, such as arranging tasks from simple to complex. Over time, adaptive strategies attempted to evolve through expert feedback or model performance evaluations, yet these assessments remained external and heuristic, dependent on manually defined indicators instead of signals integral to the model’s growth. This limitation prompted the creation of CAPO, which uses advantage estimates as an intrinsic signal to effectively guide curriculum scheduling tailored to the model's evolving capabilities. Extensive empirical evaluations show that CAPO consistently improves mathematical problem-solving across various advantage-based reinforcement learning algorithms, including GRPO, PPO, RLOO, and Reinforce++. Furthermore, CAPO exhibits strong modality generalization, achieving significant improvements in GUI-based reasoning tasks, highlighting its versatility as a curriculum paradigm effective across multiple RL methods and adaptable to multimodal domains. To summarize, our principal contributions are threefold: First, we introduce advantage as a dynamic curriculum signal and construct CAPO, a two-phase framework that includes imitation and discrimination phases, dynamically adapting to the model's evolving competence. Second, we demonstrate CAPO's broad generalization capabilities, proving its effectiveness across diverse RL algorithms and its adaptability to multimodal reasoning tasks, particularly within GUI-based environments. CAPO, as depicted in Figure 2, is an advantage-based compatible mechanism. Once samples are generated by the policy model, various algorithms compute advantage estimates, which are then seamlessly unified within our curriculum advantage framework. By leveraging these signals as strategic guidance, CAPO meticulously organizes training into an imitation phase followed by a discrimination phase, ensuring early stability while facilitating improved generalization as more challenging samples are introduced.
