The anonymity of online interactions often lowers social restraint and amplifies expressions of anger or frustration, while the dynamics of group identity can further polarize discussions. Although computational methods have progressed in detecting toxic behavior or flagging abusive content, they remain largely reactive, focusing on moderation through removal or punitive actions rather than prevention or repair. Consequently, online platforms can suppress harmful speech temporarily but fail to address the underlying social dynamics that allow hostility to resurface. Our work proposes a novel approach: mediation rather than mere moderation. Instead of only identifying harmful discourse, we explore whether a language model can track and de-escalate conflicts. We perceive language models as potential mediators capable of interpreting multi-turn conversations, assessing the fairness and relevance of arguments, and generating responses that acknowledge concerns, reduce hostility, and guide exchanges toward a more constructive path. Achieving this goal requires reasoning about context, emotion, escalation patterns, and conversational norms. Therefore, our task is divided into two complementary components: the first involves identifying injustices and emotional triggers, and the second focuses on generating conciliatory discourse that calms the exchange and redirects focus to substantive issues rather than conflict. To assess LLMs' capabilities as mediators, we compiled a large-scale dataset of real-world flame wars from Reddit and developed a multi-stage evaluation pipeline. Our framework integrates three perspectives: principle-based evaluation that grounds mediation quality in fine-grained conversational principles; user simulation that models post-intervention behavior to assess whether LLM-generated mediations reduce hostility; and human comparative assessment that benchmarks model interventions against human-written mediations. Extensive experiments reveal that API-based models consistently outperform open-source systems in both judgment and steering tasks, demonstrating greater coherence and stability. Moreover, mediation analysis indicates that LLMs effectively reduce toxicity and emotional intensity in simulated interactions, although a comparative assessment highlights their strengths in neutrality and structural precision, alongside relative deficiencies in readability and human-like engagement.
