We present Menta, a refined model specifically tailored for the prediction of mental health issues from social media text, optimized for both precision and usability. Menta leverages a weighted-loss, LoRA-based multi-task training framework designed to address data imbalance and enhance generalizability across diverse clinical classification tasks. We demonstrate that Menta achieves a compelling balance between predictive accuracy and computational efficiency, surpassing several models and performing comparably to large language models across six mental health tasks. Additionally, our open-source deployment pipeline and model implementation enable on-device inference, ensuring privacy maintenance and optimized resource utilization for mental health applications. Historically, mental health prediction using textual data from social media relied on conventional machine learning methods and models requiring extensive manual configuration. For example, Jiang et al. (2020) employed advanced contextualized word embeddings, such as ELMo and BERT, to identify mental health conditions from Reddit posts, showing that these deep contextualized models significantly outperform traditional bag-of-words approaches or static embeddings for mental health classification. Similarly, Sarkar et al. (2022) developed a multi-task learning framework to concurrently predict depression and anxiety from Reddit posts, outperforming single-task models by leveraging shared features across related conditions. Other studies have constructed scalable deep-learning tools for suicide risk screening with the potential for early intervention strategies. Recently, advancements in this domain have seen large language models being employed to enhance detection capabilities. Notably, an investigation utilized ChatGPT for zero-shot classification across stress, depression, and suicidality within social media datasets, validating the capability of large language models for mental health prediction without prior knowledge. Furthermore, research focusing on depression detection through online content corroborates that fine-tuning these models results in superior performance compared to previous models. Moreover, efforts to explore the interpretability of these models in predicting depressive symptoms and emotional states have shown promising results, particularly when prompts with emotional cues and illustrative examples are employed to improve performance.
