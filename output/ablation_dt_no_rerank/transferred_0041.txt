Such findings underscore the significance of individuals' pre-existing attitudes and expectations. For example, Hong et al. (2021) showed that a more favorable attitude toward creative AI correlated with higher music ratings in a study that examined expectancies of AI music across various genres. Interestingly, expectancy violations led to diminished evaluations, although differences between music genres were not substantial. Another central question revolves around whether listeners perceive AI-composed music as indistinguishable from human-created compositions. This study aims to clarify which musical attributes contribute to perceptions of music as either human or AI-produced. According to Shank et al. (2023), the genre is a key factor, with electronic music often being attributed to AI composers. Notably, when participants were misled about the source of the music, they perceived musical differences that did not actually exist, suggesting that expectations and beliefs about human origin significantly shape aesthetic judgments, as reported by Ansani et al. (2024). Similarly, Déguernel et al. (2022) identified specific evaluative strategies where participants discerned AI-generated music by noting features like excessive repetition, rigid structure, or atypical harmonic and melodic elements. Conversely, human compositions were associated with melodic variation, ornamentation, and familiar stylistic elements that suggest instrumental playability. Such associations illustrate a broader pattern where musical features perceived as "human" receive more favorable evaluations, whereas those linked to AI are viewed less positively. In line with this, Hong et al. (2022) investigated the influence of perceived anthropomorphism and creative autonomy on the acceptance of AI as musicians. Participants assessed AI-generated music across various genres, evaluating the quality of compositions alongside their attitudes toward AI. The results indicated that increased anthropomorphism and perceived autonomy were linked to greater acceptance of AI musicians. Furthermore, Sun et al. (2023) demonstrated that anthropomorphism affects perceptions of AI musicians' competence and warmth, both of which contribute to more positive attitudes.
