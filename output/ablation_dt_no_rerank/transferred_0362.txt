Early demonstrations of self-play-based learning were epitomized by AlphaZero, which autonomously strengthens its strategy through self-competition, devoid of external supervision. Recently, this principle has been adapted to language models, culminating in systems that progressively enhance their reasoning capabilities over time. Moreover, several systems utilize language self-play at scale, where weaker language models benefit from this approach by autonomously generating and solving tasks, thus circumventing the need for large labeled datasets. This method proves particularly advantageous in verifiable domains like code generation, wherein a "Coder" agent's program undergoes verification by a "Tester" agent's unit tests. Fully data-free methods, such as R-Zero, further advance this concept by proposing questions, attempting solutions, and acquiring insights solely from internal verification signals. Conversely, other approaches begin with a small set of examples, subsequently learning to synthesize increasingly complex problems. R-Few, while sharing conceptual foundations with earlier systems, diverges in its supervisory approach, aiming to preserve the benefits of exploration inherent in self-evolving language models while mitigating concept drift and diversity collapse. Furthermore, reinforcement learning paradigms are pivotal in refining the cognitive processes of language models, extending their competencies beyond mere exemplar-based learning. Initially, frameworks like InstructGPT demonstrated that optimizing human preference significantly enhances models' safety and helpfulness, often surpassing example-based learning in reasoning capabilities. Subsequent systems, such as DeepSeek-R1, have leveraged this principle, employing reward systems to bolster multi-step problem-solving skills. Lastly, related frameworks indicate that crafting objectives tailored to extended and intricate tasks can considerably enrich reasoning abilities.
