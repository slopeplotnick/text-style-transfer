Empirical testing demonstrates that the order in which articles are presented significantly affects the produced summaries. Specifically, these models display a pronounced preference for the first article in the sequence, shaping both the framing and semantic similarity of the summaries. Previous studies have documented this tendency in models dealing with extensive input sequences. Our findings extend this phenomenon to scenarios involving multiple documents, evidencing a systematic prioritization of the first article during information ranking. This preference is consistently demonstrated by BERTScore, owing to the model's tendency to employ the vocabulary and arguments of the initial article more extensively than those of subsequent articles. Consequently, assessments of semantic similarity reliably yield higher scores for the first article. However, metrics such as ROUGE-L, which focus on lexical overlap, do not reflect this tendency as strongly, and SummaC, which evaluates factual consistency, does not emphasize framing aspects. Therefore, these alternative metrics reveal a less pronounced bias compared to BERTScore. Across all evaluative methodologies, the first article emerges as more prominent relative to the second and third, while the distinction between the latter two articles remains negligible. This finding underscores the model's inclination to prioritize the initial article, a phenomenon not attributed to diminishing engagement with subsequent articles over time. Such bias holds significant implications for real-world applications, particularly for tools synthesizing information for end-users. For instance, AI-generated summaries in Google Search, utilizing Gemini models, may prioritize the initial articles processed, potentially disregarding subsequent ones. Consequently, this bias can lead to summaries that disproportionately represent certain perspectives, thereby influencing user comprehension, especially when there is substantial reliance on AI outputs.
