Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models have exhibited remarkable advancements in robotic manipulation tasks, benefiting greatly from large-scale, multimodal datasets collected through human teleoperation or systematically scripted procedures. During the pretraining phase, these models apply methodologies such as flow-matching and diffusion-based objectives to effectively learn comprehensive multimodal behavioral policies that span diverse manipulation modes. Subsequently, the pretrained VLA model is adapted for downstream applications through supervised fine-tuning on smaller, task-specific datasets. The use of a pretrained VLA model for novel tasks typically yields superior generalization compared to training a model from scratch, due to its extensive pretrained knowledge in areas such as visual perception, language comprehension, spatial awareness, and object recognition. However, despite their generally strong performance, VLA models exhibit a critical vulnerability during practical deployment. This fragility is attributed to the persistence of redundant action modes that do not contribute to task success after fine-tuning. During pretraining, VLA models assimilate a wide array of action modes from diverse sources, making it challenging to narrow their output distribution to the actions pertinent to a specific task. Consequently, even after supervised fine-tuning, these models may retain extraneous action modes. Furthermore, the datasets used for fine-tuning can introduce variability in stylistic attributes due to their diverse origins, as they are collected from various methodologies and operators, some of which might not align with optimal adaptation paradigms. This discrepancy between the intended actions and those executed by the VLA model is evident during task implementation, highlighting a divergence between expected and actual performance.
