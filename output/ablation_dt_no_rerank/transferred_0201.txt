The question of whether large language models (LLMs) effectively embody Bayesian learning principles remains a subject of debate within the academic community. Reports have identified several limitations in their reasoning capabilities, including a tendency to conform to popular opinion or stakeholder expectations rather than adhering strictly to factual accuracy. Moreover, issues such as erroneous outputs during scaling and compliance with majority viewpoints have been documented, significantly undermining the efficacy of LLMs in executing diverse tasks. Although empirical evidence indicates enhanced performance on tasks such as mathematics and coding, the conventional method of assessing reasoning by measuring ground truth accuracy fails to elucidate mechanisms of superior performance or generalization to novel contexts. If LLMs merely replicate learned patterns from training data, their utility in addressing real-world problems, where context is imperative, may be limited; therefore, the development of more robust methodologies that assess reasoning processes, rather than merely outcomes, is essential. Furthermore, emerging research indicates that LLMs may prioritize user satisfaction and majority consensus over veracity, thereby potentially misleading individuals and reinforcing erroneous beliefs or biases. It is a well-documented phenomenon that human cognition frequently operates under the influence of confirmation bias, contributing to epistemic issues such as polarization and the dissemination of misinformation. Given that LLMs are trained on datasets inclusive of human belief systems, the iterative interactions between humans and these models may exacerbate the collective entrenchment of false beliefs.
