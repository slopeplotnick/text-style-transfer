This study addresses the task of unsupervised fraud detection in multi-type journal entry data. Labels for fraudulent activity are scarce and often unavailable, rendering supervised learning techniques impractical at scale. Consequently, our objective is to identify anomalous entries by modeling irregular patterns across both structured and unstructured fields, reflecting real-world auditing conditions. To achieve this, we utilize a synthetic accounting dataset, introduced by Gronewald et al. (2024), which simulates the bookkeeping practices of a medium-sized enterprise. Each entry in this dataset includes features like a unique posting ID, date and time of posting and transaction, credit or debit flags, tax rate, and account ID. In addition, we benchmark our method using real-world anonymized accounting data from our industry partner. Unlike conventional double-entry bookkeeping systems where posting IDs link balanced debit-credit cycles, this anonymized dataset lacks such identifiers, disrupting typical transactional structures. Therefore, we approach it as a transaction-level fraud detection problem. To mitigate the lack of context, we enhance the large language model (LLM) prompts with bookkeeping heuristics, guidelines, and summary statistics, proving essential to surpass traditional machine learning baselines. Given the importance of preserving privacy when handling sensitive financial data, we prioritized open-source LLMs like LLaMa AI (2025) and Gemma Team (2024) over proprietary models such as GPT-4 from OpenAI (2024) during our benchmarking. Our method outperforms baseline methods, as evidenced by its superior performance on both the synthetic data from Gronewald et al. and the real-world anonymized dataset. We employ Isolation Forest, introduced by Liu et al. (2008), as our baseline due to its theoretical effectiveness in modeling tabular data and its suitability as an unsupervised anomaly detection method, which aligns with our proposed task. This method's efficacy is demonstrated through its performance on both synthetic and anonymized real-world datasets.
