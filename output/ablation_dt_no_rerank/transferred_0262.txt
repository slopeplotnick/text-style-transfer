To govern such systems, safety evaluation must shift from basic compliance checks to comprehensive system-level observation. This involves measuring the propagation of influence among agents, whether oversight signals successfully penetrate collective behavior, and when feedback mechanisms become uncontrollable. The central proposition here is that while surface-level safety mechanisms are necessary, they are insufficient once models are involved in networks of interaction. Although local conditions may appear ideal, emergent failures can still occur as feedback mechanisms, network topology, and collective incentives start to dominate systemic behavior. Recently, two scholarly contributions have addressed these emergent risks in multi-agent systems, providing insightful frameworks: MAST (Multi-Agent System failure Taxonomy) and MARAI (Multi-Agent Risks from Advanced AI). MAST categorizes failures into three distinct classifications, focusing on risks directly arising from the system’s architecture—specifically, system design issues, agent coordination, and verification processes. Conversely, MARAI delineates three failure types based on inter-agent interaction modalities—miscoordination, conflict, and collusion—that might result in misaligned behaviors. Building on these, our novel contribution involves: 1) organizing both approaches into a structured, system-centered framework, advancing from agent-level failures to risks that cannot be attributed to individual system components; 2) formalizing this through the Emergent Systemic Risk Horizon (ESRH), marking the point where localized reliability transitions into collective instability. The ESRH provides a vocabulary and a set of empirical indicators for examining LLM ecosystems as complex adaptive systems, rather than isolated agents. This reframing establishes a foundation for the ensuing discourse. Subsequently, Section 3 articulates the ESRH taxonomy and its analytical metrics.
