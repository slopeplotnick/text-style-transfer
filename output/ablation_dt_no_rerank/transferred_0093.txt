Meanwhile, a growing line of research has delved into understanding the training and inference behaviors of diffusion models, with a specific focus on the balance between memorization (i.e., the tendency to replicate training samples) and generalization (i.e., the ability to generate novel samples). Earlier studies have approached this issue from various angles, such as by establishing quantitative metrics to characterize model behavior and by analyzing the factors that drive memorization or generalization. Notably, most of these works primarily: (1) examine model behaviors when initiating sampling from scratch, and (2) validate their hypotheses on smaller-scale datasets like FFHQ and CIFAR-10. While these insights are useful in low-data scenarios, such memorization behavior becomes less likely as the data scales to the size of ImageNet or beyond. For instance, Song et al. observed a divergence in memorization and generalization abilities in ImageNet-scale diffusion models when resuming sampling at different stages. Generally, starting sampling from an earlier timestep leads to novel samples, while later timesteps tend to replicate training images. Therefore, a critical question arises: what principles govern the balance between memorization and generalization in diffusion models trained on extensive datasets? To address this, we track the origins of model behavior to its training objectives. We revisit flow matching and conditional flow matching, typically seen as complex due to the lack of specific data. However, we demonstrate that a Gaussian configuration alongside a limited training dataset allows us to determine the flow dynamics effectively. This enables us to compute the optimal trajectory for the model at any stage. By examining this trajectory, we identify two primary phases in the training process. In the initial stage (navigation), the model draws on information from multiple data points to set its course, while in the later stage (refinement), the focus shifts to a single data point. Moreover, our investigation highlights that the size of the dataset and sample are crucial in determining when this transition occurs.
