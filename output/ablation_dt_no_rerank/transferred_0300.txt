Focusing on the potential parallels between neural and artificial intelligence systems, particularly the "world model" construct, we delve into this framework's role in prediction and understanding. Consequently, our analysis of information processing mechanisms in both the brain and artificial intelligence systems reveals a convergent evolution, notably evident in large-scale language models. Building on this observation, we propose a novel theoretical framework regarding macro-scale information processing within the brain, where the neocortex and cerebellum develop predictive world models by forecasting future events, assimilating knowledge from prediction inaccuracies, and utilizing these models to achieve a variety of functions such as sensory comprehension and motor control. Moreover, this conceptual framework integrates earlier theories about brain functionality, including the internal model theory and the mirror neuron system, suggesting the necessity of such systems for executing diverse tasks under a unified structural configuration. This approach has the potential to unify research in neuroscience and artificial intelligence, thereby elucidating mechanisms that underpin human-like intelligence. Additionally, the cerebellum acts as a predictor by processing external information, anticipating future events, and refining models through correction of prediction errors. Empirical studies support that the cerebellum primarily learns to anticipate future sensory inputs rather than high-level signals.
