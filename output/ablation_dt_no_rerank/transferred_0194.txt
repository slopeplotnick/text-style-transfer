This approach can be applied across various domains: geologists describe conceptual models through diagrams presented in academic papers, mathematicians then transform these models into constraints on the prior probability model, and geophysicists use these constraints to conduct Bayesian inversion. Another example to consider is the inclusion of additional data, especially since the initial conceptual hypothesis might be entirely incorrect. This problem is part of a broader scientific challenge: how can we prove that our assumptions are correct? The Austrian philosopher Karl Popper, who devoted his life to questions of this nature, believed that science should not involve induction, where theories are derived from observations. Instead, he argued that theories should be seen as speculative constructs devised by humans to address gaps in existing theories. Once proposed, these theories need to be rigorously tested against empirical observations. Theories that fail to align with observations should be rejected or falsified, while those that survive are considered our best scientific frameworks. In terms of uncertainty quantification, models can be viewed not as true representations of reality but as hypotheses. Each model represents a different hypothesis. While these hypotheses are constrained by existing knowledge, empirical data should not confirm a model’s validity but instead serve to falsify it when discrepancies with observed data occur. Popper offers a way to partially address the subjectivity inherent in Bayesian reasoning—a form of inductive reasoning—by contrasting it with the deductive method of falsification. If the prior distribution fails to predict the current data within the margin of error, the Bayesian prior is effectively falsified. This means that no matter how many models are tested within the prior framework, none aligns with the empirical data. Although Popper's idea may diminish the subjectivity in the prior after testing, he does not provide a method for revising the prior, leaving that responsibility to human discretion.
