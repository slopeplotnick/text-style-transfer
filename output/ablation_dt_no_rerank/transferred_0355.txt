We conduct extensive experiments on a diverse set of benchmarks, including machine translation evaluation (WMT23) and reward model assessment (Reward Bench 2). Our results demonstrate that the distribution-calibrated approach we devised considerably outperforms a suite of strong self-consistency baselines. By carefully modeling the entire vote distribution, our method transforms noisy individual model judgments into more reliable ratings, matching or even exceeding the performance of individual human raters when evaluated against a human-consensus gold standard. Our main contributions are threefold: First, we show that existing aggregation methods for inference time computations with LLM judges are suboptimal, highlighting the need for a meticulously designed aggregation approach. Second, we propose an aggregation method based on Expected Risk Minimization (ERM) combined with a Bradley-Terry-Davidson model, which is fit on a small calibration set. This approach consistently outperforms current aggregation methodologies across different tasks in both reward benchmarks and machine translation. Third, in the context of machine translation, we adopt a consensus-based meta-evaluation to generate higher-fidelity ground truths in situations where labels are noisy. This enables a fair comparison to human raters and reveals scenarios where LLM judges approach or surpass "super-human" evaluation quality. Recently, Large Language Models (LLMs) have achieved significant success as "judges" to evaluate generated text, offering a scalable alternative to traditional metrics and demonstrating high correlation with human judgments across diverse domains. Some approaches directly prompt general-purpose LLMs, such as G-Eval or JudgeLM, while others fine-tune specialized models optimized for evaluation tasks, like Prometheus and Auto-J. Despite their robustness, LLM-based approaches face challenges including sensitivity to prompt design and inherent biases, such as positional bias or morbidity bias. Moreover, LLM judges exhibit variability in decision-making, with some models showing more aggressiveness in resolving subtle distinctions or ties. Our work focuses on mitigating this variability and enhancing the consistency of judgments through a principled aggregation approach.
