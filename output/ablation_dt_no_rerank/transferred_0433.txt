Verifier Gain is a newly introduced metric aimed at providing a more comprehensive understanding of a verifier's effectiveness during testing. Verifier accuracy alone does not fully capture this effectiveness; thus, Verifier Gain is derived to simulate the improvements obtained from a verifier through test-time rejection sampling. Our empirical analysis of rejection sampling with verifiers demonstrates that this theoretical framework closely mirrors real-world performance trends. When exploring self-improvement, intra-group improvement, and inter-group improvement, our findings reveal that inter-group verification often proves most advantageous, especially when contrasted with self-verification. This difference is attributed to the similarity in solution distributions between the solver and the verifier; as these distributions become more alike, the Verifier Gain diminishes. Consequently, as models enhance in size, undergo more training, or achieve higher accuracy, they tend to be less effective as self-verifiers but improve significantly as inter-group verifiers. In terms of dataset verifiability, we examined whether tasks that are easy to solve are also easy to verify and determined that verification accuracy generally aligns with solver accuracy. Despite this, self-verification yields minimal Verifier Gain across all tasks. We also noted that tasks involving mathematical or logical reasoning consistently result in higher Verifier Gains. Broadly, verifiers operate either at the outcome level, assessing the correctness of the final answer, or at the process level, evaluating the accuracy of intermediate reasoning steps. Several researchers, like Weng et al. (2023), Wu et al. (2024), and Jiang et al. (2024), have developed outcome-level self-verification methods by predicting parts of a question based on the solution. To decrease hallucinations, Dhuliawala et al. (2024) introduced language models that fact-check their own outputs by generating fact-check questions. Meanwhile, other researchers have trained general-purpose outcome-level verifiers, such as those by Hosseini et al. (2024), Zhang et al. (2025), and Cobbe et al. (2021), either independently or in joint efforts with the solver, as seen in the works by Shen et al. (2021) and Sareen et al. (2025). Additionally, process-level verification has been a focal point for those interested in verifying the procedural steps deductively.
