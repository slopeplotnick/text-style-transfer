The threat model examines the aftermath of utilizing a malicious tool by an agent, focusing on the implications of such usage. It is anticipated that the tool’s code might be inspected or reviewed; however, due to the inherent similarities between malicious and benign actions, the concealed backdoor remains undetected. In scenarios involving cloud-based or hybrid systems, users are unable to observe the internal mechanisms of tools, thereby limiting their assessment to observable outcomes. The attacker operates within permissible boundaries without attempting to gain elevated privileges or interfere with memory processes. The attack achieves success under two conditions: first, it accomplishes the attacker’s intended objectives, and second, it ensures the agent continues to perform its designated tasks effectively, with only minimal deviations. The primary concern associated with this threat pertains to financial implications, as the misuse of computational resources, such as API tokens or GPU time, results in significant costs. When multiple tools engage in such activity, the cumulative resource expenditure can become substantial. This section introduces the LeechHijack attack, beginning with an overview of its operational principles. Subsequently, the process of embedding a hidden backdoor within a compliant tool is elucidated, followed by an explanation of how this backdoor establishes a covert communication channel and compels the agent to perform additional tasks. Finally, the criteria governing the effectiveness of the attack and the measurement methodologies employed are outlined. The LeechHijack attack is executed through a two-step process. Initially, the attacker deploys a seemingly secure tool embedded with a dormant backdoor, which remains inactive during regular usage and refrains from exhibiting any malicious behavior. In the subsequent phase, the backdoor is activated in response to a specific trigger, enabling the tool to generate altered outputs that prompt the agent to undertake supplementary tasks benefiting the attacker. Upon completion of this phase, the tool resumes normal functionality, concealing any indicators of malicious activity from the user.
