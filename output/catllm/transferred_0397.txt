The development of communication through systems involving multiple agents has been extensively investigated, with particular emphasis on the potential mechanisms underlying the evolution of language. A prominent example is the Talking Heads project, initiated by Steels (2000, 2015), which illustrates how language can emerge as agents engage in interactions wherein they initially refer to environmental objects without the use of language. Through such interactions, agents generate an artificial language by participating in activities such as naming objects within their environment (Steels, 2001; see also Steels and Kaplan, 2001; Steels and Loetzch, 2012 for further insights into naming conventions). Subsequent research within the project has examined the emergence and acquisition of syntax, or sentence structure, as demonstrated in studies by Steels and Beuls (2017), Steels et al. (2018), and van Trijp (2016). A notable distinction between this project and the MODOMA project lies in the collaborative nature of language creation and learning in the Talking Heads framework, as opposed to the single-agent focus on the acquisition of natural language fragments in MODOMA. Furthermore, the language learned by the Talking Heads agents is artificial rather than natural. In parallel, Alishahi has conducted investigations concerning language acquisition through the application of algorithms to datasets that are either human-generated or artificially constructed. These investigations (Alishahi and Chrupala, 2009, 2012; Alishahi and Stevenson, 2008) have concentrated on the learning of word categories, semantic representations, and fundamental syntactic structures. Matusevych et al. (2013) contributed to this field by refining artificial input that simulates child-adult interactions, drawing on data analogous to that from the CHILDES database (MacWhinney, 2014), although their language-learning algorithm does not actively participate in conversational exchanges, contrasting with the MODOMA project. Beekhuizen et al. (2014) adopted an alternative methodology by examining the acquisition of grammatical patterns through a single-agent system designed to process sentence representations and their corresponding meanings. Additionally, the Baby SRL project, as explored by Conner et al. (2008, 2009), has focused on the capacity of algorithms to learn semantic role labeling of words in sentences, utilizing data derived from either the CHILDES database or artificially constructed sentences.
