Video language models are increasingly utilized in applications such as AI-powered glasses and domestic robotic systems, necessitating their capability to process extensive video content that may span several hours or even days. Recent advancements have introduced memory-based mechanisms to extract salient segments of video data, thereby enabling the models to focus on critical information without the computational burden of analyzing every individual frame. This approach is not only more cost-efficient but also demonstrates superior performance compared to methods requiring the processing of all video frames, which are computationally prohibitive. Current methodologies predominantly rely on textual representations, converting video segments into captions or summaries to facilitate information retrieval and comprehension. While a minority of approaches incorporate visual data, their application is primarily limited to object recognition rather than broader analytical purposes. Moreover, existing models typically operate by selecting a predetermined number of video clips, often three clips of 30 seconds each, to inform their outputs. Such a configuration presents two significant limitations. First, it inadequately integrates video visual data with textual memory for the purposes of information retrieval and generation. The capacity to extract fine-grained visual details is particularly critical for tasks involving feature recognition, spatial understanding, or scene comprehension, which cannot be fully captured through textual descriptions alone. Second, the simultaneous use of both captions and video frames during response generation may overwhelm the model, as excessive visual information can lead to cognitive overload. Consequently, a more sophisticated mechanism is required to dynamically optimize the balance between textual and visual data in response to specific queries, a research area that remains underexplored.
