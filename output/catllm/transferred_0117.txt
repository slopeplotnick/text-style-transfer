The integration of large language models with orchestration layers, shared memories, and external tools facilitates the development of multi-agent autonomy. Frameworks such as AutoGen and CAMEL establish role-based dialogues involving planners, critics, and executors, all collaborating within the same domain. Novel systems, including SWE-agent and Voyager, extend this concept by creating iterative loops wherein agents generate tasks, evaluate outcomes, and modify strategies. This approach is not merely a static process but rather a dynamic configuration, wherein the output of each model alters the context for subsequent models, thereby generating feedback that accumulates progressively. Although human involvement is anticipated, their control is often delayed, indirect, and limited. Conventional safety protocols assume observability and comprehensibility of behavior; however, such assumptions are challenged in extensive multi-agent configurations. Communication frequently occurs in unstructured forms, such as free-form natural language, JSON payloads, or API calls lacking explicit meaning. When meaning is derived internally from the network, tracing becomes problematic due to unstable interpretation rather than missing data. Technical interventions aim to address this opacity. Multi-agent sandboxes are now employed to log interaction graphs and assess metrics such as divergence, semantic coherence, influence centrality, and consensus rate to comprehend system behavior. The Inspect framework, developed by the UK AI Security Institute, performs a comparable function by appending metadata to each message to facilitate subsequent tracing of causal chains. These advancements indicate a shift from output verification to examining the movement of information within the network. As systems expand, the concept of autonomy evolves. In supervised configurations, autonomy was characterized by the capacity to act without explicit human directives. In multi-agent systems, autonomy pertains to the interplay of agents: collaborating, competing, and inferring each other's intentions. Even when initiating with identical objectives, minor variations in rewards or context may result in polarization, collusion, or runaway escalation. Autonomy is no longer merely a characteristic of individual components; rather, it emerges from the network itself.
