Previous research has demonstrated that smaller models do not necessarily exhibit a smooth degradation in performance when their size is reduced, and our results corroborate this assertion. Furthermore, it was revealed that a smaller model, MentalQLM (0.5B), effectively predicts mental health issues such as depression, stress, and suicidal thoughts. Therefore, this indicates that smaller, fine-tuned models can achieve satisfactory performance for mental health predictions, balancing computational efficiency with predictive accuracy. Other studies have similarly indicated that these models maintain their reasoning and question-answering capabilities post fine-tuning, thereby enabling them to manage diverse tasks while performing well in mental health predictions. It was discovered that models possessing 3-7 billion parameters strike a commendable balance between efficiency and accuracy upon fine-tuning. Specifically, smaller models exhibit a reduced capacity to adapt to tasks, whereas models within this parameter range demonstrate proficiency in classifying psychological signals in both zero-shot and few-shot scenarios. Consequently, with fine-tuning, our model, Menta, establishes a definitive minimum size for models to perform effectively on complex mental health tasks while retaining efficiency. When comparing smaller fine-tuned models to their larger counterparts, the former perform equivalently or even surpass in predicting depression and stress, although they exhibit inferior performance regarding suicidal thoughts. However, in contrast to smaller models that have not undergone training, fine-tuned models display significant improvement and maintain a balanced performance across various tasks. The findings demonstrate that well-optimized smaller models are capable of handling the requisite reasoning and contextual elements essential for mental health predictions. These models perform effectively in this domain because mental health tasks often necessitate attention to specific linguistic cues, such as self-references, emotional expressions, or temporal language, which smaller models can discern without requiring the extensive knowledge base inherent in larger models. Earlier research has indicated that fine-tuning on specific text types facilitates bridging the performance gap between smaller and larger models, and our study contributes to this body of knowledge by revealing that efficient fine-tuning methodologies, including LoRA, and specialized loss functions, assist smaller models in acquiring the necessary competencies for mental health predictions while minimizing computational demands.
