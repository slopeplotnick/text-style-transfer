The study investigates a specific category of attack wherein a backdoor may be activated randomly during the utilization of a tool, indicating that the vulnerability is inherent from the outset. Moreover, an advanced variant of this attack is characterized by the tool initially appearing innocuous, as attackers refrain from engaging in malicious activities immediately, allowing the tool to function as intended. Subsequently, once the tool achieves widespread adoption, the attack can be remotely triggered, rendering this delayed approach, akin to supply chain attacks, significantly more pernicious and challenging to trace. Furthermore, the attack methodology can be enhanced in terms of stealth by targeting specific users selectively, where adversaries analyze user details and focus on individuals who frequently utilize the tool, particularly those who successfully pass security validations. This strategy complicates detection during testing phases. The paper introduces a novel attack paradigm termed implicit toxicity, which exploits the legitimate permissions granted to tools in order to cause detrimental effects. To exemplify this, the researchers developed LeechHijack, a mechanism designed to covertly commandeer computational resources of systems through ordinary tool usage. This approach is compatible with any MCP tool provider and infiltrates systems without requiring elevated permissions, utilizing system resources for unintended operations. The findings underscore a fundamental vulnerability in the sharing mechanisms of MCP tools, wherein malicious tools can compromise systems without necessitating additional access privileges. Extensive experimental evaluations conducted across diverse systems demonstrate the efficacy of LeechHijack and its ability to evade existing defensive measures. In addition, the study explores mitigation strategies and establishes a foundational framework for enhancing defenses against such attacks in the future. The research emphasizes the security implications associated with systems employing large language models, advocating for rigorous scrutiny of third-party tools to prevent exploitation. Ethical and security protocols were stringently adhered to throughout the experimentation process, ensuring that the LeechHijack attack was utilized solely for demonstrative purposes and not to promote malicious activities. Importantly, no real systems, platforms, or services were compromised during the study, and all experiments were conducted within secure, controlled environments created exclusively by the researchers, thereby avoiding connections to live servers and preventing the exposure of sensitive data.
