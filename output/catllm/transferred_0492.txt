When concepts are not clearly defined within data features, probing techniques are frequently employed to identify neurons or groups of neurons that most effectively represent target concepts. Such probes are typically linear in nature; however, non-linear probes may be utilized when the concept under investigation is not linearly separable. Probes may be trained through either supervised methods, which require labeled data corresponding to the desired concept, or unsupervised methods, which aim to identify concepts that are significant to the model yet may not necessarily be interpretable by humans. This study extends beyond mere observation to incorporate the modeling of structural equations, addressing varying levels of modeling approaches ranging from Fully Specified Causal Models (FSCMs), which are inherently comprehensible with domain knowledge, to Partially Specified Causal Models (PSCMs), encompassing all FSCMs that align with the data. This distinction underscores the critical importance of selecting an appropriate modeling approach and highlights the utility of partial information in scenarios where comprehensive FSCMs are unavailable. Galhotra et al. (2021) introduced an explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) methodology employing FSCMs to compute probabilities of sufficiency and necessity. Their work predominantly addresses tabular data, integrating causal modeling into data features by presuming these features represent high-level concepts and facilitating the inclusion of additional concepts absent from the model input. The proposed method is effective exclusively when data features correspond to meaningful concepts and does not necessitate the presence of a causal generator. Furthermore, it relies on the assumption that the explanation of model outputs prioritizes identifying input values capable of improving predictions or preserving favorable outcomes when the preferred prediction remains unchanged.
