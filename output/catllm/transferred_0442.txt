Large language models frequently produce inaccuracies despite their seemingly coherent and articulate output, leading to a lack of trust and hindered practical application. To address these issues, two distinct methodologies have been proposed: Hallucination Detection, which focuses on the model's output, and Fact Verification, which involves external factual validation. These approaches, although sharing the objective of enhancing model reliability, have evolved independently, employing disparate theoretical foundations, datasets, and evaluation techniques. This divergence has impeded progress in the field. In the current investigation, an integrative framework termed UniFact has been developed, facilitating direct comparison of the two methodologies through generation of model outputs and subsequent factual assessment. Extensive testing across various models and techniques has revealed three pivotal insights: firstly, neither methodology consistently outperforms the other; secondly, each identifies unique categories of factual inaccuracies; and thirdly, a synergistic application of both approaches yields optimal results. Beyond comparative analysis, the study also explores the rationale behind their divergent development paths and demonstrates the necessity for unification. The findings advocate for a novel strategy to amalgamate Hallucination Detection and Fact Verification within large language models, which have revolutionized the digital landscape by enabling sophisticated searches and performing complex tasks previously deemed unachievable. However, their deployment in critical domains is constrained by a significant challenge: the generation of fluent yet erroneous content, termed hallucination. In sectors such as healthcare, legal practice, and journalism, where accuracy is imperative, such inaccuracies can undermine trust, propagate misinformation, and result in detrimental consequences. Therefore, the development of robust mechanisms for error detection is not merely a technological concern but a prerequisite for the establishment of trustworthy intelligent systems.
