Brain-computer interfaces (BCIs) have recently garnered significant attention as tools enabling direct interaction between the brain and devices or digital systems. These interfaces, which are categorized into invasive and non-invasive types depending on whether surgical procedures are required, have captured the interest of scientists, medical professionals, and media entities alike. Specifically, BCIs serve as critical systems for individuals suffering from severe motor or speech impairments due to conditions such as locked-in syndrome, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), spinal cord injuries, or strokes, facilitating communication and movement capabilities otherwise unattainable. Consequently, enhancing non-invasive BCIs by examining a broader spectrum of features derived from electroencephalogram (EEG) data has become imperative. The extraction of features from EEG signals is pivotal in data analysis, encompassing methods that range from simple temporal or frequency-based approaches to complex techniques integrating multiple dimensions. The transformation of raw EEG signals into actionable information, including time-based statistics, frequency-related attributes such as power spectral density, or hybrid methodologies like wavelet transforms, is crucial for accurate signal classification and system efficacy. Furthermore, it is essential to develop features that exhibit robustness across different subjects, given the substantial inter-individual variability in EEG signals. Challenges such as temporal data shifts or discrepancies across datasets necessitate the application of strategies like transfer learning and domain generalization to ensure feature reliability. A prominent question in this field pertains to identifying optimal combinations of feature-extraction and classification techniques, particularly in light of the considerable diversity in individual EEG profiles. Addressing this issue requires employing evaluation protocols designed to mitigate overly optimistic biases, which include testing on data from subjects not involved in the training phase (e.g., leave-one-subject-out cross-validation), utilizing metrics adept at managing data imbalances (such as balanced accuracy and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve), validating outcomes through statistical tests devoid of stringent assumptions regarding the data, and ensuring the absence of data leakage. For instance, processes such as data filtering or normalization should be exclusively applied to training data, avoiding any application to the test data.
