Agentic systems, akin to contemporary software configurations, comprise various components functioning collaboratively, exhibiting interfaces, and being organized through software processes. These systems are distinguished from conventional programs due to their stochastic and adaptable nature in addressing problems. Typically, evaluation of these systems is based on their eventual success or failure, which neglects an understanding of their cognitive, planning, action, or methodological evolution over time. Consequently, GRAPHECTORY was developed to delineate the temporal and semantic connections within these systems, facilitating the creation of metrics to evaluate and analyze their efficacy beyond mere success. Utilizing GRAPHECTORY, an examination was conducted on 4000 trajectories of two predominant agentic programming methodologies, namely SWE-agent and OpenHands, employing four principal Large Language Models (LLMs) in an effort to resolve SWE-bench Verified issues. Our automated assessments revealed that agents equipped with superior prompts or more advanced LLMs exhibit more intricate GRAPHECTORY structures, indicating deeper cognitive processes, broader contextual integration, and enhanced verification prior to implementing solutions. Moreover, agents' problem-solving approaches vary in accordance with the complexity of the task and the LLM employed; for resolved issues, the strategies typically involve identifying the problem, executing the solution, and conducting verification, whereas unresolved issues display chaotic, repetitive, or regressive behaviors. Furthermore, even in successful instances, agentic programming systems frequently experience sluggish processes, resulting in unnecessarily prolonged trajectories. Agentic systems employing large language models (LLMs) have emerged as a promising methodology for tackling complex, multi-step tasks across diverse domains, including software engineering, web browsing, virtual assistance, scientific discovery, and robotic navigation. Similar to traditional software systems, agentic systems consist of components such as LLMs, tools, APIs, and memory, encompassing inputs, internal states, operational methods, and outputs. However, rather than adhering to rigid procedures, these systems generate pathways—sequences of cognition, action, and observation—that collectively form the rationale for task completion. The evaluation of agentic software systems has predominantly focused on the final outcome: the capability of the generated pathway to resolve a given problem.
