Advanced reinforcement learning techniques have been employed through domain randomization and reward shaping to address discrepancies between simulations and real-world environments. It has been demonstrated by Tobin et al. (2017) that domain randomization facilitates the acquisition of robotic tasks within simulated environments, which can subsequently be executed in real-world settings. Similarly, Grzes and Kudenko (2010) investigated the application of reward shaping to expedite learning processes in multi-agent scenarios. These methodologies have proven effective in enhancing the adaptability of robots to dynamic environments, particularly within the construction domain. However, their implementation necessitates substantial computational resources and meticulous parameter tuning, which pose significant challenges in rapidly evolving construction site conditions. Furthermore, the interpretability and verifiability of decisions derived from these learned policies remain problematic. This limitation has prompted the adoption of game theory, which provides a robust framework for modeling interactions among autonomous agents. For instance, Martin et al. (2023) utilized game theory to optimize task allocation among multiple robots, employing Shapley values to quantify individual contributions. Their approach yielded favorable outcomes and facilitated an in-depth examination of solution stability and equity. Nevertheless, game theory models exhibit inherent complexities, particularly regarding interpretability and the assumption of rational agent behavior, which may not hold true in unpredictable construction contexts. Additionally, the reliance on precise utility functions, which are challenging to define in the context of intricate construction tasks, further limits their applicability. Despite these advancements, significant challenges persist in deploying artificial intelligence and learning-based methods within construction coordination, particularly concerning the explainability of decision-making processes. The opaque nature of deep learning and game theory models complicates the elucidation of coordination decisions, a critical requirement for ensuring safety and effective project management within construction. Consequently, attention has shifted toward the adoption of Large Language Models (LLMs) for coordination, as these systems possess the capability to articulate their reasoning in natural language, thereby enhancing decision transparency. LLMs have emerged as transformative tools in multi-robot coordination by virtue of their ability to comprehend language, reason contextually, and adapt decisions based on new information. Unlike traditional methods that demand comprehensive problem specifications, LLMs facilitate more intuitive human-robot interaction and contribute to improved clarity in decision-making.
