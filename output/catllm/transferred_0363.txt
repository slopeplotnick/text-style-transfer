The Adversarial Motion Policy (AMP) method effectively replicates motions, although it necessitates reference motion data during the training phase. Conversely, Score-Matching Policy (SMP) exhibits the capability to replicate a diverse array of motions, including challenging ones, comparable to AMP, without the requirement for reference data during training. Specifically, SMP demonstrates superior performance compared to SMILING and AMP-Frozen across all skill sets. AMP-Frozen attempts to eliminate the dependency on reference data inherent in AMP by employing a pre-trained discriminator; however, this approach proves ineffective, resulting in suboptimal motion imitation. DeepMimic employs phase synchronization to attenuate tracking errors for certain motions, yet SMP continues to perform competitively. Notably, DeepMimic encounters difficulties with complex motions such as spinkicks and cartwheels, particularly when pose termination is disabled, leading some runs to converge on unsatisfactory solutions. Our system is among the first to utilize score-matching in constructing a reusable motion model that rivals leading adversarial imitation learning methods. In this section, the role of ensemble score-matching in enhancing training stability and improving outcomes is examined. Furthermore, our ensemble score-matching approach, which averages multiple Score-Diffusion Score (SDS) evaluations across predetermined diffusion timesteps, is contrasted with the conventional method from previous research that employs SDS at a singular random timestep. The comparative analysis focuses on single-clip imitation tasks such as Run, Cartwheel, and Backflip. Table 5 illustrates that employing SDS at a single timestep performs adequately for basic motions but encounters challenges with more complex ones. For instance, during the replication of a backflip, policies trained with a single SDS evaluation frequently result in either standing still or falling backward rather than executing a complete flip. Utilizing multiple SDS evaluations over predetermined timesteps provides a more consistent reward signal, thereby facilitating the training of policies for intricate motions such as acrobatics.
