Position bias is a phenomenon wherein language models exhibit differential attention towards information based on its position within the input, potentially neglecting other segments. Despite the advanced capabilities of these models in processing linguistic data, empirical studies have demonstrated their susceptibility to the arrangement of input data. Specifically, in tasks such as summarizing multiple documents into a cohesive summary, the sequence of document presentation can significantly influence the content included in the final summary. Consequently, summaries may disproportionately emphasize certain sections while omitting pivotal details, contingent upon the placement of information. An illustrative instance of this is the "Lost in the Middle" problem, wherein researchers have identified that these models perform optimally when critical information is positioned at either the commencement or the conclusion of the input, yet exhibit diminished performance when such information resides in the middle. This pattern has been observed in tasks including question answering or extracting salient details from multiple documents. Additionally, other investigations have revealed that models engaged in evaluative tasks exhibit a propensity to favor the initial or terminal options presented, even when the optimal choice is located elsewhere, a bias often termed as lead bias. Although some models, such as GPT-3.5-Turbo, demonstrate reduced susceptibility to this bias, others remain acutely sensitive to content presented early in the input, particularly in contexts involving concise summaries. This bias not only adversely impacts performance but also raises concerns regarding fairness. For example, if tweets are categorized by dialect and sequenced in a specific order, the resultant summaries may unjustly prioritize the group appearing first, thereby underscoring the profound impact of input order.
