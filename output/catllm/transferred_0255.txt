Language models have been demonstrated to surpass traditional algorithmic models due to their superior predictive abilities and comprehensibility, in addition to their capacity to concurrently manage diverse tasks. Specifically, Small Language Models (SLMs) have emerged as a pragmatic alternative to Large Language Models (LLMs), as they are engineered for operational efficiency through techniques such as quantization, knowledge distillation, and lightweight architectural designs. SLMs are capable of performing tasks such as text sorting, information summarization, and question answering on devices constrained by limited resources, including mobile phones and embedded systems. Consequently, they represent a viable option relative to LLMs, given their reduced computational demands while maintaining performance in specific tasks. Empirical studies indicate that in scenarios characterized by limited data availability, smaller models can perform comparably or even exceed the performance of larger models in focused domains such as text sorting and healthcare. Therefore, SLMs are considered suitable for identifying mental health issues on social media platforms. However, prior research has predominantly focused on singular tasks or domains without integrating multiple mental health issues such as depression, stress, and suicidality. Unlike earlier LLMs, which possess an excessive number of parameters unsuitable for environments with resource constraints or privacy concerns, SLMs prioritize user-friendliness, rapid response times, minimal memory usage, and cost-effectiveness. Previous studies, which compared several SLMs with LLMs across various mental health tasks using social media data, have revealed that few-shot prompting confers greater benefits to SLMs. Kim et al. developed an SLM named mhGPT, which outperforms larger models such as MentaLLaMA and Gemma on mental health tasks despite having fewer parameters and utilizing less data. Moreover, another study suggests that SLMs can be applied in clinical settings for structured tasks beyond mere issue detection. Nonetheless, these studies often lack task balance and are infrequently optimized with efficient fine-tuning, frequently relying on either minimal prompting or comprehensive fine-tuning.
