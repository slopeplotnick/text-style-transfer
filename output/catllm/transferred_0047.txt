The efficacy of these models across diverse scenarios remains a considerable challenge. Despite extensive training on varied datasets, models frequently exhibit deficiencies when encountering novel camera angles, lighting variations, or other unforeseen scene alterations. Consequently, their utility in dynamic and unpredictable real-world environments is diminished. To address these limitations, two primary strategies are typically employed: enhancing data diversity and refining spatial representation capabilities. The data-centric approach involves the utilization of expansive datasets, such as Libero-Plus, which encompass a multitude of perspectives to familiarize models with a broader array of visual contexts. However, the acquisition of such data in real-world scenarios is both costly and labor-intensive, posing challenges to its sustainability over time. Conversely, the representation-centric method seeks to augment models' geometric understanding, such as object positioning in three-dimensional space, through techniques like multi-view supervision or architectures specifically designed for 3D awareness. Although these approaches enhance models' ability to accommodate viewpoint variations, they continue to exhibit limitations in adapting to cluttered environments and lighting fluctuations, suggesting incomplete adaptability to all visual conditions. Predominantly, it is assumed that either an increase in data volume or an improvement in 3D-centric design is necessary for model robustness. Nevertheless, limited research investigates whether these issues stem from the representation of spatial information. To elucidate this, a methodology is employed that bifurcates the model into two components: spatial modeling, managed by the visual encoder, and physical modeling, which integrates spatial data with task instructions and historical actions to devise actionable strategies. Spatial modeling encompasses the identification of object location, orientation, interactions, and occlusions, forming the foundational spatial information for subsequent processes. In contrast, physical modeling synthesizes this spatial data to formulate decisions and actions. Variations in camera perspectives predominantly impact the spatial configuration of the scene rather than the intrinsic meaning of the task or the execution of actions. Therefore, performance degradation is primarily attributed to misalignment in spatial modeling, notwithstanding the ongoing efficacy of the physical modeling component. Essentially, while the model retains cognitive and planning capabilities, inaccuracies in spatial information precipitate challenges during execution.
