The findings indicate that the Menta model demonstrates greater efficacy in identifying depression and stress compared to detecting suicide risk. Both fine-tuned and standard language models, including larger variants, exhibit enhanced performance in tasks related to depression and stress rather than suicidality. For instance, Bauer (2024) reviewed the limitations of language models in recognizing subtle indicators of suicidality, attributing these challenges to a scarcity of data and reliance on contextual and temporal factors, which are often ambiguous in isolated posts. Similarly, Lamichhane (2023) reported that models achieved an F1-score of 0.86 for depression detection, whereas the corresponding score for suicidality detection was significantly lower at 0.37 when utilizing social media data. These observations suggest that depression and stress are comparatively easier to identify due to their more distinct patterns and the availability of larger datasets, whereas suicidality is characterized by lower prevalence, higher contextual dependency, and increased complexity for model interpretation. Consequently, it is proposed that language models may be more effective for general mental health screenings, such as identifying depression or stress, but less proficient in detailed suicide risk assessment unless augmented by diverse datasets or longitudinal data tracking. Several limitations of the study are acknowledged. Although models such as Qwen-3 (4B) have demonstrated considerable predictive capability in mental health contexts, certain factors constrain the broader applicability of the findings. The exclusive focus on social media posts precludes the inclusion of other critical dimensions, such as temporal variations in writing patterns, user profile information, and interactive behaviors, which could enhance the understanding of mental health dynamics. Future research should consider integrating these aspects to improve methodological robustness and ecological validity. Furthermore, the scope of the study was restricted to depression, stress, and suicidal ideation, thus excluding other significant mental health conditions, such as anxiety, bipolar disorder, and emotional dysregulation. Additionally, the reliance on publicly available, anonymized social media data, while ensuring confidentiality and replicability, limits the ability to draw conclusions regarding safety, ethical considerations, and long-term reliability within clinical healthcare environments.
