The order in which articles are presented to the model significantly influences the summaries generated, with a tendency observed for the model to prioritize information from the first article, thereby affecting both the framing of the summarized content and its alignment with the original articles. Previous research has highlighted the inclination of such models to focus disproportionately on earlier segments of lengthy inputs, and the current findings extend this observation to scenarios involving multiple documents. Specifically, it is evident that the first article consistently receives greater emphasis during the model's ranking of information, as demonstrated through the application of the scoring method BERTScore, which consistently reflects this pattern. This phenomenon occurs due to the model's propensity to utilize the lexical and argumentative elements from the first article more extensively compared to subsequent ones, resulting in higher similarity scores for the first article. Conversely, alternative evaluation metrics such as ROUGE-L, which primarily measure word overlap, and SummaC, which assesses factual consistency without emphasizing framing, exhibit a reduced sensitivity to this first-article bias. Nevertheless, across all three metrics, the first article remains distinctly prioritized relative to the second and third articles, although no substantial differences are observed between the latter two. These results indicate that the prioritization of the first article is not attributable to a progressive decline in attention towards later inputs but rather reflects an inherent bias in the model's processing mechanism. Such biases may have tangible implications in practical applications, particularly in AI-driven summarization tools that synthesize information for end-users. For example, AI summaries generated by systems such as Google Search's Gemini models may disproportionately favor the content of the initial articles processed, potentially marginalizing subsequent perspectives and influencing the overall understanding of a topic. This raises concerns about the potential for biased summaries to shape public perception, particularly in contexts where users exhibit high levels of trust in AI systems.
