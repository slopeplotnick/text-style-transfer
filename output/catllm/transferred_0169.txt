This study addresses the issue of safety in complex systems and proposes a novel conceptual framework to enhance the understanding of systemic risks. Specifically, it argues for a shift in perspective, advocating for the consideration of safety as an emergent property arising from the interactions between system components rather than as an isolated attribute of individual models. To facilitate this reconceptualization, the Emergent Systemic Risk Horizon (ESRH) is introduced as a theoretical construct designed to elucidate the conditions under which systems may exhibit instability. The framework progresses through a systematic examination of interactions, beginning with dyadic relationships, advancing to multi-agent dynamics, categorizing diverse types of risks, and culminating in the proposal of Institutional AI as a method for mitigating such challenges. Existing approaches to ensuring the safety of language models predominantly focus on human-model interactions, employing interventions such as prompts and filters to suppress harmful outputs and enforce compliance with predefined rules. Such strategies operate under the assumption that a model's behavior is both predictable and relatively static. However, the inherent complexity of language models, including their dependence on internal states, prior dialogues, and contextual variations, introduces significant unpredictability, wherein minor contextual changes can precipitate divergent outcomes despite apparent adherence to established guidelines. The issue is further exacerbated in scenarios involving interactions among multiple models, wherein the output of one model serves as the input for another, creating feedback loops that amplify initial discrepancies. Empirical evidence, such as findings from JAILJUDGE experiments, demonstrates that the behavior of interconnected models can diverge substantially from that observed in isolated testing. Moreover, studies conducted in simulated market environments reveal that even when individual models are designed to exhibit fairness, their collective interactions may culminate in inequitable outcomes, thereby underscoring the inadequacy of solely optimizing components in isolation. These observations underscore the imperative to transition from a reductionist focus on prompts or discrete actions to a holistic examination of systemic communication and interaction patterns.
