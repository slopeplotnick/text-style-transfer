Online videos have become a significant medium for communication, and their ubiquitous presence facilitates the rapid dissemination of hateful content. Such material exacerbates discrimination and social division and can precipitate real-world violence. Conventional approaches to addressing this issue involve extracting features from video, sound, and text; however, they often fail to capture the synergistic interaction of these elements. Two primary challenges are encountered in the detection of hateful videos: firstly, the comprehension of context, given that these videos frequently utilize subtle cues across temporal dimensions and various media types, such as the congruence between images and words or the relationship between visual and auditory content. Secondly, the integration of heterogeneous information is problematic, as current methodologies extract features from video, sound, and text without effective interaction among them. Addressing these challenges necessitates sophisticated cognitive processes that traditional methods inadequately perform. Innovative strategies have emerged, aiming to enhance understanding through models that integrate visual and linguistic elements. These models incorporate reasoning steps; however, direct reasoning tends to produce rudimentary descriptions that fail to connect with the underlying hateful meanings, and some contemporary approaches may yield erroneous explanations that do not accurately represent the model's cognitive processes, posing issues for sensitive tasks. Recent research indicates that hate cues in videos can manifest abruptly in brief segments or be dispersed throughout the video. Furthermore, these signals possess complex meanings that require synthesis with foundational features. These challenges underscore a significant deficiency: current systems are ineffective in executing reliable reasoning or in synthesizing diverse types of information to detect hateful videos comprehensively.
