With the exception of Study 1, all synthetic datasets replicated the factor structure of their corresponding scales. Moreover, the results of the Multigroup Confirmatory Factor Analysis (M-CFA) indicate that both synthetic and real participants demonstrated similar response patterns to the scales. These findings align with prior research on group-level synthetic data. Interestingly, Studies 3 and 4 revealed higher levels of measurement consistency between datasets in comparison to Studies 1 and 2, which potentially suggests that scales developed using synthetic participants may exhibit greater applicability when used with real participants as opposed to the reverse scenario. This observation could be attributed to the tendency of large language models (LLMs) to perceive scale items in a more standardized manner, exhibiting reduced susceptibility to random variations and biases arising from study conditions and population-specific traits. Consequently, it is plausible that factor structures derived from synthetic samples may exhibit broader applicability across different samples relative to those derived from real-world data. Further investigation is warranted to examine the factors influencing the interpretative and response processes of both LLMs and human participants in survey contexts. Although CFA yielded favorable outcomes, synthetic samples failed to replicate the pairwise correlations, distribution patterns, and variance observed within real-world samples. Collectively, these findings underscore that, despite the shared factor structure, score distributions between synthetic and real datasets diverge significantly across all measured dimensions. Accordingly, while group-level synthetic data and their associated structures demonstrate sufficient reliability for pilot testing purposes, synthetic data at the individual level are not suitable for such applications. These limitations are consistent with the findings reported by Petrov et al. Notably, a divergence was observed in the results of Study 1 compared to Studies 2, 3, and 4, which can be attributed to several factors. Firstly, the factor structure for this scale was identified using the minimum residual (minres) method, differing from the methods employed for other scales, potentially contributing to variations in responses. Secondly, this particular scale utilized a 5-point Likert response format, whereas the other scales employed 6-point or 7-point formats.
