The utilization of models across various scientific disciplines is widespread; geologists frequently incorporate diagrams within their papers, mathematicians employ models to establish rules for probability frameworks, and geophysicists utilize them for Bayesian inversion processes. Moreover, the consideration of alternative data is paramount, as initial hypotheses may prove erroneous. This challenge is embedded within a broader scientific inquiry concerning the verification of assumptions. Karl Popper, an Austrian philosopher, dedicated his intellectual pursuits to such questions, proposing that science should eschew the inductive methodology of deriving theories from observations. Specifically, Popper posited that theories are conjectures devised to address deficiencies in preceding theories and necessitate empirical testing. The failure of a theory to align with observational data necessitates its rejection, conversely, theories that withstand scrutiny are deemed optimal. In the context of uncertainty quantification, models are perceived as conjectural representations of reality, with multiplicity inherent in the diversity of models. These suppositions may be constrained by pre-existing knowledge; however, empirical data should not serve to affirm a model, but rather to refute it should discrepancies arise. Popper provides a mechanism to address the subjectivity inherent in Bayesian reasoning—a form of inductive reasoning—as opposed to the deductive approach of falsification. Consequently, if the prior distribution is unable to forecast current data, the Bayesian prior is invalidated, signifying a discordance between prior models and empirical evidence. While Popper's philosophy potentially diminishes the subjective nature of the prior upon testing, he does not furnish a methodology for its alteration, leaving modifications subject to human discernment.
