Results and observations: Table 4 shows what happens when you change things around. The hybrid routing is important: forcing one way to do things makes it worse, especially when you have to mix small and big changes. The XML-only way gets a score of 0.95, and the python-pptx-only way gets 2.06. The hybrid way without going over again gets 2.36, and doing things over again makes it better, getting 2.84 IF and 3.21 VQ. Looking at each case, giving slide images to PPTPilot helps with layout decisions: seeing things helps with choices about order, space, and alignment without messing up text editing. Doing things over again helps with tough edits, with one more pass often fixing format issues after big changes or lots of edits, with only a little extra work. Judge configuration analysis: The top part of Table 4 looks at three ways to check things: one judge looking at everything at once, one judge looking only at slide images, and our two-judge setup with clear change analysis. The two-judge way with clear changes is much more stable when there are many edits, as the judge can directly look at structured changes instead of guessing from pictures. Extra material: Details about PPTArena, PPTPilot, how things are done, how we check things, future plans, problems, and bigger impacts are in the extra material. We introduce PPTArena and PPTPilot to make editing PowerPoint slides something you can measure for smart systems. PPTArena uses real slides, human-made edit plans, and style rules to make a test that shows how well instructions are followed, how things are structured, and layout quality beyond just making things. PPTPilot shows that having a plan-edit-check cycle makes things more reliable, doing better than strong private systems and new VLMs. But systems still struggle with hard, long, and mixed tasks, showing that good PowerPoint editing is not solved yet. We hope PPTArena and PPTPilot help with future work on better PowerPoint editing systems that can safely and reliably work on the slides people actually use.
