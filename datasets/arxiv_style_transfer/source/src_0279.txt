They used a different dataset called AI2-THOR instead of the one they used before, which was from the TEACH dataset. This change was needed to make sure they could repeat the study easily and compare algorithms fairly. It also let them compare performance with similar tasks indirectly. But when they compared LTAA with traditional MRTA methods, they used the same dataset. The authors are also testing SMART-LLM with the TEACH dataset to make the comparison more balanced. The study was done on a small scale, which helped them compare the basics and analyze capabilities thoroughly. However, real-world construction has many more tasks with complex time and space issues that weren't fully covered in the study. They used general features like elemental/compound and heavy/dexterous to describe construction tasks instead of specific ones to make the study more general. This was necessary because there isn't a big database of real-world construction operations. The authors think that if there was such a database, testing LTAA in more situations with changing plans and resource issues would show how strong the framework is. Also, the variability in LLM outputs is a concern for safety in construction. Even though they checked multiple times to avoid failures, the randomness allowed for detailed reasoning but could lead to different allocations. Construction sites that need everything to be exact might need more checks than what they have now. When looking at the computing resources needed, they couldn't measure SMART-LLM's costs per stage because all stages used one combined prompt. To compare it with LTAA, which only looks at allocation, they guessed SMART-LLM's costs by dividing the total tokens and time by three. This guess is okay but might not show the exact costs for allocation, and future work with detailed checks would help get a clearer picture.
