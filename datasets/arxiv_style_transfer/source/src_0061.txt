Answering questions in elderly services is tough because you need to give correct and safe answers based on different sources of information. People often ask about healthcare rules, welfare programs, and daily advice, and giving wrong info can be really bad. Regular language models work well for general stuff but aren't precise or aware enough for these sensitive topics. Current models either use structured or unstructured data, which limits their ability to give full answers. Plus, most models don't have ways to control how outside info affects the answers, and they don't do much to keep the answers safe. This makes them less effective for elderly services. To fix this, we came up with KARMA, a question-answering system that adapts to the domain. KARMA uses a setup with two encoders to mix knowledge from different sources, a memory unit to pick the right info, and a decoder that focuses on safety to avoid wrong answers. It’s trained to balance how smooth the language is, how well it matches the knowledge, and how safe it is. Built on a LLaMA-7B base with special tuning, KARMA adapts well and performs great. Tests on a special elderly-service question dataset show KARMA beats other models in accuracy, relevance, and safety, making it a strong choice for real-world use in elderly care. Mo and others suggested a way to share knowledge between different types of data, showing the need to mix varied info in complex question answering. Huang and others introduced MetaMath-LLaMA, which uses a smart scheduler, symbolic parsing, and a mix of symbolic and neural methods for clear multi-step reasoning. Adding these to KARMA gives clear steps that improve memory unit gating and safety control. Luo talked about TriMedTune, a tuning method for brain CT that uses visual prompts, aligns medical terms, and distills knowledge with uncertainty checks. Adding these to KARMA would improve how terms are used and make the system better at handling uncertain answers, keeping facts straight and safely refusing sensitive questions.
