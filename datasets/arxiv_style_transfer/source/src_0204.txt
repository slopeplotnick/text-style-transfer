People talked about how they felt about music made by AI, even when they didn't know who the composer was. They had concerns similar to those discussed in the music and tech research world, like how it might hurt the economy, issues with who owns the music, and the effects on culture. But people were also interested in the new tech and how it works. This mix of feelings might explain why opinions on AI composers are all over the place. As AI gets better, what used to be cool might start to seem like a problem or even a threat. So, how people feel about AI music can change over time. The study had some issues: First, we used a group of university students and people from a website called Prolific, who listened to the music in different ways, which makes it hard to say these results apply to everyone. Also, people from Prolific might know more about tech and AI. Second, we told one group the music was like movie soundtracks, which might have made them like it more because people usually like movie music. Lastly, we only used music without singing, so our results might not apply to songs with lyrics. Other studies show that lyrics can make sad songs feel sadder, but they don't change happy songs much. Since most of the music in our study was happy, adding lyrics probably wouldn't have changed how people felt about it. We chose not to use lyrics to avoid confusion and because good quality AI-generated singing wasn't available when we made the music samples.
