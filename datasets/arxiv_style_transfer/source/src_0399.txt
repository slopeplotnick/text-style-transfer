We’re working on a way to find fraud in journal entry data without needing labeled examples of fraud, since those are hard to get and not always available. This means we can’t use methods that rely on labeled data, so instead, we look for unusual patterns in the data to spot entries that don’t seem normal. There’s a made-up dataset created by Gronewald and others in 2024 that mimics the bookkeeping of a medium-sized business. Each entry in this dataset has things like a unique ID, the date and time of the posting and transaction, whether it’s a credit or debit, the tax rate, and an account ID. We also test our method on real accounting data from a business partner, but this data is anonymized, so it doesn’t have the usual links between debit and credit entries you’d expect in standard bookkeeping. Because of this, we treat it as a problem of finding fraud at the transaction level. To make up for the missing details, we add bookkeeping rules, guidelines, and summary stats to the large language model (LLM) prompts, which helps it do better than traditional machine learning methods. Since privacy is important when working with sensitive accounting data, we used open-source LLMs like LLaMa AI (2025) and Gemma Team (2024) instead of proprietary ones like GPT-4 from OpenAI (2024) for testing. Our method performs better than the baseline methods on both the synthetic dataset from Gronewald et al. (2024) and the real anonymized data. For the baseline, we used Isolation Forest, a method introduced by Liu et al. in 2008, because it works well with tabular data and is designed for unsupervised anomaly detection, which fits the goal of this project.
