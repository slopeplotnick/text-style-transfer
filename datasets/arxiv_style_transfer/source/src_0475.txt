Even though this way of doing things is getting more popular, there hasn't been much research on how solvers and verifiers work together. Most of the past research looked at how one model checks its own answers and gets better at it, but this isn't always a sure thing. Models can be biased towards their own way of thinking, and their training might make this bias stronger. Plus, different tasks might not all get the same benefits from checking answers. Also, focusing only on self-checking doesn't really help us understand what happens when the solver and verifier aren't the same. With open-source models that have different versions and setups, we can look into how checking works across different models. So, we're expanding our study to include both within the same model family and between different families, and we're asking: When does checking really help, and how do things like model family, size, extra training, how similar the solver and verifier are, or the type of task affect how well checking improves the solver? To figure this out, we're testing verifiers on a bunch of different tasks, like ones that need exact logical thinking or symbolic math (like 3-SAT, Sudoku, and matrix multiplication), math reasoning tasks (like AIME and GSM8K), commonsense and factual reasoning (like CSQA and GPQA), and general knowledge in areas like STEM and social sciences using 37 models from 7 different model families. Our study shows that self-checking doesn't always work out: models often prefer solutions that match their own thinking, extra training can make this bias stronger, and some tasks just don't get as much from checking as others do. So, we're offering some practical advice based on our findings on how to use verifiers in a smart way.
