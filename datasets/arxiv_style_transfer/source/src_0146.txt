When systems evolve on their own without guidance, they tend to hit a limit fast and become unstable, which often leads to less variety and ways to cheat the reward system. Our results show this clearly. On the left side of the graph, R-Zero loses variety in its questions as training goes on, dropping from 35 to under 20 in the first 50 steps. After 50 steps, the 2-gram diversity score goes up, but this happens because the questions get way longer, as shown in the middle graph. This isn’t real variety, though—it’s just because longer questions naturally create more unique combinations of words, which makes the diversity score look better without actually exploring new ideas. The same thing happens with question length. R-Zero keeps making its questions longer and longer, eventually making them way too long. This shows it’s trying to cheat the system by using surface-level tricks, like being wordy, to make it seem like its questions are more varied or harder. On the other hand, R-Few keeps its variety steady during training and doesn’t make its questions longer, which shows that adding a bit of human input stops the system from losing variety. On the right side of the graph, both methods make their questions harder over time, but there’s a big difference in why this happens. To measure difficulty, we used Gemini-2.5-Pro to relabel questions made at different training stages, then tested how well another model, Qwen3-8B-Base, could answer them. Difficulty is the percentage of questions the model gets wrong. Both methods show an overall increase in difficulty, but the reasons are different. R-Zero makes its questions harder mostly because they’re longer, not because they need deeper thinking. This is another way it cheats the system, making things seem harder without actually improving reasoning. R-Few, on the other hand, keeps its question length steady, letting the system evolve naturally toward harder questions that are actually more challenging without losing variety or structure.
