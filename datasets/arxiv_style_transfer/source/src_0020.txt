People are using big language models more and more as automated judges to check the work of other systems that create stuff. This idea is called "Thinking-LLM-as-a-Judge." It's a cheaper and faster way than having humans do it, which takes a lot of time and money. To deal with the randomness and mistakes that can happen when making judgments once, a common trick is to use a lot of computer power to make several different guesses and ratings for each thing being checked. But how good the final decision is really depends on how these different results are put together. The usual ways of doing this, like going with what most results say or using scores that show how confident the model is, often don't work well and aren't the best statistically. These methods struggle especially when there's a tie. For example, just counting votes can't tell the difference between a close 5-to-4 vote and a clear 9-to-0 vote, missing out on important details about how strong the evidence is. This lack of attention to how strong the evidence is makes the evaluations less reliable. In our work, we say that putting together the results is really important for using computer power effectively. We suggest a new way called Distribution-Calibrated Aggregation that goes beyond just counting votes. Our method looks at all the positive, negative, and tie votes to keep all the information from the votes. We use a model that looks at three possible outcomes and considers both how much one side is preferred and how likely ties are. By figuring out the best settings using a small test group and then using a specific error measure, our method stays true to the evaluation goal while fitting the data well, avoiding mismatches, and giving more accurate decisions.
