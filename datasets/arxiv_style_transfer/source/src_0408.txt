Basically, the data shows that the way we automatically create knowledge graphs (KGs) makes it easier to see and understand how training data affects what the model creates. This helps with things like explaining how models work, checking for copyright issues, and making datasets more transparent. For example, if you ask a model to make an image in a specific style, like “Ghibli-style,” it doesn’t just copy exact images it was trained on. Instead, it learns the general look and feel of that style from a bunch of images. These features are stored in the model and used to create new images, but they’re not direct copies. Because of this, it’s hard to say exactly which training images influenced the output. The influence happens in a more general way, through shared ideas like textures, themes, or how things are put together in the image. To figure out how a style influences the model, we need to look at patterns and meanings, not just match specific objects.
