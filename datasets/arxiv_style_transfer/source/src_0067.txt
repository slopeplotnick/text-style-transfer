Big language models (LLMs) usually do better on a wide range of tasks because they’re bigger and can follow instructions well. But smaller language models (SLMs) can do just as well or even better on certain tasks, like predicting depression and stress. For other tasks, SLMs have improved a lot, but they still don’t perform as well as LLMs (Xu et al., 2024). This shows that SLMs can work really well when the task and the way they process language match up with what they’re good at. However, there’s a big problem: SLMs aren’t great with long inputs because they can’t handle as many tokens and struggle with keeping track of long or complicated contexts (Kumar, 2025). This makes them less useful for tasks where understanding long or complex stories is important, which is something LLMs are better at (Lu et al., 2024). Overall, the results suggest that instead of just making models bigger, we should focus on making them better suited for specific purposes in mental-health AI. As things like efficiency, transparency, safety, and privacy (Zhang et al., 2021) become more important in clinical machine learning, the balance between how well a model works and how practical it is will shape the future of digital mental-health tools. SLMs are already meeting the need for tools that can quickly and privately detect mental-health issues in real-time (Harari et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2018), while also fitting the push for AI in healthcare to be more transparent and accountable (Wies et al., 2021; Smith et al., 2023). This study shows that fine-tuned SLMs, when trained with strategies that use data from different sources, can perform as well as or better than LLMs in predicting mental-health outcomes. This suggests that smaller, more focused models could be a more sustainable and ethical option for monitoring mental health in real-world situations. So, what tasks are SLMs best for? In this study, we show that training them with data from different sources works well for predicting depression, stress, and suicidal thoughts, especially when paired with a smart loss design. This matches what Yao et al. (Benton et al., 2017) found—that sharing patterns between related mental-health tasks can help improve results. But unlike their approach, which used a multi-task setup, our method gets similar benefits with a more efficient fine-tuning process (LoRA) that uses less computing power and memory. This shows that even small, lightweight models can make good use of diverse data if the training goals are set up the right way.
