To deal with this, we need tools that can track how reasoning spreads and changes over time, instead of just judging if something is right or wrong in the moment. The idea of the Emergent Systemic Risk Horizon marks the point where we can’t assume things are separate or can be undone anymore. After this point, we have to think about safety as something that applies to the whole connected system—how well it can stay stable, make sense, and respond when everything is constantly interacting. To do this, we’ll need new experiments, data, and ways to measure how groups behave over time. We’ll also need systems of rules and oversight that work at the same big scale as these networks. This is what we’ll talk about in the next part, where we explain the idea of Institutional AI. It’s a way to build oversight and rules directly into systems with lots of interacting parts. As language model systems get more complicated, we need ways to manage them that can change and grow along with the systems. When these models start acting on their own—thinking, negotiating, and working together without people constantly watching—old methods like central control, fixed safety rules, or occasional checks don’t work anymore. Those methods assume things are stable, easy to watch, and have clear accountability, which isn’t the case in these kinds of systems. So, we suggest Institutional AI, where the rules, checks, and ways to evaluate things are built into the system itself instead of being added on from the outside. The goal isn’t to copy human institutions but to include some of their useful features, like shared decision-making, clear processes, and the ability to update rules as needed, into networks of models working together. There’s also something called constitutional AI, where models follow a set of rules or principles they were trained on. This can help individual models stay aligned and consistent in one-on-one situations, but it doesn’t stop bigger risks that come up when systems interact.
