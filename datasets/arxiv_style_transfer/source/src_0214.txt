The issue with adversarial training making attacks easier to transfer isn’t because of specific model designs but because of how adversarial training changes the way models process information. This happens with all kinds of model designs, so it’s important for overall security. To figure out why adversarial training increases transferability, researchers looked at how models explain their decisions using a method called integrated gradients. In one example, they compared two versions of a DenseNet121 model: one trained with adversarial training (AT) and one trained normally (ST). The AT model focused on important parts of an image, like the head, body, and legs of an ostrich, while the ST model focused more on small details like feather textures and background areas. This difference matters because AT models pay attention to general features that are common across many images, while ST models focus on specific details that don’t transfer well to other models. That’s why adversarial examples created by AT models work better across different models—they target features that are shared by many models. For example, DenseNet121 and DenseNet201 have similar ways of processing images, and MobileNetV3 is also similar because it uses design ideas from EfficientNet.
