To understand the trends from the accuracy and false positive rate results, we look at the verifier gain in the last row. Verifier gain shows how useful it is to use the verifier during rejection sampling, which means sampling until the verifier accepts a solution. This helps us see how good the verification is: self-verification gives the smallest gains, and more accurate solvers don't get better by themselves. Gains go up a bit with intra-family verification, while cross-family verification gives the most benefits. We check how well the verifier works with different model families and sizes. In Figure 3, we do the same experiments as in Figure 2 but show each verifier metric based on model size within each model family. We see that verification accuracy and false negative rate get better as models get bigger, but false positive rate is less predictable and often goes up with model size, like intra-family verification for DeepSeek. For top post-trained models like Qwen3 and DeepSeek, verifier gains are biggest in the cross-family setting, smaller in the intra-family setting, and very small during self-verification. This seems to go against Song et al. (2025), who say self-verification GV-Gaps get bigger with more pretraining FLOPs. But their study looks at older model families, and Figure 2 also shows bigger verifier gains for older models like Qwen2.5 and Llama3. We think stronger post-trained models like DeepSeek and Qwen3 show little gain in self-verification and limited gain in intra-family verification for two reasons: they might already do self-verification on their own when used as solvers, which makes an extra forced verification round less helpful, and their distributions are sharpened a lot by post-training, which limits the improvement from rejection sampling.
