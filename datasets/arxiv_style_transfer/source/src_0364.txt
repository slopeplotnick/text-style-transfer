How do language models use context to answer health questions, and what happens when the context is mixed up? We look at how well language models can handle long, conflicting health information using a dataset called HEALTHCONTRADICT. It has 920 examples, each with a health question, a factual answer backed by science, and two documents that say opposite things. We try different ways of asking the questions, like using correct, wrong, or mixed-up context, and see how it affects the answers. Compared to other tests, HEALTHCONTRADICT shows better how language models deal with context. Our tests show that these models are good not just because of what they learned before but also because they can use the right context and ignore the wrong one. But they can still give answers that sound right but aren't true. This is a problem because people might trust wrong info about health, which can be risky. Also, health info changes fast, especially during crises, and unverified info spreads quickly online, affecting how these models learn. Some methods have been suggested to help models deal with mixed-up info. One method changes the model's knowledge if it doesn't match the context, and another traces back to see why a model gave a certain answer. But these methods focus on either what the model already knows or the context. Another method uses both by checking if the info fits together. There's also a method that predicts two answers, one from context and one from what the model knows. Another technique makes sure the model picks the right answer when there's conflicting info. Other solutions try to stop models from giving bad answers. During training, they add wrong or unrelated info to make the models better at handling mixed-up info. During testing, they use strategies to spot and stop wrong info, ask questions in a careful way, and use multiple readers to check the answers. They also search for strong answers to stop attacks and predict which facts might change soon to avoid using old info. Current methods focus on either fixing mixed-up info or stopping bad behavior in models, but both are important for better answers in health topics.
