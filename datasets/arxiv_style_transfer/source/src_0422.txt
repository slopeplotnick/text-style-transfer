Videos online are a big way people communicate now, and because they're everywhere, hateful stuff spreads fast. This kind of content makes discrimination and social division worse and can even lead to violence in real life. The usual way to deal with this involves pulling features from video, sound, and text, but it doesn't really get how these things work together. There are two main problems with spotting hateful videos: first, understanding the context—these videos often use subtle cues over time and different types of media, like how certain images and words match up or how what's shown connects with what's heard. Second, mixing different types of information—current methods pull features from video, sound, and text but don't interact well with each other. Both problems need deep thinking that typical methods can't do well. Some new ways try to improve understanding by using models that combine visuals and language. These methods add reasoning steps, but direct reasoning only gives basic descriptions without connecting to hateful meanings, and some recent ways can give wrong explanations that don't show how the model really thinks, which is a problem for sensitive tasks. Besides reasoning, recent studies show hate cues in videos can pop up quickly in short bits or spread out over the whole video. Signals also have complex meanings that need to be mixed with basic features. These issues show a big gap: current systems aren't good at reliable reasoning or mixing different types of information to spot hateful videos.
