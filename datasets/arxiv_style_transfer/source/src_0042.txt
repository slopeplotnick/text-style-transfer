The trade-off method is about giving tasks to the robots that are most likely to do them well, based on numbers that show how good they are at those tasks. If two robots have almost the same chance of success, then how much work each one already has is considered. This way of doing things helps get the best results for the whole project, makes the most of what each robot is good at, and lowers the chances of tasks going wrong by matching the right robot to the right job. It’s useful in emergencies where failing isn’t an option, when time is tight, or when safety or mission success is on the line. The idea is that the way decisions are made changes as the project goes on, since what’s best at the start might not be best later, and there’s less room to fix mistakes as things progress. At first, the focus is mostly on getting things done successfully (80%) and less on sharing the work fairly (20%). Later on, it shifts to 40% success and 60% fairness, so things are more balanced over time. This method keeps adjusting decisions based on how far along the project is, how much work is left, and how tasks are spread out. It uses smart reasoning to handle competing goals and is okay with doing slightly worse in the later stages to make sure the work is shared out fairly. Sometimes, it might give a task to a robot that’s not the best fit just to avoid one robot being overloaded. It’s good for complicated situations, keeps performance high early on while making things fairer later, and changes automatically as the project moves forward. It works well for projects where both getting things done and being fair are important, long-term projects where keeping everyone happy matters, and situations where both efficiency and fairness count. The main idea is to share the work fairly and use all the robots equally because that’s the best way to keep things running smoothly in the long run. Most of the focus (70%) is on fairness, with 30% on getting tasks done successfully. This setup makes fairness the priority while still keeping performance at a decent level, so the work is spread out evenly even if it means not always doing things in the most efficient way.
