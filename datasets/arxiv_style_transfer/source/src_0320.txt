Enhanced taxonomic frameworks recognize that when robots with specialized skills do complex tasks, construction sites have unique challenges. These challenges make construction different from controlled environments where traditional methods worked well. Construction sites are messy, with changing material flows, weather issues, and complex interactions between different trades. They need systems that can handle space limits, time dependencies, and safety-critical operations. Also, construction needs human oversight that can't be pre-set or easily programmed. These challenges require flexible coordination methods that can deal with changing conditions and messy environments. Traditional optimization methods have tried to solve these challenges with different algorithm strategies. Classical optimization methods are the foundation of established multi-robot coordination approaches, offering mathematical precision and performance guarantees through various computational techniques. Kuhn (1955) created the theoretical basis for optimal assignment problems with the Hungarian method, showing that matching tasks and resources could be efficiently solved using linear programming. But the Hungarian method was limited to simple one-to-one assignments and couldn't handle different robot skills or task dependencies. In construction, robots have specialized skills like heavy lifting or precision assembly, and workflows need coordinated task sequences with clear dependencies. These limitations led to more advanced optimization methods. Atay and Bayazit (2006) developed mixed-integer linear programming (MILP) for multi-robot task allocation, considering robot skill constraints, task order relationships, and communication limits. Military and aerospace applications improved MILP-based coordination with advanced methods addressing different skills and timing constraints. MILP offered big advantages in modeling complex multi-robot situations with clear constraint satisfaction. However, MILP approaches depend on centralized optimization systems that struggle with scalability when coordinating large robot teams. Construction projects have many robots spread across large, messy sites with limited communication, making centralized coordination impractical. These scalability issues led to distributed coordination methods.
