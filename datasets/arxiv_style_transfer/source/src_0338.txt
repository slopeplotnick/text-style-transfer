In the left part of Figure 2, there’s an example of how we set up the prompt and the triples we pull out for one training image. It shows how different details about the image are organized. The output is made up of several triples that match the Fashionpedia ontology, covering things like the type of clothing (depicts), its shape (has_silhouette), material (has_material_type), pattern (has_pattern), and other relationships defined by Fashionpedia. Each triple is in the format (image_id, relation, attribute), which makes it easier to understand what the image represents. This setup helps build and compare graphs based on concepts. To figure out influence, we compared the graph of each generated image with the graphs of all the training images by checking how many nodes they had in common. For each prompt, we kept the training images that had the most overlap with the generated image’s graph. The prompt we used was: "Create triples for the fashion item in the image based on the relationships of Fashionpedia Ontology, provided at: \\({ONTOLOGY_PATH} = \). Focus exclusively on the fashion item of the image with image_id: \\)([IMAGE_ID=)} and give me the triples that describe the image (image_id, relationship, attribute_value). Please do not give more comments." For example, for image 58810.jpg, the triples were: 58810.jpg, depicts, Shirt; 58810.jpg, belongs_to_category, Upperbody; 58810.jpg, has_nickname, None; 58810.jpg, has_silhouette, Slim-fit; 58810.jpg, has_waistline, Straight; 58810.jpg, has_length, Long-sleeved; 58810.jpg, hascollar_type, Classic; 58810.jpg, has_lapel_type, Not Applicable; 58810.jpg, has_neckline, Crew-neck; 58810.jpg, has_sleeve_type, Long Sleeves with Cuffs; 58810.jpg, has_pocket_type, Not Applicable; 58810.jpg, has_opening_type, Button-front Closure; 58810.jpg, has_material_type, Cotton-blend Fabric; 58810.jpg, has_leather_type, None; 58810.jpg, has_fabric_treatment, Not Applicable; 58810.jpg, has_pattern, Plaid; 58810.jpg, has_animal_pattern, None. These graphs, which had the most nodes in common with the generated image’s graph, were then used in the next step of unlearning. Besides finding similar images, we also used Neo4j to explore the entire graph, like spotting the most common attributes or seeing how concepts were spread out in the dataset. For unlearning, we retrained the generative model after taking out certain training samples. This helped us check if those samples had any effect on the output. We ran 10 unlearning tests using both the baseline and our method. In each test, we created images for 15 prompts before and after unlearning. To match the baseline setup, we removed a similar number of training images that were the most influential for each prompt.
