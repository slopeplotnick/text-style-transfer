This paper looks at whether a computer program that simulates how people learn language can figure out grammar and word types on its own by interacting with a language model called Delilah, which gives examples of how adults use language. After learning this grammar, the program, called the daughter model, can use it to talk to another program, called the mother model. The daughter model can make new sentences using the grammar it learned and share them with the mother model during their conversation. If the settings allow, the mother model can give feedback on how good the grammar is, and the daughter model can use that feedback to improve. The daughter model can also use what it learned to understand new sentences from the mother model, like figuring out what kind of words the mother model is using. It’s known from studies that words that serve a grammatical purpose show up a lot, while words with specific meanings don’t show up as often. Some of these content words might only appear once or a few times in a text. This pattern matches something called Zipf’s law, which says that the most common words in a language are used much more often than less common ones, and this pattern has been studied by researchers like Mandelbrot and Zipf. There’s also a link between how words are distributed in languages and the categories of grammar that linguists study. This paper checks if the same kind of link can be found in language created by a computer.
