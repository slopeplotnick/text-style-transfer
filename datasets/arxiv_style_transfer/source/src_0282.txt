Fact Verification: We found that how well FV methods work depends a lot on how good the retrieval part is and if there's enough outside info. Table 3 shows that if the retrieval part messes up and doesn't give the needed proof, there's a big problem. If the retriever can't find the right stuff, maybe because the keywords don't match in the LLM-Q setup, the verification part has to decide without enough info. This lack of proof makes the model act weird: it might doubt things for no reason (causing false alarms, like in Case 1) or believe wrong stuff without checking (missing mistakes, like in Case 2). But if the retrieval process includes the generated answer (LLM-QA), the system usually gets the right documents, letting the FV model make better guesses. Hallucination Detection: HD methods often freak out because language generation can be really flexible. Table 4 shows HD methods do well with straightforward answers (Group A) but not so well with more creative ones (Group B). In those cases, a model might be sure about a fact (like when a band was formed) but unsure how to say it. Current HD methods can't tell the difference between valid variety in wording and actual lies. So, when there's a lot of different ways to say something, it gets wrongly flagged as a mistake, even if it's right. Implications for Hybrid Design: Our study shows HD and FV have different error limits, which is why they should be combined. FV is like a precise anchor: it's really reliable when it finds the right proof, but not so good when it doesn't. On the other hand, HD is like a safety net: it can catch possible mistakes even without outside info, but it gets confused by flexible wording. So, the best hybrid approach shouldn't treat them the same. It should trust FV more when the proof is right, and rely on HD's signals when the retrieval doesn't work. This balance is why we designed our hybrid framework in RQ3 the way we did.
