Flow-based diffusion models are now a top choice for training models to create images and videos. But how they remember and generalize things isn't well understood. In this study, we look again at the flow matching goal and check out its marginal velocity field, which can be exactly calculated, letting us find the exact FM target. Looking at this velocity field shows that these models naturally have a two-part training goal: first, they focus on a mix of data types, and later, they focus on the closest data example. This two-part goal leads to different learning actions: the first part helps them understand different data types to create overall patterns, while the second part focuses more on remembering small details. Using this knowledge, we explain why certain techniques work well, like changing the timing of steps, using guidance without classifiers, and making choices in the design of latent spaces. This study helps us understand how diffusion model training works and gives ideas for future improvements in design and algorithms. Diffusion models have become a strong type of generative method, able to create high-quality samples in different areas like images and videos. These models learn complex data patterns by gradually changing a starting distribution, like Gaussian, into the data distribution through a process that uses denoising or transport directions. Different ways have been suggested to describe this diffusion process, including probabilistic models, score-based ODEs/SDEs, and flow matching. These views lead to different training goals, like predicting noise, score, or velocity, and relate to different ways of setting up the probability flow ODE. Recently, most have agreed on the flow matching method, where the model is trained to predict the velocity field using a simple schedule. Because it's easy and has stable training, this method has become the standard for training top diffusion models.
