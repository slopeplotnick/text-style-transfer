The difficulty of a problem is related to how hard it is, but both within the same family and across different families, verifier improvements show clear positive links. AIME stands out in the last chart, maybe because some models have faced similar issues after training. The best-fit lines for verifier accuracy show two separate groups of tasks (marked red and blue), meaning that how hard it is to verify something isn't just about how accurate the solver is. This makes us wonder if some tasks are just easier to verify than others. Song and others (2025) found that models can't get better on tasks needing factual recall, but they can improve on Tokoku if there's enough pretraining. We see a similar split in Figure 7: AIME, GSM8K, 3SAT, and Tokoku have a higher ratio of verifier accuracy to solver accuracy and do better in all verification settings. To explain this, we see that in our made-up datasets, Selenium and 3SAT are classic examples of problems that take a long time to solve but can be checked quickly. On the other hand, there's no easy way to check the result of multiplying two matrices without basically doing the math again for Matrix Multiplication. In the real-world datasets, GSM8K and AIME involve problems you can solve with high-school math, while MMLU (Social Sciences) needs specific knowledge, CommonsenseQA depends on general world knowledge, and GPQA and MMLU (STEM) need specialized science knowledge. For these last tasks, checking an answer needs the same knowledge as solving the problem. Key points: Even though tasks that are easy to solve are usually easier to verify, some tasks are just naturally easier to verify. These include made-up problems with logical or structured thinking (like 3SAT, Sudo), and real-world tasks that mainly use math reasoning instead of a lot of factual recall (like GSM8K, AIME). These tasks also give bigger benefits from test-time rejection sampling with verifiers.
