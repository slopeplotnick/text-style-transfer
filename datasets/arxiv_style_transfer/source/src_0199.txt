The recordings were checked to make sure they were clear and followed the instructions. Once approved, they were written down by trained people using the same system. Only the recordings and written versions that passed the final check were added to the dataset. Payment was adjusted based on language needs. This process made sure the final dataset was technically good, had speakers from different backgrounds, and followed ethical rules. The prompts weren't scripted and were made by language teams to be culturally relevant and sound natural. Each team had a leader and usually three assistants, and they checked each other's work before the leader approved the prompts for the project. They used Microsoft Forms to pick a knowledge area, then chose a topic and related subtopics. Language teams learned to make prompts that got different kinds of answers, like asking "What's your favorite fruit and why?" instead of just "What's your favorite fruit?" They were taught how important it is to design prompts to get answers that aren't just statements but also questions, exclamations, commands, and more. Teams were also encouraged to make topics and subtopics more culturally relevant and sensitive when needed. The aim was to capture the language used in South Africa as much as possible, so the topics and subdomains were very relevant to South African life and specific to that language.
