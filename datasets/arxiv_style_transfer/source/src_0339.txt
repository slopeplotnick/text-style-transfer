The EMOPIA dataset is used for research on generating music that matches emotions, but there aren’t many big datasets with human-labeled MIDI files. Most of the existing ones are small, with fewer than 200 MIDI files that have emotion labels. EMOPIA is the biggest dataset available, with 1,087 music clips from 387 songs, and four people labeled them for emotions. The dataset has four emotion categories based on the valence-arousal plane, and more details about it are in the Appendix (Section B). Another dataset we use is GiantMIDI-Piano, which has 10,855 solo piano pieces by 2,786 composers. It’s used for research like analyzing classical music and generating symbolic music. To connect our text data with EMOPIA files, we grouped 27 emotion labels into four categories based on the AV quadrants. We used the NRC VAD lexicon, which has arousal and valence scores for 55,000 words, to assign each emotion to one of the four categories. The mappings and their positions on the AV plane are shown in Figure 1, and Appendix A explains more about the VAD and how it connects to EMOPIA. We use MIDI files to encode and create symbolic music data because they can store musical performance details in a compact way while still showing emotion-related aspects of music. MIDI files are also widely available, so they’re useful for training. In our training, we treat each MIDI file as a sequence of musical events, which works well with sequence-to-sequence models for learning and creating music.
