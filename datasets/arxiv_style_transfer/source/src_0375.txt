In Figure 3, we're looking at how the 7B model learns with CAPO and GRPO. At first, both methods get better at the same rate, but once the model gets the hang of things, CAPO does better. The gray line shows when the model stops copying and starts figuring things out on its own. After this, CAPO's unpredictability keeps going up, unlike GRPO, which stays the same, and the rewards keep getting better. This means CAPO not only gets better rewards later on but also stays more unpredictable, which is good for trying out different ways of thinking. The smooth increase in unpredictability means CAPO doesn't suddenly become predictable by mixing in negative examples too soon. By waiting, CAPO keeps the copying stage steady and uses negative feedback better later, helping it handle different tasks better. Now, let's compare the usual static way of teaching with our new dynamic method. In the static way, we figure out how hard each example is by checking how well the model does on it and then train the model in that order. Our dynamic method changes the focus during training without needing to reorder the data. As shown in Table 3, both ADARFT and the static method GRPO(+SC) don't really improve much over the basic GRPO. But our dynamic method GRPO(+Ours) does a lot better in most tasks. This shows that just guessing how hard something is isn't enough, and changing the focus during training works better.
