There's a new way to measure how useful verifiers are, called Verifier Gain. Just looking at how accurate verifiers are doesn't tell the whole story. Verifier Gain shows how much better things get when using a verifier during testing. We looked into how verifiers help with rejecting wrong answers and found that this idea matches what happens in real life. We also checked how much better things get with different types of verification: doing it yourself, within the same group, or with different groups. It turns out that using verifiers from different groups usually works best, especially compared to doing it yourself. This is because when the verifier and solver are too similar, the verifier isn't as helpful. As models get better, whether by getting bigger, being trained more, or just being more accurate, they're not as good at checking themselves but are better at checking other groups. We also looked at how easy it is to verify different tasks. Tasks that are easy to solve are usually easy to verify, but checking your own work doesn't help much across all tasks. Tasks that involve math or logic tend to give better verifier results. Verifiers can work in two ways: they can check if the final answer is right or if the steps to get there are correct. Some researchers have come up with ways for models to check their own answers by predicting parts of the question based on the solution. To avoid mistakes, some models ask themselves fact-check questions. Other researchers have trained verifiers to check final answers or to work with the solver. Some have also worked on checking the steps in the process.
