These metrics check if the edits match the intended direction and stay consistent with the meaning throughout the sequence. To see if the person's identity is kept the same in the edited video, we calculate the average similarity between face details from each frame and the first edited frame using a pre-trained ArcFace model. This shows how well the person's look and facial features are kept during the editing process, with higher numbers meaning better identity consistency. A user study also showed that people preferred our method for how good it looks and how well it stays aligned over time. More details about the study and the calculations for the metrics are in the extra material. Looking at the results in Table 1, text-based methods like LucyEdit score high on some syncing metrics (e.g., Speech Corr. 0.80 vs. our 0.72), but they fail at applying the actual edit, as seen in their low 0.11 Directional CLIP score compared to our 0.21. Our method balances everything better, doing well with syncing while also applying the visual edits properly. VACE, on the other hand, uses extra conditioning like pose, canny, and depth to get higher CLIP alignment, but it doesn’t do as well with keeping things consistent over time or maintaining identity. Sync-LoRA, which trains specifically for synced editing, keeps good alignment over time while making accurate edits that preserve the person's identity. This shows how our approach balances motion consistency and edit accuracy instead of focusing only on one thing. In Figure 6, we show tests where we leave out one syncing factor at a time. We train models on data that doesn’t include one factor, like speech, gaze, blinking, or pose, by setting its weight to zero in our filtering score. For example, in the first test (top two rows), leaving out pose causes noticeable mistakes in head direction. In the second test (bottom two rows), leaving out any factor causes problems like spatial drift or misalignment in gaze or blinking. Leaving out any factor makes syncing worse, especially in the man’s mouth and the woman’s eye movements, showing that all four factors work together to provide complete motion information.
