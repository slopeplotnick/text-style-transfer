When small groups of people or systems interact closely and briefly, small issues start to show up. As these groups exchange information in partly connected networks, bigger risks appear because these issues begin to spread. When everything connects and these issues reach a certain point, big problems happen, and the group's behavior changes in ways that weren't planned. This way of looking at things is just an early idea to help people talk about and study these behaviors. Without this kind of framework, researchers can't easily spot, measure, or control these behaviors in groups. To prove this idea, we'll need to do a lot of experiments in controlled settings, which we plan to do later. Small risks show up in direct interactions between a few people or systems, where early signs of bigger issues start. These risks show how fragile shared understanding is, even before a network works together on a large scale. Even though these issues start small, they can grow into bigger problems as interactions continue. Once a problem becomes part of a system's understanding, it spreads and gets bigger with each interaction. The number of problems grows faster than the number of people or messages. Bigger risks show up when larger groups interact in partly connected networks. At this level, no single problem stands out, but the way interactions happen causes things like accuracy or diversity to get worse. Each part might seem fine on its own, but together, they don't work as expected. These bigger risks show that problems can happen even if nothing is completely broken. Issues come from how partly correct parts interact, not from single errors. This is where local predictability starts to break down and things become unstable.
