Our method makes actions more precise, like we saw in simulation tests. What's more, it helps fix problems with policy failures because of bad teleoperation data. In the Pen Grasping stage, the operator's different ways of grasping and timing cause big differences in states between grasping modes, leading to a sparse observation-action distribution in the expert dataset. So, the base policy often acts unstable, like not closing the gripper right, opening it again after grasping, or switching between two grasping modes. Our scaling method during testing mostly fixes these problems: the gripper closes correctly, and the robot avoids bad modes. This improvement comes from our scaling method filtering out bad paths caused by noise in the teleoperated dataset. Trying to fit the mapping from features to action errors makes learning harder because the model has to understand not just good features but also bad ones. During test-time scaling, only picking the best action matters. Our tests show that the drop in performance happens because of overfitting on the training set, which makes generalization worse. When separate encoders are used instead of Internal Features and trained together with CFN, the features end up very similar, making accurate pseudo-counting hard and causing a big drop in performance. This method also reduces efficiency because it doesn't use VLA's natural understanding of scenes and actions. In this work, we introduce TACO, a test-time-scaling framework that uses a simple pseudo-count estimator as a high-quality checker for action chunks. TACO works with different VLA models and consistently improves the base policy in many real-world and simulated tests. Even though it has some limits—like not being able to evaluate new actions made only by perturbation and relying on the underlying VLA's ability to represent—our method shows that simple test-time scaling can effectively prevent exploration and reduce instability and performance drop caused by distribution shift in action prediction.
