We're using a method to judge outlines based on principles, not comparing them to human examples. Outlines are shorter and just show the structure, so comparing them to a full survey isn't helpful. Instead, we look at how well they're organized, how logically they flow, and how clear they are. This way of judging fits better with what makes a good outline, which is mostly about following academic rules. We use the original survey title to get a survey from each system. All systems can look up info online, so they all have the same chance to get information. We check three types of systems: ones for writing long texts in general, ones for writing papers, and ones specifically for making surveys. They all get the same input and are judged the same way. The scores are shown below. The table shows different patterns for the three types of systems. General long-text systems are good with language and staying on topic, but their surveys aren't deep: they're not very original, don't combine ideas well, and don't analyze much. Paper-writing systems are better at structure, but their results are often stiff and follow a template, which limits how deep they go and how many views they show. Survey-making systems score the highest overall, with better organization, clearer combination of ideas, and better separation of facts and opinions. They're especially good in science and tech subjects, where getting the right info and organizing it well is important. These differences show that writing good surveys needs more than just general language models—it needs special methods that focus on using literature and organizing ideas well.
