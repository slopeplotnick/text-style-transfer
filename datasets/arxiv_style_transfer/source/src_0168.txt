Some studies have looked into more organized ways of creating prompts. For example, Plan-and-Solve (Wang et al., 2023c) breaks a task into two parts: planning and solving, which works better for math and logic problems. Prompt Chaining (Chen et al., 2023) checks if linking summaries or steps in between helps with summarizing text. Complexity-based Prompting (Fu et al., 2023) says the difficulty of a task should decide how the prompt is made, which is similar to our idea of looking at how specific prompts are. Most research focuses on the format of tasks or examples used, but we’re focusing on how detailed and structured prompts are, treating it as something that can vary. Some recent studies have come up with ways to refine prompts automatically or with human help. For instance, black-box optimization methods (Diao et al., 2023) adjust prompts based on feedback without needing to look at model details. Prompt Engineering a Prompt Engineer (Zhou et al., 2023) shows that large language models can be prompted to improve their own prompts, which is a kind of self-improvement strategy similar to our generalization loop. Prompt Refinement with Image Pivots (Lu et al., 2023) uses visual features to tweak text prompts for generating images. Even though we only work with text, we also aim to adjust prompts to make results better. But unlike these studies, DETAIL focuses on how being specific in text affects how models reason, instead of looking at things like meaning changes or mixing text with other types of data. Reviews like the one by Liu et al. (2023) have gone over different prompting methods for reasoning, showing how many types of prompts and strategies exist. However, not many studies look at how the level of detail in prompts changes how models behave across different tasks and systems. While methods like CoT and Plan-and-Solve show that the content of prompts is important, they don’t go into detail about this.
