People understand videos by figuring out what happens, why things are done, and when important stuff happens. Regular video object segmentation methods mostly look at how things appear or their categories. Reasoning VOS takes it up a notch: the model has to understand vague instructions, use common sense and causal thinking, mix time changes with knowledge to find the target, like "the runner most likely to win" or "the object causing the accident." This makes things simpler but also harder to understand, changes the way models work, and needs a lot of data to fine-tune. The main idea is that good video reasoning is about making careful choices, not just one quick guess. The model has to know where to look, when to pay attention, and who or what the question is about. Based on this, we made ReVSeg, which gets rid of hidden segmentation tokens and instead turns reasoning video segmentation into a clear sequence of reasoning steps. ReVSeg breaks down reasoning VOS into three parts that fit well with model abilities: understanding the video (figuring out the question and what's happening), grounding in time (finding key frames or times related to the question), and grounding in space (finding the target objects in chosen frames). ReVSeg uses these steps with one model, making sure the meaning from early steps carries on to later ones. Doing reasoning this way keeps the model aligned with its training, avoids heavy retraining, and turns a messy problem into a clear process. Once the reasoning steps are clear, the big challenge is how to make the steps better. Supervised learning doesn't help much because middle steps aren't well labeled. So, we use reinforcement learning, treating reasoning VOS as a behavior that gets rewarded only when the whole decision path leads to the right result. But using RL isn't easy: reasoning VOS gives very few success signals and needs coordination across understanding, time picking, and space finding. To tackle this, we add rewards at key decision points, making sure RL improves how the model reasons. Along with breaking down the task, RL changes it from a hidden guessing game into a clear reasoning strategy: breaking down shows what the model should decide, while RL shows how those decisions should work together. This teamwork creates a reasoning strategy that's both consistent inside and strong in practice.
