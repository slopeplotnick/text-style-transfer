This paper looks at why safety setups meant for human and model interactions don't work well when large language models talk to each other. Most current rules focus on keeping one model safe by using prompts, fine-tuning, and moderation to control how a single model acts. But they don't manage how multiple models interact. These setups assume one model talks to one user under steady supervision. But research and industry are moving fast towards systems where models talk to each other, using each other's outputs as inputs in chains. In these setups, even if each model is doing what it's supposed to, the whole system can still fail. We suggest shifting from focusing on individual model safety to looking at the safety of the whole system. We introduce the idea of the Emergent Systemic Risk Horizon (ESRH) to show how problems come from how models interact, not just from one model acting up. The paper offers (i) a theory on the risks when models interact, (ii) a way to connect different levels of failure, and (iii) a plan for Institutional AI, which is a setup for better oversight in systems with many agents. Current safety methods assume one model works under human watch, where bad behavior can be stopped with prompt tweaks, learning from human feedback, or controlling outputs. These methods have made models more reliable and polite, but they only work on a case-by-case basis. They control how one model responds to one input, not how many models work together. As big models are used more in tools, agents, and systems with many agents, this one-model safety idea starts to fail. Models that are good on their own can still cause problems together that no single model was trained to avoid, because of feedback loops, copying, or unexpected teamwork. This creates a gap between making sure each model is doing well and keeping the whole system safe, where making sure each part is good doesn't ensure control over the whole network.
