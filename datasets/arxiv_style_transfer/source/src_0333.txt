Lately, brain-computer interfaces (BCIs) have been getting a lot of attention as tools that let the brain connect directly to devices or digital systems. These tools can be either invasive (requiring surgery) or non-invasive (not requiring surgery). Scientists, doctors, and even the media are interested in them. BCIs are systems that help people with serious movement or speech problems, like those caused by conditions such as locked-in syndrome, ALS, spinal cord injuries, or strokes, to communicate or even move again when they otherwise couldn’t. Because of this, it’s become really important to figure out how to improve non-invasive BCIs by analyzing a wider range of features from EEG data. There are many ways to extract features from EEG signals, and these methods are key for analyzing the data. They can focus on simple things like time or frequency or use more complex techniques that combine multiple dimensions. Turning raw EEG signals into useful information, like statistics based on time, frequency-related features like power spectral density, or methods that combine time and frequency like wavelet transforms, is really important for making sure the signals are classified correctly and the system works well. It’s also important to have features that work well across different people because EEG signals can vary a lot between individuals. Problems like changes in the data over time or differences between datasets mean that methods like transfer learning and domain generalization are needed to make sure the features are reliable. A big question is figuring out which combinations of feature-extraction and classification methods work best, especially when people’s EEG signals are so different. To answer this, researchers need to use evaluation methods that avoid being overly optimistic. This involves things like testing on data from people who weren’t part of the training process (like leave-one-subject-out cross-validation), using metrics that handle imbalances in the data well (like balanced accuracy and AUROC), checking the results with statistical tests that don’t assume too much about the data, and making sure there’s no data leakage. For example, things like filtering or normalizing the data should only be done on the training data and not on the test data.
