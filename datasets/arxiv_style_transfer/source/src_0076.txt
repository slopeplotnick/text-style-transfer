People have noticed that predictions usually settle down before the last step (Pengxiang et al., 2025). There are ways to balance exploring and speeding up by looking at past results or how predictable things are, which helps cut out unnecessary steps (Wei et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025b; Or-El et al., 2025). Some work focuses on fixing mistakes and improving results, letting models fix their own answers by redoing parts they aren't sure about (Zhang et al., 2025a; Su et al., 2025). Quick methods also tackle this issue by using ideas from continuous processes through learning in stages, keeping things consistent, or matching flows (Sahoo et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025; Meister et al., 2024). Other ways to speed things up include storing data for quick access and using methods that check and confirm parts at the same time (Yuan et al., 2025b; Wang et al., 2025a; Sohoni et al., 2025; Yuan et al., 2025a). Although these methods are effective, they often need extra training, new models, or complicated rules, so there's a need for a simple way to decide when to stop without extra training or specific setups. We look at the process of deciding when to stop and introduce SchED, a schedule-based way to stop early that doesn't need extra training or specific models. Basically, we look at how confident we are in each part of the answer and compare it to a changing threshold that gets easier as we go along. By separating confidence from the number of steps and making the threshold change smoothly, we can stop as soon as predictions are stable, avoiding sudden changes or fixed limits. SchED works with regular schedules and doesn't need changes to how models are trained (Nie et al., 2025; Ye et al., 2025).
