Except for Study 1, all the fake datasets copied the factor structure of their matching scale. Also, M-CFA shows that both fake and real participants respond to the scales in similar ways. These findings match earlier research on group-level fake data. Interestingly, Studies 3 and 4 show high levels of measurement consistency between datasets compared to Studies 1 and 2, which might mean that scales made from fake subjects work better with real people than the other way around. One way to look at this is that when LLMs answer, they tend to see scale items in a more standard way, less affected by randomness and biases from study conditions and population traits. So, factor structures from fake samples might apply better to other samples than those from real-world data. More research should look into what affects how LLMs and people understand and answer survey questions. While CFA gives good results, fake samples don't match the pairwise correlations, distribution, and variance of real-world samples. Overall, these results show that even though both datasets have the same factor structure, their score distributions are quite different across all measured areas. So, group-level data and their structures are reliable enough for pilot testing, but individual-level fake data aren't good for this. These limits match what Petrov and others found. It's important to note that while Studies 2, 3, and 4 have similar results, Study 1 is quite different. This could be due to several reasons: first, the factor structure of this scale was found using a different method (minres) than other scales, which might not have led to different responses. Second, this scale uses a 5-point Likert scale for answers, while others use a 6-point or a 7-point.
