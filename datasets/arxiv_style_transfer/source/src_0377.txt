We have a way to guess how much better a solver will get when we use a verifier to help pick the right answers. To see if this guess is accurate, we try out different solver and verifier combinations from a group of 12 models, picking the three smallest ones from each of four groups. For each problem, the solver keeps trying until the verifier says one answer is right, up to nine tries. If it doesn't find a right answer, we just keep the last try. The results we got, along with the expected improvements, are in Figure 4. Sometimes the improvements aren't clear when they're small, and the nine-try limit makes it hard to see the full picture, but overall, the results match what we expected. We noticed that checking your own work doesn't help as much as getting someone else to check it, especially if they're from a different group. The main point is that our way of guessing improvements is pretty good and doesn't need a lot of extra work to figure out. People usually think answers that look like their own are more likely to be right, which is a known bias. Our findings suggest something similar happens with solvers and verifiers. As we showed before, models that are good at reasoning don't gain much from checking their own work but do better when checked by different models, showing a similar bias. The key takeaway is that when a solver and verifier think alike, the verifier is more likely to accept wrong answers, which lowers the improvement. Using a verifier that thinks differently helps avoid this problem.
