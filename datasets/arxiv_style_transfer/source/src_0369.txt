When you test it, you see that the order of the articles you give to the model affects the summaries it makes. Basically, these models tend to focus more on the first article, which changes how they frame the information and how similar the summaries are to the original articles. Other researchers have already noticed that these models pay more attention to earlier parts of long inputs. What we found is that this also happens when the model deals with multiple documents. It’s clear that the first article gets more attention when the model ranks information. A scoring method called BERTScore shows this pattern every time. This happens because the model tends to use the words and arguments from the first article more than the others. So, when you look at how similar the summaries are, the first article always scores higher. On the other hand, methods like ROUGE-L, which only look at word overlap, don’t show this as much, and SummaC, which checks if the facts match, doesn’t focus on framing. That’s why these methods don’t pick up on the first-article bias as strongly as BERTScore does. For all three ways of looking at it, the first article stands out compared to the second and third, but there’s no big difference between the second and third articles. This shows that the model gives priority to the first article, not because it gets less interested in the later ones over time. This kind of bias can have real-world effects, like in tools that summarize information for users. For instance, Google Search’s AI summaries, which use Gemini models, might treat the first articles it processes as more important and ignore the ones that come later. This can lead to summaries that favor certain viewpoints and might even affect how people understand the topic, especially if they trust the AI a lot.
