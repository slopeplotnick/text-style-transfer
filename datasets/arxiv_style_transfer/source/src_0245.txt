Making sure datasets are licensed properly is key to making them easy to use and access ethically, especially for languages that don't have a lot of resources. Open licenses like Creative Commons (like CC BY 4.0) help people reuse data on a big scale. Projects like Mozilla Common Voice show how useful open licenses are for developing language technology. But many researchers in low- and middle-income countries still can't access resources for AI development, which raises concerns about fair data management and giving local communities control. For the Swivuriso project, we used the Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 International (CC BY 4.0) license to make sure it's open to everyone and ethically accountable. Swivuriso is available on Zenodo and Hugging Face, with permanent DOIs and detailed documentation. Each release comes with audio, matching transcripts, and detailed info on demographics, language, and accessibility. We also have a statement on ethical use, limiting things like voice cloning to protect the privacy of speakers. The CC BY 4.0 license is very open: people can share, change, and use the dataset for anything, even for business, as long as they credit the creators and note any changes. The license doesn't allow extra legal or technical limits, covers database rights, and has a "cure period" to fix any license issues, all to make sure it's legally clear and supports global scientific work. But while open licenses are great for making data useful, they often miss things like local control or protecting Indigenous knowledge. Using the same licenses everywhere can unintentionally take away community ownership, especially for cultural stuff or data from the community. This problem is leading to new ways of thinking that focus more on taking care of data, sharing benefits, and giving communities control, which is becoming more popular in African data management.
