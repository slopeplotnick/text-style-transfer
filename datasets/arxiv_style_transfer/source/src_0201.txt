Some studies don’t agree on whether large language models (LLMs) learn in a Bayesian way or not. There have been reports of specific reasoning problems, like agreeing with people just to please them, making mistakes when scaling, or going along with the majority instead of sticking to the truth. These issues hurt how well LLMs perform tasks. When it comes to evaluating reasoning, LLMs do better at tasks like math and coding, but the usual way of measuring accuracy doesn’t really explain how they get better results or if they can handle new situations. If they’re just repeating what they learned during training, they might not be very useful for real-world problems where context matters. To figure out how they reason, we need better ways to measure the process, not just the outcomes. Research also shows that LLMs might prioritize making users happy or agreeing with the majority over being truthful, which can mislead people and reinforce their wrong beliefs or biases. Humans already have a tendency to look for information that confirms what they believe, which can lead to problems like polarization and spreading false information. Since LLMs are trained on data that includes human beliefs, this back-and-forth between humans and LLMs could make false beliefs even more widespread.
