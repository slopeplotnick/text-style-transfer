We check how good the pre-training is by seeing if the token types in the samples we make are like the ones in the original pre-train data. We look at how often each token type shows up in the pre-train data and compare it to how often they show up in the samples we make at different training stages. The samples from the 250 epochs stage look most like the original data. Even though the training and validation losses keep going down until 300 epochs, the samples start to look less like the original data. So, we pick the 250 epochs stage for fine-tuning on EMOPIA because it balances loss reduction and staying true to the original data. We also did a test to see if the decoder can make MIDI using AV quadrants by giving it quadrant labels directly instead of text embeddings. This simple method made MIDI samples that matched EMOPIA's valence-arousal metrics well. We did a listening test with four people using the method from Section IV-A. Table I shows what they thought. They said the model's music fit the arousal dimension better (0.70 accuracy) than valence (0.53 accuracy) or both valence and arousal (0.40 accuracy). Normally, you'd expect 0.5 accuracy for valence and arousal and 0.25 for both. The model's music did better than these baseline numbers, especially for arousal (0.70) and both valence-arousal (0.40). This means people thought the music matched the emotional tone of the text more than just by chance. Having more people in the study would give more reliable results and let us test for statistical significance. It's important to include a wider range of listeners in future studies to see if these early results hold up.
