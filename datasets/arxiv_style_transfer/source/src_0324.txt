NLP, or Natural Language Processing, started as an idea from a Swiss linguist named Ferdinand de Saussure, who said that the meaning of language comes from how words relate to and differ from each other. People can communicate because they share common language structures. In 1950, Alan Turing came up with the idea of a "thinking machine," saying that if a machine could talk to humans through a teleprinter, it would show it could think. Today, NLP is important for understanding human language and creating responses that make sense in context. Examples include smart assistants like Siri and Alexa, which help users and handle requests on their own. Large Language Models, or LLMs, are advanced AI systems built using deep learning techniques. They’re trained on huge amounts of data and can produce text that sounds like it was written by a person. These models use transformer architecture, which helps them understand complicated language details and connections over long pieces of text. This allows them to do things like translate languages, have conversations, and create content. LLMs can understand human languages and are useful in many areas of research and industry. OpenAI’s ChatGPT is a well-known example of an LLM that’s widely used in everyday life. Sometimes, though, language models make mistakes called "hallucinations." This happens when the model, based on patterns it learned during training, creates information that sounds believable but isn’t true, especially when it deals with something it hasn’t seen before. These mistakes can cause anything from small misunderstandings to big problems in areas like translation, coding, or systems that work on their own. Fixing these hallucinations is really important to make sure the models are reliable, safe, and useful, especially in situations where accuracy is critical. One way to handle this issue is by using fine-tuned models. These are pre-trained models that are adjusted for specific tasks, and they work better than general-purpose models because they’re trained on smaller, focused datasets. Fine-tuning involves carefully changing the model’s settings to make it more accurate and better at generalizing. It builds on the basic language knowledge the model learned during its initial training and then sharpens its ability to handle specific tasks.
