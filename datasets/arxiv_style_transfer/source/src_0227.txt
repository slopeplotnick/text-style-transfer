Recently, there have been some new datasets like Mozilla's Common Voice, which has recorded speech in six South African languages. People contributed to it, and it’s shared under a license that lets anyone use it freely. FLEURS has about 12 hours of recordings for each of four South African languages, mainly for testing and evaluation purposes. Vuk'uzenzele (VixSD) is a smaller dataset made by a community, shared under a license that focuses on community ownership and ethical use. Even though it’s not very big, it shows how locals can help create resources. AfriSpeech-200 has English recordings with South African accents and covers healthcare topics, which is helpful for making speech recognition systems that understand accents. But overall, there’s still not much data with natural, unscripted speech or a wide range of topics. This shows there’s a need for more diverse datasets like Swivuriso, which mix scripted and unscripted speech, cover more topics, and include a wider variety of speakers. Collecting speech data in communities that don’t have much representation needs to be done carefully, respecting consent, privacy, cultural values, and the long-term effects on those communities. Past projects like Common Voice and ALFFA have shown how important it is to include ethical and participatory practices in every step of the process. For example, a study on the Catalan version of Common Voice showed how important it is to have clear consent rules, protect privacy, and make sure everyone can take part. The ALFFA project also worked closely with local experts and linguists during data collection to involve the community. Swivuriso builds on these ideas by focusing on ethical practices and making sure everyone is included. Local organizers helped manage the recordings, supported the participants, and made sure the community was well-represented. They followed ethical rules approved by institutions, like removing personal details, anonymizing data, and adding safeguards to prevent misuse, such as voice cloning or biometric tracking. Making sure datasets are easy to access and use is important, especially for languages that don’t have many resources. Open licenses, like Creative Commons, are great for this because they let people use the data freely, which is helpful when collecting data is hard or expensive. Projects like Common Voice show how open licensing can make it easier to share resources and speed up the development of language technology for languages that don’t have much support.
