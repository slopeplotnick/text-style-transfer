It's tough to train good reward models because they're subjective and depend on the situation, and learned proxies can be tricked. There aren't many annotations, which makes it hard for the model to handle detailed behaviors. Because of these issues, people have tried different ways to rely less on annotations. Direct Preference Optimization doesn't use explicit reward models but still needs preference data. Verifiable rewards use real outcomes but only work when you can automatically check them. Self-evaluation methods have problems with being sensitive to prompts and biases. All these methods still rely on outside signals to judge quality and ignore what's in the model's own representations. But the truth of a generation should show in its internal processing. For example, when a model says "The capital of France is Paris," its hidden states show factual knowledge; if it makes stuff up, different patterns show up. This makes us wonder: Can we judge text quality from internal signals, so we don't need outside help to align LLMs? We show that looking at a simple geometric feature of LLM hidden states, called effective dimensionality, can reliably indicate LLM response quality. We suggest using stable rank, a matrix-theory measure, as an unsupervised way to judge generation quality. Stable rank measures how many independent semantic directions a response uses by looking at the ratio of total variance to the main direction variance in the hidden-state representation. It balances having rich information with being coherent: a high stable rank means information is spread out across many dimensions instead of just a few. The theory behind this comes from two areas of study. First, the softmax bottleneck analysis shows that to model language accurately, you need to work with a high-dimensional semantic space because the probability distribution of natural language is naturally complex.
