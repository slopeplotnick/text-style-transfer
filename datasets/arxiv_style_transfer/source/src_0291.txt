So, we've come up with a new standard called HEALTHCONTRADICT. It uses clear numbers to check how well language models can handle long and conflicting medical info. It's better than the latest medical question-answer tests at showing how different models perform. When we tested it, language models that are good with medical stuff did better at using the right info, ignoring wrong info, and figuring out conflicting info. As far as we know, the TREC Health Misinformation tracks are the only public resource with expert-made health questions that have real answers and scientific proof, plus document pools with both supporting and opposing evidence judged by experts. These parts are key for making health questions with real answers, using pairs of conflicting documents to test how well language models handle medical knowledge conflicts. In HEALTHCONTRADICT, we look at important and trustworthy documents that show how good a document is at backing up the answer to a question. We made the labels the same by changing the 2019 tags "effective"/"ineffective" and the 2021 tags "supportive"/"dissuades" to "yes"/"no," matching the 2022 labels. We also say that if two documents for the same question have opposite views - one yes, one no - they're a contradictory pair. Plus, we say a document is right if its view matches the scientific proof, and wrong if it goes against the scientific proof. The original sets have 130 expert-checked questions (50 in 2019, 35 in 2021, and 45 in 2022). Questions with yes/no views and those with both supporting and opposing documents are included. Using this rule cuts the set down to 110 questions. We match supporting and opposing documents into contradiction pairs, making sure each document is used only once because some documents might be linked to more than one question. We leave out questions that can't make at least one contradiction pair because there aren't enough unique documents. This gives us a final set of 81 questions and 920 pairs of conflicting documents.
