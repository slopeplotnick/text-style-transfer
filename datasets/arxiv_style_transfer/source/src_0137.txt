You can use machine learning, like neural networks or generative AI, to make predictions. For planning under uncertain conditions, Monte Carlo Tree search algorithms are useful. AI has two main parts: predicting and deciding. I’ll explain how these are used in exploration. To make predictions, you need datasets. The hardest part of mineral exploration is creating and organizing datasets, not which machine learning method you pick. These datasets can be really complex—having lots of variables, being 3D, or having extreme values. Problems like this are rare in other fields. There are two big issues with organizing data that people often miss: data isn’t always on the same scale, and not all data should be combined into one dataset. What works in one area might not work in another. For example, ground truth data, like core samples, often gets scaled up to match geophysical data. Geophysical and geochemical datasets are sometimes combined in ways that don’t make sense geologically. Machine learning models from one area are often applied to another without testing if that’s okay. These mistakes cause more false positives and make AI look bad, but the real issue is how people handle the data. A big mistake that’s been known for a long time in mining is that data at different scales has different variance. For example, interpolated geophysical data has less variance than the actual measurements, which is guaranteed when you interpolate. Also, drilling data measured at a small scale is often scaled up or shifted to compare it with geophysical data. This creates a fake correlation between the labels and predictors because of how the data was handled. Because of these issues, current maps for mineral potential or prospecting often have a lot of false positives.
