We got 17 people to help out, either from school posters or just asking random people on the street. The study got the okay from the Institutional Review Board, and if any of the volunteers were underage, their guardians had to agree to it. We kept all the interview stuff safe and didn't use any personal info. Most of the people knew how to make short stories and rated themselves at about 3.8 out of 5 for their skills. Everyone had written stories before, either by hand or using computer programs. We split them into groups of about four, each with a staff member. The workshop took about two and a half hours. During the workshop, we asked them to talk out loud about what they were thinking, which helped us see their initial and final ideas and how they got there. We also talked to four of them afterward. By looking at the workshop recordings and interview info, we found four different ways people interacted while making stories. The workshop went like this: First, there was a 15-minute introduction where the staff explained what the workshop was about and how it worked, using examples to show the four main parts. Volunteers were reminded to keep talking out loud about their choices. Then, there was a 60-minute group discussion where the staff gave out cards. Each group worked together to make their own story using the four main parts, talking about their thoughts and choices as they went. The staff walked around to help out when needed. After that, there was a 45-minute session where volunteers shared their stories and got feedback from others and the staff, focusing on creativity and how well the stories were put together. Finally, there were 30-minute informal interviews with four volunteers to talk about their creative experiences.
