People tend to act out more and get angrier online because they feel anonymous, and being in groups can make arguments worse. Computers can spot bad behavior online, but they usually just react by deleting stuff or punishing people, not really fixing the problem. This means online platforms can stop bad talk for a bit but don't deal with why it happens, so it can come back. We're trying something different by using mediation instead of just moderating. Instead of just saying what's bad, we're looking at whether a language model can follow a fight and help calm it down. We think these models could help by understanding conversations, seeing if arguments are fair, and responding in a way that calms things down and makes the talk more helpful. To do this, we need to think about what's going on, how people feel, how things get worse, and how people usually talk. We split the job into two parts. One part is about figuring out what's unfair or making people upset. The other part is about saying something that calms things down and helps people talk about their real issues instead of fighting. To see if these models can really help, we made a big set of real online fights from Reddit and set up a way to test them. Our test looks at three things: first, how well the model follows good conversation rules; second, how people act after the model steps in to see if it really helps; and third, how the model's help compares to what humans would say. From a lot of tests, we saw that models you can pay for do better than free ones at both figuring out and calming down fights, showing they stick to the topic better. Also, the models help lower bad talk and strong feelings in fake fights, and while they're good at staying neutral and clear, they're not as easy to read or as engaging as humans.
