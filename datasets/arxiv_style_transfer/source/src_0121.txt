Hey, here's the deal with the study. We compared real and fake data and didn't find any big differences in the average scores using one test, but another test showed the scores were spread out differently. This means the fake data was close to the real data in terms of middle values, but the way the scores were spread out was different. So, we think this partly supports our fourth idea. We showed some graphs in Figure 2 to compare the real and fake data. To make sure we weren't biased, we used data from another group for a second study. We picked a dataset that checks how good people think they are with tech stuff, using 25 questions split into five areas: talking, handling info, making content, using safely, and solving problems. We chose this because it's well-validated and available to the public. We registered our plans before making the fake data. This time, we changed how we made the fake data because the new dataset didn't have ethnicity info, so we only used age and gender. Also, the dataset had unique IDs for each person, so we matched fake people to real ones by age and gender. Some IDs were repeated, so we took them out to match the samples exactly.
