Big risks happen when the whole system starts acting in ways that don't match what the people who made it wanted, and it starts doing its own thing. At this point, fixing one part or connection doesn't help anymore because the system has gone past a point where it can be controlled easily. When the system gets to this stage, it starts running itself without needing outside help. Once things reach this level, you can't really prevent problems anymore; you can only react to them, and the usual safety measures don't work well. The risks talked about here don't need someone causing trouble on purpose. A lot of system problems can just happen on their own because of how things work together, copy each other, or use results over and over, even if everyone involved is doing what they're supposed to. This kind of problem comes from how things are set up to interact, like feedback, shared situations, or matching goals, rather than someone messing with things on purpose. But people who want to cause trouble can use the same ways that make things go off track naturally. Someone who can change messages, tweak instructions, or mess with how things connect can make problems happen faster, cause people to work together in bad ways, or change how everyone thinks. This framework looks at both natural and intentional problems as part of a range: one shows how things can go wrong on their own, and the other shows how someone can make it worse or start it. What we're talking about here shows that big system problems can't just be blamed on small parts messing up. When things start working together in a big way, the line between what causes problems and what results from them gets blurry: something that seems like a good move for one part can end up causing big issues for the whole system. The usual ways of checking things, which look at single results or fixed tests, aren't enough. They check if things follow rules, not how they act in real situations. To understand safety in this new setup, we need to change how we look at things. Instead of checking if each part is doing what it should, we need to see how everything works together, like how they talk to each other, give feedback, and change when many parts are involved.
